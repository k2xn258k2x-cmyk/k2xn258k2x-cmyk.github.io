There is a mental model of the internet that almost everybody carries and almost nobody has examined, which is that things published there stay published. A page exists at an address, somebody can return to it, and the returning is assumed to be reliable in the way that a book on a shelf is reliable. That model is comfortable, it is how people organise their research habits, and it is wrong in a way that only becomes visible when somebody needs a specific page on a specific day.

The reality is that a published page is a service somebody is providing, continuously, at their own expense, for as long as they choose. It persists because a person pays for hosting, maintains software, renews a domain, and decides not to reorganise. Any of those can stop for reasons entirely unconnected to the page's value, and when one does the page simply is not there, with no notification to anybody who was relying on it.

![Archiving the Web Before It Disappears](/assets/img/feature-screenshot-placeholder.svg)

The disappearances fall into recognisable categories, and it is worth naming them because each affects different material. Sites are redesigned, which silently removes pages nobody thought were load-bearing. Organisations restructure and quietly retire material describing how things used to be. Small operations run by volunteers stop when a volunteer stops. And some pages are removed deliberately, at the moment they become inconvenient, which is precisely when somebody was likely to want them.
<!--more-->


That last category deserves separate attention, because it inverts the usual assumption about risk. Ordinary decay is random with respect to importance: a hobbyist's site vanishes whether or not anybody depended on it. Deliberate removal is the opposite. Material disappears because somebody decided it should, and the deciding is triggered by exactly the circumstances that make the material valuable to anyone examining the situation.

The practical consequence for anybody whose work rests on external material is that a reference is not a record. A link in a document is a promise that something will still be there, made by somebody who has no obligation to keep it and no way to inform anybody when they break it. Somebody writing today and being read in three years is making a claim supported by evidence that may have evaporated between the writing and the reading.

What closes the gap is treating a page as something to take a copy of rather than something to point at. The copy lives in the person's own material, is searchable alongside everything else, carries whatever description they attach, and continues to exist regardless of what happens at the original address. The link remains useful as a courtesy to a reader, and the copy is what the work actually rests on.

The timing is the part people get wrong, and the error is structural rather than careless. Nobody archives everything, so a judgment is required, and the judgment is made at the moment somebody first encounters something, which is the moment they know least about whether it will matter. The professionals who solve this stop trying to predict and instead archive broadly on cheapness, which is available because a copy costs seconds and the alternative cost is unbounded.

What follows is a conversation between two people who both build work on material other people publish, one investigating and one documenting, and who both learned the lesson by losing something.


## Two people who lost something

Raj had suggested it after Lena mentioned a lost page in passing.

‘You said something in the group about a company site being redesigned,’ he said, ‘and you said it the way people say things they have stopped being angry about, which usually means it cost them.’

‘It cost me about three weeks and nearly a section of a story.’ Lena was matter of fact. ‘Say why you asked.’

‘Because I have the same experience from an entirely different direction and I want to know whether we arrived at the same practice.’

‘Say your version first.’

‘A cooperative in Oaxaca that I had been researching for four months.’ Raj counted. ‘A small site, maintained by somebody's nephew, with a history page describing the founding and the families involved.’

‘And it went.’

‘It went during production, without any drama, because the nephew moved to Mexico City and stopped paying for it.’ He shrugged. ‘Which is not a scandal. It is a person getting on with their life.’

‘And what did you lose?’

‘Names and dates that existed nowhere else, about a cooperative founded by people who are now elderly.’ Raj was quiet about it. ‘I had read that page four times and taken no copy, because it had always been there.’

‘Did you recover it?’

‘Partially, from an archive service, and the version they held was two years old and missing the section I needed.’ He was frank. ‘Which taught me that a general archive is a fallback rather than a plan.’

‘Mine was deliberate rather than incidental,’ Lena said.

‘Say it.’

‘A company under investigation redesigned its site during the period I was working.’ She was precise. ‘And the new site had no leadership page, no history, and no board listing.’

‘Which is the material you needed.’

‘Which is the only material establishing who held which position in which year, and it was gone, and the replacement site was perfectly ordinary and gave no indication that anything had been removed.’ Lena shrugged. ‘Which is the point. A redesign is deniable.’

‘Did you have anything?’

‘I had one page, saved by accident because I had been reading it when my connection dropped and I had taken a copy to read later.’ She said it flatly. ‘One page out of about eleven, and that one page carried a section of the story.’

‘By accident.’

‘Entirely by accident, and I have not relied on accident since.’


## What Lena does now

‘Say the practice,’ Raj said. ‘Because I want to compare it to mine and mine is looser.’

‘Mine is a session rather than a habit, which is the first thing.’ Lena was deliberate. ‘Every Friday, on the connected machine, I go through whatever I have been working on and take copies.’

‘Not as you encounter things.’

‘Also as I encounter things, when something is obviously important.’ She counted. ‘But the session catches everything I read and did not think about, which is most of what I read and which is where the losses come from.’

‘Say what you actually save.’

‘Anything about the entities I am examining.’ Lena was precise. ‘Leadership pages. Anything describing structure. Filings that are published rather than obtained. News coverage. And the pages nobody would think to save, which are the ones about the organisation itself.’

‘The about page.’

‘The about page, the history page, and anything describing how something used to work.’ She was emphatic. ‘Because those are the pages that disappear during a restructure and they are the pages establishing the baseline against which a change is visible.’

‘So the boring pages.’

‘The boring pages are the evidential ones and I learned that expensively.’ Lena shrugged. ‘A press release about a scandal is covered everywhere. A quiet page listing who held which role in which year exists in one place.’

Raj asked about the mechanics.

‘You are working across two machines.’

‘Which is the constraint that shapes everything.’ She described it. ‘The connected machine takes the copies. The material moves across on a drive. The air-gapped machine is where the work happens.’

‘Which means the archiving is batched by necessity.’

‘Which means I cannot archive impulsively from the machine where I am writing, and which is why the Friday session exists rather than being a discipline I chose.’ Lena was frank. ‘Somebody without my constraint should archive at the moment they read something.’

‘Do you ever regret the batching?’

‘Once, and it was a page that went down on a Wednesday afternoon.’ She was matter of fact. ‘Which is the risk of a weekly cadence and which I accept, because the alternative is carrying a connected machine into a workflow where it does not belong.’

‘And once it is across?’

‘It becomes an entry like anything else, which is the part I want to stress.’ Lena was definite. ‘It is not a saved file in a folder of saved files. It is an entry, in the investigation it belongs to, with descriptions on it and my own analysis underneath.’

‘Say what the analysis looks like.’

‘A section saying why this page matters and what it establishes.’ She counted. ‘Which takes ninety seconds at the time and which is the difference between an archive and a hoard.’

‘Explain that.’

‘A folder of saved pages is a hoard. In two years I will not remember why I saved any individual one of them.’ Lena shrugged. ‘A page with a paragraph saying what it establishes is evidence, and the paragraph costs ninety seconds while the memory is fresh.’


## What Raj does now

‘Mine is less rigorous and I want to describe the difference honestly,’ Raj said.

‘Say it.’

‘I archive at the moment I read, on the machine I am working on, without any session.’ He was matter of fact. ‘Which works because my constraint is not security and my material is not adversarial.’

‘Nobody is removing your pages on purpose.’

‘Nobody is removing my pages on purpose and that changes the whole risk profile.’ Raj counted. ‘My losses come from a volunteer moving house, a hosting bill going unpaid, or an organisation quietly stopping. Slow, undirected, and unrelated to my interest.’

‘Which means you can be less systematic.’

‘Which means I can be less systematic about timing and I have to be more systematic about breadth.’ He was definite. ‘Because I cannot predict which small site will vanish, so I take everything from any site that looks like it is maintained by one person.’

‘How do you tell?’

‘Design that has not changed in some years, a contact address rather than a form, and text written by somebody who is describing something they care about.’ Raj laughed. ‘Which is not a rigorous test and which has been right every time.’

‘Say what you take from a site like that.’

‘Everything, essentially. Six or eight pages, which is the whole site.’ He shrugged. ‘Which sounds excessive and takes about four minutes, and the alternative is guessing which of eight pages will matter in a year.’

‘That is the same argument I make about the boring pages.’

‘It is your argument arriving from a different direction, and I want to say what it protects.’ Raj was serious. ‘These are cooperatives and workshops and small producers, frequently documenting a craft that a small number of elderly people still practise.’

‘So the loss is not yours.’

‘The loss is not mine and that is what changed how I think about it.’ He counted. ‘If a page describing a family's process disappears and I have the only copy, then my documentary has a source and the practice has a record. If I did not save it, there is no record.’

‘Which is a different obligation from research.’

‘Which is an obligation I did not go looking for and which I now take seriously, and it is the reason I archive more than a documentary strictly requires.’

He mentioned a second category.

‘I also archive things that are supposed to change,’ Raj said.

‘Say the case.’

‘Festival submission pages. Funding announcements. Anything with a deadline and a set of requirements.’ He was matter of fact. ‘Which are updated seasonally and which are the only record of what was actually required when I applied.’

‘Why does that matter?’

‘Because a submission is judged against the requirements as published, and the published requirements change, and I have twice been in a conversation about whether something was required.’ Raj shrugged. ‘Having the page as it was on the day is a completely different position from remembering it.’

‘That is nearer to my work than the cooperatives are.’

‘It is exactly your work at a much lower stake, and I did not connect the two until you described the leadership pages.’


## From a copy into knowledge

‘I want to talk about what happens after the copy,’ Lena said, ‘because the copy is four seconds and everything valuable happens afterward.’

‘Say your sequence.’

‘Descriptions first, three families.’ She counted. ‘Which source it came from. Which subject it concerns. Which investigation it belongs to.’

‘Three on every archived page.’

‘Three on every one, applied at the moment of saving, because applying them later means opening two hundred entries and I will not.’ Lena was definite. ‘Thirty seconds at the point of capture against an afternoon in six months.’

‘Then the placement.’

‘Then it goes under the investigation it belongs to, which is a page in my structure, and that is where I would look for it.’ She shrugged. ‘And the descriptions are what let me find it when I am not looking there.’

‘Which is the perpendicular thing.’

‘Which is exactly it, and archived material benefits from it more than anything else I hold.’ Lena was precise. ‘Because a page about a company is relevant to whichever investigation touches that company, and that is frequently two or three, and the structure only puts it in one.’

‘And then the analysis.’

‘Sections underneath.’ She described them. ‘One saying what this page establishes. One noting anything I have cross-checked. And occasionally one recording what I noticed that the page does not say.’

‘Say the third one.’

‘An absence, which in my work is frequently the finding.’ Lena was matter of fact. ‘A leadership page listing eleven people and omitting one who was demonstrably in post is a fact about the page rather than about the company, and it will not be visible to me in two years unless I write it down now.’

Raj said his was thinner and served a different purpose.

‘Mine is one section and it is always the same question,’ he said.

‘Which is?’

‘Why did I save this.’ He shrugged. ‘Three or four sentences, written immediately, describing what caught my attention.’

‘Which sounds slight.’

‘Which sounds slight and is the difference between a usable archive and four hundred pages I have to reread.’ Raj was definite. ‘Because in eleven months I am editing, and I have forty archived pages about one region, and I need to know which one had the thing about the roasting pits.’

‘And the sentences tell you.’

‘The sentences tell me in four seconds, and a search across them finds the right page immediately, which searching the pages themselves would not do as reliably.’

‘Why not?’

‘Because the page uses the producer's vocabulary and my note uses mine.’ He was thoughtful. ‘I search for what I was thinking, not for what they wrote, and my note is the only place my thinking exists.’

Lena made a noise of recognition.

‘That is a better articulation of something I do without describing it.’

‘Say your version.’

‘I search my own analysis considerably more often than I search the archived content.’ She was working it out. ‘Which I had not noticed and which is obviously true. The page is the evidence. The note is the index.’

‘And you need both.’

‘I need both and I had been thinking of the note as commentary on the important thing.’ Lena shrugged. ‘It is the retrieval mechanism for the important thing, which is a different role entirely.’

‘Descriptions and sections are the same thing at different scales.’

‘Which is a tidy way of putting it and I am going to steal it.’


## The documents behind the pages

‘There is a layer I want to describe because it took me a year to build,’ Lena said.

‘Go on.’

‘A great many of the pages I archive are not the material. They are pages linking to the material.’

‘Say the case.’

‘A regulatory page announcing a filing, with a link to the filing.’ She was precise. ‘The page is a paragraph. The document is forty pages and contains everything.’

‘So you take both.’

‘I take the page and I download the document and attach it to the same entry.’ Lena counted. ‘Which means one entry contains the announcement, the document, and my analysis, and all three are searchable together.’

‘Say why that matters rather than keeping them separate.’

‘Because the announcement establishes when something was published and by whom, and the document establishes what it said.’ She was definite. ‘Separating them means holding two objects and remembering they are related, which I will not do reliably across four hundred entries.’

‘And the documents are searchable.’

‘The contents of the documents are searchable, which is the part that changes the work.’ Lena was emphatic. ‘I can search a term and get a result from inside a forty-page filing I attached eight months ago and have never read in full.’

‘Have you found things that way?’

‘Repeatedly, and one of them was a story.’ She was matter of fact. ‘A name appearing in a schedule at the back of a document I had attached for an entirely different reason and had never reached page thirty of.’

‘Which you would not have found by reading.’

‘Which I would never have found by reading, because nobody reads the schedules at the back of four hundred documents.’ Lena shrugged. ‘The reading is impossible and the searching is instant, and the only requirement is that the document is attached rather than sitting in a downloads folder.’

Raj described his equivalent.

‘Mine is smaller and identical in shape,’ he said. ‘Cultural organisations publish reports.’

‘Say the kind.’

‘Production statistics, denomination-of-origin registers, cooperative membership lists.’ He counted. ‘Which are frequently linked from an article rather than being the article, and which contain the actual numbers.’

‘And you attach them.’

‘I attach them and I have about ninety of them, and I have read perhaps twelve.’ Raj laughed. ‘And I have searched all ninety, many times, which is the correct relationship to have with a set of reference documents.’

‘Say what you get from them.’

‘Verification, mostly.’ He was matter of fact. ‘Somebody tells me on camera that a practice dates from a particular period, and I want to know whether the register agrees, and I can check in four seconds rather than deciding to believe them.’

‘Which changes what you can put in a film.’

‘Which changes what I am willing to assert, and a documentary that asserts things it cannot support is a documentary somebody will eventually dismantle.’


## Finding things worth keeping

‘There is a relationship between two things we both use and I want to name it,’ Raj said.

‘Say it.’

‘The reader finds things and the archiving keeps them, and they are two halves of one practice.’

‘Which I had not described that way and which is correct.’ Lena was thinking. ‘My sources publish and the reader tells me. And then a judgment happens, and the ones that matter get taken.’

‘Say the proportion.’

‘Perhaps one in twelve.’ She was matter of fact. ‘Which sounds low and is deliberate, because archiving everything that appears is a hoard rather than an archive.’

‘How do you decide?’

‘Whether it concerns an entity I am examining, and whether it contains something specific.’ Lena counted. ‘Commentary is not archived. Anything containing a name, a figure, a date, or a document link is archived.’

‘That is a mechanical rule.’

‘It is deliberately mechanical, because the judgment version fails.’ She shrugged. ‘Deciding whether something is important requires knowing what the story is, and I do not know what the story is until considerably later.’

Raj said his rule was different and equally mechanical.

‘Anything announcing a deadline or a requirement,’ he said. ‘And anything from a site I have identified as fragile.’

‘Which is the volunteer test.’

‘Which is the volunteer test, applied once per site rather than per page, and then everything from that site is archived automatically as I encounter it.’ He was definite. ‘Which moves the judgment from the page to the source, and the source is a considerably easier thing to assess.’

‘That is better than mine.’

‘Say why.’

‘Because I assess every page and you assess a site once.’ Lena was working through it. ‘Which for my adversarial sources would not work, because a hostile organisation's site is not fragile, it is actively curated.’

‘So your material requires page-level judgment.’

‘My material requires page-level judgment for one category and would benefit from source-level judgment for the others.’ She paused. ‘Which is a distinction I am going to make. My monitored news sources are not adversarial and I have been assessing them page by page for two years.’

‘So archive everything from those.’

‘Archive everything from about six of them, which is perhaps forty items a week and which costs nothing.’ Lena was matter of fact. ‘And keep the page-level judgment for the entities themselves, where the volume is lower and the stakes are higher.’

‘That is a genuine change from a forty-minute conversation.’

‘It is a change I would not have reached alone, because I had one rule and never noticed it was doing two different jobs badly.’


## The thing Raj archives that Lena would not

‘There is a category I take that you would find strange,’ Raj said.

‘Say it.’

‘Comment sections.’

Lena laughed.

‘You are correct that I would find it strange. Say why.’

‘Because on a community site the comments are frequently the only place where anybody disagrees.’ He was serious under it. ‘The page says how something is done. Four people underneath say that is not how their family does it, and one of them explains a variation that exists nowhere else.’

‘Which is the actual documentation.’

‘Which is occasionally the only documentation of regional variation, and it is attached to a page nobody is maintaining, which makes it doubly fragile.’ Raj shrugged. ‘A comment section is the first thing to go when a site migrates.’

‘Have you used one?’

‘Twice, and one of them changed a sequence in the film.’ He counted. ‘A commenter described a step performed in one valley and not in another, which I then went and filmed, and which nobody I had interviewed had thought to mention because to them it was obvious.’

‘Because people do not describe what everybody around them does.’

‘Which is the central difficulty of documenting anything, and a stranger arguing in a comment section is somebody who has noticed a difference.’ Raj was definite. ‘They are doing comparative work for free and they do it nowhere else.’

Lena thought about it.

‘I would not archive comments and I want to say why, because the reason is specific rather than snobbery.’

‘Go on.’

‘Because on the sites I work with, a comment section is either moderated into agreement or is entirely people who arrived from somewhere else.’ She was matter of fact. ‘Which makes it noise rather than evidence, and archiving noise dilutes an archive I need to be able to trust.’

‘So the value depends on whether the commenters are participants.’

‘The value depends entirely on whether the people commenting are the community the page describes.’ Lena nodded slowly. ‘On yours they are. On mine they are strangers, and that is the whole difference.’

‘That is a better rule than mine, which was simply that I take them.’

‘Then take them where the commenters are participants and ignore them elsewhere, which is a rule you can apply without thinking.’


## The page that came back different

‘I want to describe something that happened last spring,’ Lena said, ‘because it is the case that justifies the whole practice and it is not the case people expect.’

‘Not a disappearance.’

‘Not a disappearance. A change.’ She was precise. ‘A page that is still there, at the same address, saying something different.’

‘Say what changed.’

‘A single sentence in a description of a corporate structure.’ Lena counted. ‘One clause about which entity held which stake, removed, with nothing else on the page altered.’

‘How did you notice?’

‘Because I had a copy from eight months earlier and I was checking a detail against it, and the detail was not in the live page.’ She was matter of fact. ‘Which took me about four seconds to see and would have been invisible without the copy.’

‘And the live page gave no indication.’

‘The live page gave no indication of any kind, because pages do not carry their own history.’ Lena shrugged. ‘Which is the thing people underestimate. A vanished page announces itself. A quietly amended page does not.’

‘Which is worse.’

‘Which is considerably worse, because a missing page makes you go looking and an amended page makes you confidently wrong.’ She was definite. ‘If I had read only the current version I would have written something inaccurate and been certain of it.’

Raj asked something.

‘Do you compare deliberately?’

‘I did not and I now do, for a small number of pages that matter.’ Lena described it. ‘Which is not sophisticated. I take a fresh copy every few months and read the two.’

‘How many pages?’

‘Perhaps eleven, across three active investigations, and I check them quarterly.’ She counted. ‘Which is an hour, four times a year, and it has produced two findings.’

‘Say the second.’

‘A date, changed by two years, on a page describing when a subsidiary was established.’ Lena was flat about it. ‘Which is either a correction of an old error or something else, and which I would never have asked about without the earlier copy.’

‘And which you can now ask about.’

‘Which I can now ask about, with both versions, which is a completely different conversation from asking somebody to explain a date I merely remember differently.’

Raj said his version had no adversary and the same lesson.

‘A distillery page was rewritten after the founder died,’ he said. ‘By a family member, entirely reasonably, and it is a better page.’

‘But different.’

‘But it now describes the practice in the present tense using the son's method, which differs from the father's in one specific respect.’ Raj shrugged. ‘And the earlier version, which I have, describes the father's.’

‘Which is the historical record.’

‘Which is the historical record of a practice that changed, and neither version is wrong, and the change is the interesting thing and is only visible because I have both.’


## Two rules and a naming problem

‘Practical closing,’ Raj said. ‘Because somebody reading this needs something they can do.’

‘Say yours first.’

‘Two rules. Archive the whole context rather than the page you want.’ He counted. ‘And decide about a source once rather than about a page every time.’

‘Say the first properly.’

‘When I find a page that matters on a small site, I take the whole site, which is six or eight pages and four minutes.’ Raj was definite. ‘Because the page I want is meaningless without the surrounding claims, and because I cannot predict which of the other seven will matter.’

‘Give me a case.’

‘I archived a workshop's process page and not their about page.’ He was rueful. ‘And eight months later I needed to know how long the workshop had existed, which was on the about page, which was gone.’

‘One page away.’

‘One page away and four minutes of effort at the time, and I have not made that mistake since.’

Lena gave hers.

‘Mine is timing and it is the only thing I would tell anybody.’ She was emphatic. ‘Archive before you need it rather than when you realise you need it, because by the time you realise, the decision has been made by somebody else.’

‘Which is the whole of it.’

‘Which is the whole of it and it is difficult, because archiving something you do not yet know you need feels like waste.’ Lena shrugged. ‘And it is waste, on perhaps nineteen items in twenty, and the twentieth carries a story.’

‘So the economics are asymmetric.’

‘The economics are enormously asymmetric and that is the argument. Four seconds against a gap in a piece of work that cannot be filled.’

They discussed naming, which both said they had got wrong.

‘Mine is source codes,’ Lena said. ‘A short prefix identifying the organisation, then the date, then a description.’

‘Why the prefix?’

‘Because everything about one entity sorts together, which matters when I have forty pages about the same company.’ She was matter of fact. ‘And because the source is the thing I always know when looking.’

‘Mine is project first,’ Raj said. ‘Then a description of what the page is about rather than its title.’

‘Say why not the title.’

‘Because a page title is frequently the organisation's name and nothing else, which tells me nothing.’ He shrugged. ‘Eleven pages all called the same thing is not an archive.’

‘So you rewrite it.’

‘I rewrite it into what the page actually contains, which takes ten seconds and which is what makes forty archived pages navigable.’

Lena raised the last thing.

‘There is one more decision and it is about separation,’ she said.

‘Say it.’

‘Whether archived material should be distinguishable from my own writing.’

‘And should it?’

‘Absolutely and unambiguously, and I use a description that means exactly that.’ Lena was firm. ‘Because in three years, reading my own material, the single most important fact about any passage is whether I wrote it or somebody else did.’

‘Which is provenance.’

‘Which is the whole of provenance and it costs one description on each archived entry.’ She was definite. ‘Everything else in this conversation is optimisation. That one is not optional.’


## What neither of them can archive

‘I want to name the limits,’ Lena said, ‘because somebody reading this will assume the practice covers more than it does.’

‘Say the first.’

‘Anything requiring a login.’ She was matter of fact. ‘A great deal of what I read sits behind an account, and what I can preserve is what I can reach as a reader.’

‘Which is a real gap.’

‘Which is a substantial gap and the response is not technical, it is that I write more.’ Lena counted. ‘When I cannot take a copy I write a longer note, with the specific phrasing quoted, and the date and the address.’

‘So the note becomes the record.’

‘The note becomes the only record, which is weaker and is considerably better than nothing, and it is a decision I make deliberately rather than a gap I ignore.’

‘Mine is different,’ Raj said. ‘Video.’

‘Say it.’

‘An enormous amount of what documents a craft is somebody's phone footage on a video site, and those disappear at least as often as pages.’ He shrugged. ‘And what I can preserve is the page describing it rather than the thing itself.’

‘Which is nearly useless.’

‘Which is not useless and is a considerable step down, because a description of a video is not a video.’ Raj was frank. ‘I have lost two that mattered and I have no good answer for it.’

‘What do you do?’

‘I write out what I saw, in detail, immediately, which is a poor substitute and is what somebody would have done before video existed.’ He laughed without much humour. ‘Which is not a solution. It is a person doing the best available thing.’

Lena said the third limit was the one people miss.

‘Neither of us can archive what was never published,’ she said.

‘Which sounds obvious.’

‘Which sounds obvious and matters, because the most important material in my work is generally the material somebody chose not to publish.’ She was precise. ‘Archiving is a practice about the public record, and the public record is a fraction of what exists.’

‘So it is one tool.’

‘It is one tool, addressing one category of loss, and treating it as a complete answer to evidence is how somebody ends up with a very tidy archive of what an organisation wanted them to see.’


## Becoming your own librarian

The mental model almost everybody carries is that things published on the internet stay published. What actually exists is a service somebody is providing, continuously, at their own expense, for as long as they choose, and the choosing can stop for reasons entirely unconnected to whether anybody depends on it.

The disappearances divide usefully. Ordinary decay is random with respect to importance: a site maintained by one person stops when that person moves, and the material goes whether or not it was the only record of something. Deliberate removal is the opposite and is worth treating separately, because material disappears at the moment somebody decides it should, which is precisely the moment it becomes valuable to anybody examining the situation.

Both practitioners here learned this by losing something, which appears to be the only way anybody learns it. One lost a leadership history during a corporate redesign and salvaged part of a story from a single page saved by accident. The other lost a founding history when a volunteer moved to another city and stopped paying for hosting, and discovered that a general archive service held a version two years old and missing the section he needed. A fallback is not a plan.

The category most people neglect is the one both identified independently: the unremarkable pages. About pages, history pages, structure pages, anything describing how something used to work. Coverage of a dramatic event exists in many places. A quiet page listing who held which position in which year exists in one, and it is the page that disappears during a restructure and the page that establishes the baseline against which any change is visible.

The timing problem is structural rather than a matter of discipline. Nobody archives everything, so a judgment is required, and it happens when somebody first encounters something, which is when they know least about whether it will matter. Both practitioners resolved this by making the judgment mechanical rather than evaluative. One archives anything containing a name, a figure, a date, or a document link, and archives nothing that is commentary. The other assesses the source once rather than the page repeatedly, on the reasoning that a site maintained by one person is fragile regardless of which page is in front of him.

That second approach produced the most useful exchange in the conversation. One practitioner realised she had a single rule doing two jobs badly: page-level judgment is necessary for adversarial sources whose material is actively curated, and unnecessary for the monitored publications where she had been applying it for two years. Splitting the rule by source type is a change she describes as unreachable alone.

What happens after the copy determines whether the result is an archive or a hoard. Descriptions applied at the moment of capture, since applying them later means opening hundreds of entries that nobody will open. Placement in the structure where the work lives. And a short section saying what the page establishes and why it was saved, written immediately, costing ninety seconds while the reasoning is fresh. Both practitioners independently observed that they search their own notes more often than the archived content, because the page uses its author's vocabulary and the note uses theirs.

Attached documents are the layer that changes what is possible. Many archived pages are not the material but announcements linking to it, and taking both into one entry keeps the timing, the source, and the substance together. The contents become searchable, which means a term can surface a result from page thirty of a filing nobody has read, and at least one practitioner describes finding a story that way, in a schedule at the back of a document attached for an unrelated reason.

The case neither expected is not disappearance but amendment. A page still at its address, saying something different, with a single clause removed and nothing else altered. A vanished page announces itself and makes somebody go looking; a quietly amended page makes them confidently wrong. Both now hold a small number of pages under periodic recapture, which for one is eleven pages checked quarterly and has produced two findings that could not have been asked about without the earlier version.

The rule both would state above every other concerns timing, and it is uncomfortable because it requires accepting waste.
