The transition from one person to two is the hardest scaling step any system ever takes, and it is harder than the step from two to twenty. The reason is that a single-user arrangement contains an enormous number of unstated assumptions, all of which have been correct and none of which has ever been examined. Everything is available because there is nobody it should be unavailable to. Everything is consistent because one person made every decision. Nothing needs a boundary because there is nothing on the other side of one.

A second person makes every one of those assumptions into a question. What does the other person see. What should they not see. Where do the two arrangements meet. What happens when both need the same information at once, and what happens when one needs information the other must not have. None of these questions existed the previous week, and all of them must now be answered deliberately rather than by default.

![When a Solo Practice Becomes a Group Practice](/assets/img/feature-screenshot-placeholder.svg)

In most professions these questions are matters of convenience, and getting them slightly wrong produces friction. In professions where the material concerns identifiable people, they are matters of obligation, and getting them wrong is not friction but a failure. A clinician's records belong to a relationship between that clinician and a particular person, and the existence of a colleague down the corridor does not alter who is responsible for them.
<!--more-->


What that produces is a requirement most tools handle badly: a genuine division rather than a permission setting. A permission is a rule enforced by something, which means a rule that can be misconfigured, changed, or bypassed by whoever administers it. A division is structural, which means there is no configuration to get wrong because there is nothing connecting the two halves in the first place.

Alongside the division there must be a shared area, because a group practice is not two solo practices in one building. Schedules must be visible to both. Policies must be common. Forms and templates must be consistent, since a practice that produces two different intake forms is a practice with a documentation problem. That material is administrative rather than clinical, and administrative material genuinely benefits from being shared.

So the architecture that serves this situation has two properties that sound contradictory and are not. Complete separation for anything belonging to a clinical relationship, achieved structurally rather than by rule. And genuine sharing for anything belonging to the practice as an entity, in a single place both people maintain.

There is a further requirement that only becomes visible once somebody thinks about the physical situation. Two people in one suite of rooms share equipment, corridors, and sightlines. A screen that is safe in a solo practice, where the only person who might see it is the person it belongs to, is a different object in a practice where a colleague walks past. Behaviours that were sensible defaults become inappropriate, and the change is not obvious until somebody names it.

What follows is a conversation between two clinicians working this out on the afternoon they formalised their arrangement, in a small conference room with two laptops open, before either of them had seen a patient under the new one.


## Two laptops and an afternoon

The room had a table, four chairs, and a window that faced a car park, and Meera had described it in the lease negotiation as adequate.

Sarah had brought a list, which Meera had come to expect from her.

‘Four questions,’ she said. ‘And the first one determines the other three, so I want to settle it before we do anything.’

‘Say it.’

‘Whether we have one arrangement or two.’

Meera nodded slowly.

‘Which is the whole question and I have been thinking about it for a fortnight,’ she said. ‘Say what the options look like to you.’

‘One folder that we both connect to, with clinical material protected individually.’ Sarah counted. ‘Which means my patients are in the same place as yours and are unreadable to you because they are protected with my password.’

‘Which would work.’

‘Which would technically work and which I have a reservation about that I am struggling to articulate.’

‘Then let me articulate it and you tell me whether it is yours.’ Meera was precise. ‘Your patients' records would be in a file that I open every day.’

Sarah sat back.

‘That is exactly it.’

‘Which is not a claim that anything would go wrong.’ Meera was careful. ‘The protection is genuine. I could not read them.’

‘But they would be there.’

‘They would be in a folder on a machine I use, and the fact that they are unreadable is a property of a mechanism rather than a property of the situation.’ She was definite. ‘And I have spent fifteen years arranging things so that the situation itself is safe rather than relying on mechanisms.’

‘Say the alternative.’

‘Two entirely separate arrangements. Your folder, my folder, no relationship between them of any kind.’

‘Which is harder.’

‘Which is not harder at all, which is the part I did not expect.’ Meera pulled her laptop round. ‘It is one file. You open it, you connect it to your folder, and that is your arrangement. I open the same file, connect it to mine, and that is mine.’

Sarah looked at it for a moment.

‘So the separation costs nothing.’

‘The separation costs nothing and it is complete, and there is no setting anybody could get wrong because there is nothing joining them.’

‘Then why is this a conversation rather than a decision?’

‘Because two separate arrangements is not a practice,’ Meera said. ‘It is two people in a building.’


## What a practice needs to share

‘So there is a third thing,’ Sarah said.

‘There is a third thing and working out what belongs in it took me most of last week.’

‘Say what you arrived at.’

‘A shared arrangement, which is a third folder that we both connect to, containing everything that belongs to the practice rather than to either of us.’ Meera counted. ‘Which is a considerable amount and none of it is clinical.’

‘Say the categories.’

‘Policies. Scheduling. Insurance and billing. Professional development. And templates.’

‘Say templates, because that is the one I care about most.’

‘Which I thought you would.’ Meera opened something. ‘Because a practice with two clinicians producing two different intake documents is a practice with a problem, and it is exactly the kind of problem that appears eighteen months later during something unpleasant.’

‘So we agree the forms.’

‘We agree the forms and they live in one place and neither of us keeps a private copy.’ She was definite. ‘Which is the whole point of shared material. One version, in one place, that both of us take from.’

Sarah was already looking at the structure.

‘Say how you have arranged it.’

‘Five areas at the top, which are the five categories.’ Meera showed her. ‘With sub-areas beneath where they need them.’

‘Which is fewer than I expected.’

‘Which is deliberately fewer, because I built my own arrangement four years ago with far too much structure and spent a year removing it.’ She was matter of fact. ‘Five things I can take in without reading, and everything else goes underneath.’

‘Say what is under policies.’

‘Consent procedures. Record retention. What we do when somebody does not attend. Emergency contact protocol.’ Meera counted. ‘Which are the four we actually have and which took an afternoon to write and which we both signed.’

‘Which is a practice document rather than yours.’

‘Which is the difference between a practice and two people, and I want to say that I found writing them clarifying rather than tedious.’ She was thoughtful. ‘I have been operating four policies for fifteen years and I had never written any of them down, because there was nobody to tell.’

Sarah asked about the templates.

‘Show me the intake one.’

Meera opened it. Sarah read for a while.

‘This is your structure,’ she said. ‘The four sections.’

‘Which is a suggestion rather than a requirement, and I want that clear.’ Meera was careful. ‘Your clinical arrangement is yours. If your sessions want a different shape, they should have one.’

‘I have been using four sections since you set me up and I have not questioned it.’

‘Then question it now, before you have three hundred records shaped that way.’ She was definite. ‘Because the thing I would tell anybody about section structure is that it is very cheap to decide and very expensive to change.’

Sarah thought about it.

‘I would add one,’ she said. ‘Risk.’

‘Say why.’

‘Because I came from a service where risk assessment was a separate document and I have been putting it in observations, and it does not sit right.’ She was precise. ‘It is a different kind of statement.’

‘Then it is a different section, and that is a good reason, and your records should have five.’

‘Which means our records differ.’

‘Which means our records differ and that is entirely correct,’ Meera said. ‘We share a practice. We do not share a clinical method.’


## The thing Sarah had already done

‘There is something I should tell you and I have been slightly dreading it,’ Sarah said.

‘Say it.’

‘I have been keeping a separate document about the practice.’ She was frank. ‘Since before any of this. Things I did not want to ask about, questions about how you do things, notes about the building.’

Meera laughed.

‘Say why you were dreading telling me.’

‘Because it looks like I have been keeping notes about you.’

‘Which you have been, and which is entirely reasonable, and I did the same thing when I joined a practice at thirty-one.’ Meera was matter of fact. ‘Say what is in it.’

‘Practical things.’ Sarah counted. ‘Which cupboard the forms are in. Who to telephone about the heating. That the second consulting room is cold in the mornings.’

‘Which is institutional knowledge and which exists nowhere.’

‘Which exists in your head and which I have been reconstructing by asking you the same questions twice.’

Meera sat back.

‘That should go in the shared arrangement,’ she said. ‘And it should not be called what you are calling it.’

‘Say what it should be called.’

‘Practice operations, or something equally dull, and it should be one of the five areas rather than a document.’ She was definite. ‘Because everything in it is exactly what a third clinician will need in September and will otherwise ask both of us about, twice.’

‘Which is what I have been doing.’

‘Which is what everybody does when they join anything, and which nobody writes down because the person who knows does not know they know it.’ Meera was thoughtful. ‘I could not have written that document. I would not have known what was in it.’

‘Because you have known where the forms are for fifteen years.’

‘Because I have not had to find out anything about this building since I signed the lease, which means I am the worst possible person to record how it works.’ She shrugged. ‘You are the best, and only for about another month.’

Sarah understood immediately.

‘Because after a month I will have stopped noticing.’

‘Because after a month you will know where the forms are and you will never again be able to see which of the things you know are things somebody has to be told.’ Meera was definite. ‘Which is an argument for writing it now rather than when it is convenient.’


## The schedule that both of them see

‘Now the part that actually has to work every day,’ Sarah said. ‘Because policies are written once and a schedule is consulted forty times.’

‘Which is why it lives in the shared arrangement and why I want to be careful about what is in it.’

‘Say the concern.’

‘That a schedule is a list of names and times, and a list of names and times is information about who is receiving treatment.’ Meera was precise. ‘Which is not clinical detail and is not nothing.’

‘So what goes in?’

‘Initials and a marker for which of us.’ She showed the arrangement. ‘Which is enough for scheduling and is not enough for anything else.’

‘Say the marker.’

‘A description on each appointment saying whose it is.’ Meera counted. ‘Which means I can filter the shared schedule to mine, or to yours, or see both.’

‘Which is what I need for coverage.’

‘Which is exactly what you need when I am away and you are covering, and which is the only reason either of us needs to see the other's appointments at all.’

Sarah was working through it.

‘And the room.’

‘And the room, which is the practical constraint we actually have, because we have two consulting rooms and three people including the trainee in September.’ Meera was matter of fact. ‘So the schedule is doing double duty as a room booking.’

‘Which is administrative in the purest sense.’

‘Which is exactly the kind of thing a shared arrangement is for, and it is why I stopped keeping my appointments in my own arrangement entirely.’

‘Say what you did before.’

‘My appointments were dates on the clinical records themselves, which worked perfectly for one person and does not work at all for two.’ She shrugged. ‘Because you cannot see them and should not be able to.’

‘So the dates moved.’

‘The dates moved into the shared schedule and the clinical records kept theirs, which means a record still surfaces when it needs reading and the schedule tells us both what the week looks like.’

‘Two things holding the same information.’

‘Two things holding different information about the same appointment.’ Meera was precise. ‘The schedule holds when and where and which of us. The record holds a date meaning read this before that appointment. Which are not the same fact.’


## What stays where it is

‘Say what does not change,’ Sarah said.

‘Almost everything about how either of us works clinically.’ Meera was matter of fact. ‘Which is the part I want to emphasise, because a change of this kind can turn into a reason to rebuild everything.’

‘Which would be a mistake.’

‘Which would be an enormous mistake and I nearly made it.’ She was frank. ‘I spent an evening planning a restructure of my own clinical arrangement to align with yours, and then I asked myself what problem it solved.’

‘And?’

‘None. My records serve me and yours serve you, and there is no operation either of us performs that requires them to look alike.’ Meera shrugged. ‘So mine is unchanged. Four hundred records, three sections, protected, on my machine.’

‘And mine continues as it is.’

‘With five sections, since ten minutes ago, and entirely independently.’

Sarah asked about the boundary.

‘What is the rule for what never crosses?’

‘Anything from a session.’ Meera was immediate. ‘Which is a simpler rule than any I could construct about categories, and simple rules are the ones people follow.’

‘Say the awkward case.’

‘Somebody telephones the practice in difficulty and reaches you, and they are my patient.’ She counted. ‘Which happens, and which produces information that is clinical and that I need.’

‘And which cannot go in the shared arrangement.’

‘Which must not go in the shared arrangement, so it does not.’ Meera was definite. ‘You telephone me, or you tell me, and I write it in my record.’

‘Which is a conversation rather than a document.’

‘Which is a conversation rather than a document, deliberately, and I want to say that this is not a limitation we are working around.’ She was precise. ‘Two clinicians discussing a patient in person is the correct mechanism. It has always been the correct mechanism.’

‘Rather than a written handover.’

‘A written handover between us, held in a place we both reach, is a document that exists and that nobody needs.’ Meera shrugged. ‘The record belongs in one place, with one clinician, and the conversation is how it gets there.’

Sarah made a note.

‘There is a case I want to raise,’ she said. ‘Which is coverage.’

‘Which is the genuinely difficult one and I have an answer I am not entirely happy with.’

‘Say it.’

‘I am away for a fortnight in June. Four of my patients have appointments in that fortnight.’ Meera counted. ‘Which means you are seeing people you have never met, about whom you know nothing.’

‘Which is not workable.’

‘Which is not workable and the alternative, giving you access to my records, is also not workable.’ She was definite. ‘So the answer is a written summary, in the shared arrangement, for those four people, for that fortnight.’

‘Say what is in it.’

‘Initials. What the work is broadly about. Anything about risk. And what I would want you to know before somebody walks in.’ Meera was precise. ‘Which is perhaps two hundred words each and which is considerably less than a record.’

‘Which is a judgement about what is necessary.’

‘Which is exactly the judgement, and it is the same judgement I make writing to a general practitioner.’ She was matter of fact. ‘What is necessary for the purpose and nothing beyond it.’

‘And afterward?’

‘Afterward the summaries are removed, because their purpose has ended, and you write your own record of the sessions you conducted in your own arrangement.’ Meera counted. ‘Which are your records of your encounters, and which stay with you.’

Sarah considered that.

‘So one patient ends up with material in two places.’

‘One patient ends up with a record of my work with them and a record of your work with them, held by each of us.’ She was definite. ‘Which is exactly what happens when two clinicians see somebody, in any practice, on paper or otherwise. It is not a consequence of any arrangement. It is a consequence of two people.’


## The habit that has to change

‘There is something I need to tell you and it is a change for me rather than for you,’ Meera said.

‘Say it.’

‘I have been letting my password stay available during a working period for fifteen years.’

‘Which is what you taught me.’

‘Which is what I taught you and which is correct in a solo practice and is not correct here.’ She was precise. ‘I have thought about this a great deal and I am confident about it.’

‘Say the reasoning.’

‘In the shared arrangement, neither of us should let a password persist.’ Meera counted. ‘Because you might sit down at a machine I was using. Because either of us might be interrupted. Because the shared arrangement is on both our machines.’

‘Which is a different situation from my own records.’

‘Which is an entirely different situation, and the distinction matters.’ She was definite. ‘In your own arrangement, on your own machine, caching is correct and I would keep doing it. Anything else would be unworkable during a fifty-minute appointment.’

‘So the rule differs by arrangement.’

‘The rule differs by whether anybody else could plausibly be at the machine, which is a question about the room rather than about the software.’ Meera shrugged. ‘Solo practice, one answer. Shared practice, two answers.’

Sarah asked the obvious question.

‘Does the shared arrangement contain anything protected at all?’

‘Almost nothing, deliberately.’ Meera was matter of fact. ‘Which is a design decision rather than an accident, because anything requiring protection in a shared place is a signal that it is in the wrong place.’

‘Say the exceptions.’

‘Two. Our professional indemnity details and a small number of employment matters, both of which concern us rather than any patient.’ She counted. ‘And I would say those are the boundary cases rather than the rule.’

‘Which is a useful test.’

‘Which is the test I would give anybody. If something in your shared area needs protecting, ask whether it should be there at all.’

Sarah raised the other habit.

‘And blanking the screen.’

‘Which becomes more important rather than less, and which I want to be explicit about.’ Meera was definite. ‘We have two consulting rooms and one corridor and we walk past each other perhaps thirty times a day.’

‘Which was never true before.’

‘Which was never true in fifteen years of solo practice, where the only person who could see my screen was me.’ She was matter of fact. ‘So blanking on standing up was a habit I had for patients. It is now a habit I have for both of us.’

‘Which sounds like distrust and is not.’

‘Which is emphatically not distrust and I want that said plainly.’ She was firm. ‘It is that I should not be in a position where I have seen the name of one of your patients, because then I know something I have no business knowing and neither of us can undo it.’

‘So it protects both of us.’

‘It protects me from knowing and it protects you from having to wonder,’ Meera said. ‘Which are two separate benefits and the second one is the reason I would insist on it even if I were confident about the first.’


## What happens at three

‘There is a trainee starting in September,’ Sarah said. ‘Which I have been deliberately not thinking about until we settled this.’

‘Which is sensible and which is now worth thinking about, because it is the test of whether what we have built is a structure or a special case.’

‘Say what happens.’

‘She gets a folder.’ Meera was matter of fact. ‘Which is the entire answer and which took me a while to believe.’

‘Say it properly.’

‘Her clinical work is her own arrangement, on her own machine, exactly as ours are.’ She counted. ‘She connects to the shared arrangement for policies, templates, and the schedule. And her appointments get a description saying they are hers.’

‘Which is one description and one folder.’

‘Which is one description and one folder, and there is nothing else to do.’ Meera was definite. ‘No account to create. No permission to grant. Nobody to ask.’

‘Which is the part I would not have expected.’

‘Which is the part that persuaded me the arrangement scales, because I tried to imagine what breaks at four clinicians and I could not find anything.’ She was thoughtful. ‘The shared arrangement gets more schedule entries and one more description. Everything clinical is unaffected because everything clinical is already separate.’

‘Say what would break.’

‘The schedule, eventually, if the practice became large.’ Meera counted. ‘Six clinicians in a shared schedule is a considerable number of appointments, and at that point somebody would want to filter by default rather than by choice.’

‘Which is a filtering problem rather than a structural one.’

‘Which is a filtering problem and we are two people, so it is a problem for a version of this practice that may never exist.’

Sarah asked something else.

‘What happens if somebody leaves?’

Meera paused.

‘That is the better question and I had not asked it.’

‘Say what you think.’

‘Their clinical records go with them, because they are theirs.’ She was working through it. ‘Their obligations to those patients are theirs and do not transfer to me because they used to rent a room here.’

‘Which is legally correct as far as I know.’

‘Which is legally correct and it is also what the arrangement produces automatically, which is a pleasant coincidence rather than something I designed.’ Meera was matter of fact. ‘Their folder is on their machine. They leave, and it leaves.’

‘And the shared arrangement.’

‘Stays, minus their schedule entries, which is an afternoon of tidying.’ She shrugged. ‘And their contributions to policies and templates remain, because those belong to the practice.’

‘Which is the correct division.’

‘Which is the correct division and I want to note that neither of us worked it out,’ Meera said. ‘It falls out of having put clinical material in individual folders and practice material in a shared one, which we did for entirely different reasons.’


## What Meera is not certain about

‘I want to say something that is not a plan,’ Meera said, near the end of the afternoon.

‘Go on.’

‘I have been alone for fifteen years and I do not know how I will find this.’

Sarah waited.

‘Say what you mean.’

‘Every arrangement we have described this afternoon is about separation.’ Meera was precise. ‘Separate folders, separate records, separate responsibilities, a boundary nothing crosses.’

‘Which is correct.’

‘Which is entirely correct and it is also a description of two people working alone in the same building.’ She was frank. ‘And the reason I took on a colleague is that I did not want to work alone any more.’

‘That is a considerably more interesting problem than the folders.’

‘Which is why I have left it until last and which is why I do not have an answer.’ Meera shrugged. ‘The clinical separation is not negotiable. So whatever I actually wanted has to happen somewhere else.’

‘Say where you think it happens.’

‘In this room, probably. On Thursday afternoons, which I have blocked out for both of us and not told you why.’

Sarah laughed.

‘I had noticed and I had assumed it was administrative.’

‘It is supervision, in both directions, which at our respective stages is a slightly awkward word.’ Meera was careful. ‘I have been practising for fifteen years and you have been practising for eleven, and neither of us has had anybody to talk to about a difficult case in a considerable time.’

‘Which is a thing no arrangement of folders addresses.’

‘Which is a thing no arrangement addresses and which is the actual reason for the change, and I nearly spent the whole afternoon on the folders and forgot to say it.’ She was matter of fact. ‘Which is what happens when the practical questions are interesting.’

‘Say what happens on a Thursday.’

‘We talk about cases without records in front of us, in a room, with the door shut.’ Meera was definite. ‘Which is how it has always been done and which is the one part of clinical practice that has never needed a document.’

‘And nothing goes in either arrangement afterward.’

‘Whatever I conclude about my own patient goes in my own record, in my own words, as a clinical note.’ She counted. ‘Which is the same as any supervision I have ever had, and which is the only place any of it belongs.’


## Adding folders rather than infrastructure

Scaling a knowledge arrangement from one person to two exposes every assumption that has been holding silently. Everything is available because there was nobody it should be unavailable to. Everything is consistent because one person made every decision. Nothing needed a boundary because there was nothing on the other side. A second person turns each of those into a question requiring a deliberate answer.

The two clinicians here considered a single shared arrangement with individual protection separating clinical material, and rejected it for a reason worth stating carefully. The protection would have been genuine and one of them would still have held the other's patient records in a folder she opened daily. Her objection was not that anything would go wrong but that the safety would have been a property of a mechanism rather than of the situation, and she has spent fifteen years arranging things so the situation itself is safe.

What they built instead is three arrangements rather than one. Two individual clinical arrangements with no relationship between them, and a third shared arrangement holding everything belonging to the practice rather than to either clinician. The separation is structural rather than configured, which means there is no setting anybody could get wrong because nothing joins the two halves.

The shared arrangement holds five categories, which is deliberately few: policies, scheduling, insurance and billing, professional development, and templates. One clinician notes that writing four policies down took an afternoon and was clarifying rather than tedious, since she had been operating them for fifteen years without ever having to state them, because there had been nobody to state them to.

Templates matter more than they appear, since a practice producing two different intake documents has a documentation problem that surfaces eighteen months later during something unpleasant. What does not need to be common is clinical method: one clinician uses four sections and the other adopted a fifth for risk assessment during the conversation, on the grounds that it is a different kind of statement from an observation. The practice is shared and the clinical method is not.

The schedule illustrates the boundary precisely. Initials and a marker for which clinician, which is enough for coordination and room booking and is not enough for anything else. One clinician moved her appointments out of her own records into the shared schedule, since dates on clinical records worked for one person and cannot work for two, while keeping dates on the records themselves for a different purpose. Two things holding different facts about the same appointment.

The rule for what never crosses is deliberately simple: anything from a session. Simple rules are the ones people follow, and the awkward cases resolve through conversation rather than through documents. When one clinician takes a call about the other's patient, she telephones rather than writing, because two clinicians discussing a patient in person is the correct mechanism and always has been. A written handover between colleagues in a shared place is a document that exists and that nobody needs.

Coverage is the genuinely hard case and produced the least comfortable answer: brief summaries in the shared arrangement for the specific patients and the specific period, containing initials, the broad nature of the work, anything about risk, and what somebody would want to know before a person walks in. Around two hundred words each, removed when the purpose ends, with the covering clinician writing her own records of her own sessions. The judgement involved is the same one that governs writing to any other professional: what is necessary for the purpose and nothing beyond it.

Two habits changed, and both concern the room rather than the software. Password caching, which is correct and necessary in a solo arrangement, is inappropriate in a shared one where either person might be at a machine. And blanking a screen, previously a habit maintained for patients, becomes a habit maintained for a colleague. One clinician is emphatic that this is not distrust: it protects her from knowing something she has no business knowing, and it protects her colleague from having to wonder whether she does.

The test of whether any of this is a structure or a special case is what happens at three, and the answer is that a new clinician gets a folder and a description. No account, no permission, nobody to ask. What breaks eventually is the schedule at a size neither of them expects to reach, which is a filtering question rather than a structural one.

The question neither had asked, raised at the end, is what happens when somebody leaves. Their clinical records go with them, because their obligations to those patients are theirs and do not transfer to anybody who rented them a room. Their folder is on their machine; they leave and it leaves. The shared material stays, since it belongs to the practice. Neither clinician designed that outcome. It falls out of having put clinical material in individual folders and practice material in a shared one, for entirely different reasons.
