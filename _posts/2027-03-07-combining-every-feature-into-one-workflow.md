There is a way of assessing tools that almost everybody uses and that almost nobody defends when it is stated plainly, which is to evaluate capabilities one at a time. Somebody lists what a thing can do, examines each item, and forms a judgement by aggregation. The method is tidy, it produces comparable results, and it measures something other than what determines whether a tool is any good.

What actually determines that is how the capabilities behave in sequence, under the conditions of a real working day, when somebody is thinking about their work rather than about the tool. A capability that performs impressively in isolation and requires a change of context to reach is a capability that will be used during a demonstration and skipped during a busy Tuesday. The demonstration is not a small version of the working day; it is a different activity with different constraints.

![Combining Every Feature Into One Workflow](/assets/img/feature-screenshot-placeholder.svg)

The constraint that matters most is the interval. Professional work is not conducted in uninterrupted hours; it happens in the gaps between other things, and the gaps have shapes. Four minutes before somebody arrives. Fifteen minutes between commitments. Ninety seconds while a question is still open. Any capability requiring more time than the gap allows is a capability that exists and is not used, which is indistinguishable from its absence.
<!--more-->


The second constraint is switching. Every transition between contexts costs something, and the cost is larger than the seconds involved because it interrupts whatever state somebody had established. A workflow requiring four applications is not four times slower than one requiring a single application; it is qualitatively different, because the person spends part of their attention on the mechanics of moving between things rather than on the thing itself.

Which produces the standard by which any tool should actually be judged. Not whether each capability is good, but whether somebody using them in sequence experiences a continuous activity or a series of separate operations. The measure of success is that the person stops noticing the tool, which sounds like faint praise and is the highest achievement available to anything somebody uses every working day.

That standard is difficult to demonstrate abstractly, because it concerns transitions rather than features and transitions are invisible when described one at a time. What shows it is a working day, followed from beginning to end, with attention paid to the joins rather than to the capabilities.

What follows is one such day, in a solo psychiatric practice, from the first opening of a laptop to the moment it closes. The person is thinking about her patients throughout. The tool appears in this account far more often than it appeared in her attention.


## Eight fifteen

The first thing on the screen is not a list of anything. It is a small panel telling her what is coming.

Two patients before lunch, one at two, and one at four. She knows this. She has known it since Friday. What the panel is for is the possibility that something has changed, which happens perhaps twice a month and which she would otherwise discover by somebody not arriving.

Nothing has changed. She reads it in four seconds and moves on.

Below it, a shorter list: what she opened yesterday. She does not consult this deliberately so much as absorb it. Three names, and the third one is the reason she opens it at all, because a record she touched yesterday afternoon is a record with something unfinished in it and she cannot at this moment remember what.

She opens it. A treatment plan section with two lines she wrote and did not complete. Not urgent. She reads them, understands what she meant, and adds nothing.

That interaction took ninety seconds and would not have happened at all if the list had required her to go looking for it. She is aware of this in an abstract way and has never articulated it. What she experiences is simply having remembered something.

The panel on the left carries a small indication that something is due this week. One patient's treatment review, which is a six-monthly obligation and which she would not otherwise have thought about until it was late. She notes it. Thursday, probably.

Then the list of what she has starred, which is her current roster: the patients she is seeing this week, currently eleven of them, rotated as her week rotates. She does not open any of them. She simply sees the names and the day acquires a shape.

Coffee. Chai, in fact, which is a detail nobody would care about and which is the actual reason the first fifteen minutes of her day feel like a beginning rather than a start.


## Eight fifty

Her first patient arrives at nine. She has ten minutes and she needs about five.

She opens the patient's record. It asks for the password, which she enters once. This is the moment she would describe, if anybody asked, as the point at which she stops being a person with a laptop and starts being a clinician, though she has never said that aloud and would find it slightly pretentious.

The password will not be requested again for the duration of this working period, which is the property that makes the whole arrangement usable. She spent a period early on imagining an alternative, where every touch required re-entry, and had concluded that nobody could work that way. She was correct and has not thought about it since.

The record opens into its familiar shape. Three sections, the same three in every one of her four hundred records. Session notes. Treatment plan. Follow-up.

She reads the most recent session in full. Four hundred words, written by her, about eight days ago, describing a conversation she remembers in outline and not in detail. What she recovers from reading is the specific thing: the phrasing somebody used about their sister, which she had noted at the time and which had struck her as significant.

She would not have recalled that phrasing. She is completely certain of this, and it is the reason she reads before every appointment rather than trusting her sense of where things stand.

The two sessions before it are collapsed. She expands one, scans the observations, collapses it again. This takes eleven seconds and gives her the trajectory rather than the detail, which is what she wants from material three weeks old.

The descriptions on the record sit at the top: anxiety, medication management, long term. She does not read these deliberately. They are the shape of the record and she perceives them the way she perceives the colour of a folder.

There is a document attached from another provider, an assessment from before this patient came to her. She does not open it. She searches for a term inside it, from the search box, and the relevant passage surfaces without her having to read forty pages. This takes six seconds and would have taken four minutes with the document open.

Nine o'clock. She closes nothing and stands up to open the door.


## Nine to nine fifty

She writes during sessions, which some colleagues consider a mistake and which she has thought about carefully.

The argument against is that a person typing is a person not attending. The argument for is that a person reconstructing at the end of a day is a person producing a summary rather than a record. She has resolved it by writing very little during and a great deal immediately after, which means what happens in the room is perhaps forty words across fifty minutes.

Today it is three fragments. A phrase the patient uses about work. A change in how she describes her sleep. And one observation about affect that Meera wants to remember and would not.

At around the twenty-minute mark the patient mentions a medication she has stopped taking. Meera has a vague sense that this was discussed and would not assert it.

She types the name into the search box while the patient is still speaking. Three appointments back, in a session note, with what the patient said at the time about why she had stopped.

She does not read it aloud. She reads it in about four seconds and says something that reflects having known it, which is the difference between a clinician who remembers a patient and one who asks a patient to repeat herself. She has come to regard this as a small ethical matter rather than a convenience.

Thirty-five minutes in, somebody knocks. A delivery, badly timed.

She locks the screen before standing up. The whole display goes opaque, immediately, one action. The patient sees a blurred surface with nothing legible on it.

She has done this perhaps six thousand times and it no longer registers as an action. What she notices is that she has stopped worrying about it, which took about a year.

Ninety seconds later she is back, unlocked, and the conversation resumes. The patient has said nothing about the interruption and has not looked at the screen, and Meera would not have known whether she had.

At forty-five minutes the patient produces a letter from another provider, folded, from a bag.

Meera photographs it. It attaches to today's section rather than to the record as a whole, which matters because in eight months somebody may ask when this arrived and the answer will be visible rather than inferred.

Fifty minutes. The patient leaves.


## Nine fifty to ten

Ten minutes, and this is the part that matters most and that nobody sees.

She creates a section for today, titled with the date and nothing else. Underneath it she writes properly, expanding three fragments into four hundred words, while the conversation is still entirely present.

She uses a heading to separate what was said from what she observed, which is a distinction she considers fundamental and which is invisible in most clinical records. What somebody reports and what a clinician notices are different kinds of claim and she has never been comfortable with them sharing a paragraph.

Two phrases go in bold. Not because they are important in a general sense but because they are the things she will want her eye to find when she reads this before the next appointment. She is writing for herself in eight days, and she knows what that person will be looking for.

There is a medication decision today, which she makes rarely and treats seriously. It goes in a block with a coloured bar down the side, set apart from the prose entirely.

The reason is retrieval rather than emphasis. In four months, scanning this record, she will not read paragraphs. She will look for the block. A decision buried in a paragraph is a decision she has recorded and not captured, which is a distinction she learned expensively about a year in.

She sets a date for the next appointment on the record itself, which means the record will appear when it needs to be read rather than requiring her to remember that it exists.

She looks at the descriptions. Nothing has changed today. The clinical picture is what it was. She adds nothing, and the absence of a change is itself a small piece of information she registers without recording.

Then something occurs to her about the medication the patient mentioned, and she searches it across everything.

Four records contain it. She scans them, which takes perhaps forty seconds, and confirms that what she was half-remembering is not a pattern. It is two patients, unconnected, and the resemblance she had noticed is a resemblance rather than a finding.

This is the operation she would describe as the most professionally valuable thing here, if anybody asked and if she could explain it without sounding as though she were doing research on her own patients. What it actually is: a way of checking whether an impression is real, and an impression checked against itself is not a check.

Ten o'clock. Next patient. The record from the last one closes and its protection resumes without her doing anything.


## Eleven fifteen

Fifteen minutes and no patient, which happens twice a week and which she protects.

She opens her reading, which is four sources and takes about eleven minutes on a good day. Two clinical, one adjacent, and one that is not about her field at all and which she keeps because it occasionally saves her from thinking in only one direction.

Today an article describes a technique for something she encounters constantly and has never handled particularly well.

She does not finish the article. She stops about two thirds of the way through, at the moment the idea takes hold, and creates a new entry with the button in the corner.

Four sentences. What the technique is, why it might suit two specific people she is currently seeing, and one reservation about it. She describes it with a category she uses for exactly this, which currently holds about thirty items, and stars it so she encounters it again.

The whole interruption takes under two minutes and she returns to the article, which she now reads differently, because she has already extracted the thing that mattered.

She has thought about why she writes before finishing. The answer is that a reaction has a life of about four minutes, and an article finished first produces a note about the article rather than a note about the reaction, and the reaction was the valuable part.

Afterward she looks at her figures, which she does perhaps monthly and did last week.

Twelve hundred sessions documented. Four hundred patients. A chart showing her activity across the last fortnight, which is remarkably even and which describes somebody more methodical than she would claim to be.

She is aware that this number is the only complete account of her professional output that exists anywhere, and that she did not construct it deliberately. It accumulated because she wrote things down.


## Eleven thirty

There is one record she opens before the afternoon and it is not for a patient she is seeing today.

A long-term case, eighteen months, where the treatment plan has been revised perhaps six times and where she has been quietly uncertain for a fortnight about whether the current direction is right.

She opens the history on that record, which shows every previous state of it, most recent first.

What she is looking at is not a backup. It is a sequence of decisions with dates on them, and reading it in order is reading her own reasoning as it developed.

Four revisions ago she considered an approach she did not take. She had entirely forgotten this. Reading it now, she can see what made her set it aside, and the reason was sound at the time and the circumstances that made it sound have changed.

She sits with that for about a minute.

This is the operation she would defend most strongly to a sceptical colleague, and it has nothing to do with data protection. It is that a clinician's reasoning develops across months and is invisible to the clinician doing the developing, because each individual revision felt like a small sensible adjustment.

Reading six revisions consecutively shows a direction of travel. She can see that she has been moving steadily toward something more conservative, across a period during which the patient has not become more fragile, and she cannot immediately say why.

She writes four lines about this in the treatment plan section, as a note to herself rather than as a clinical statement, and she will raise it in supervision.

That entire sequence took eleven minutes and produced the most significant clinical thought she will have today.


## One forty

The two o'clock patient telephones to reschedule.

Meera is standing, holding a phone in one hand, with the laptop open on the desk beside her.

She types three letters. The record appears before she has finished, from a list of suggestions that narrowed as she typed.

She changes the date while the patient is still speaking, confirms it aloud, and the conversation ends.

Fifteen seconds, including the part where she was listening rather than typing.

She has a specific memory of the equivalent operation in her previous arrangement, which involved a calendar in one place, a record in another, and a period of holding a telephone against her shoulder. She estimates it at ninety seconds and a small amount of stress.

The difference is not the seventy-five seconds. It is that the shorter version happens while she is still attending to the person on the telephone, and the longer version does not.


## Two twenty

The two o'clock patient rescheduled and the two twenty is somebody she has seen eleven times.

She prepares in four minutes rather than five, because this record is one she knows.

What she notices while reading is something she was not looking for. The treatment plan section is empty for the last three sessions.

An empty section in her records is not an absence. It is a statement. It says that nothing changed, deliberately, on three consecutive occasions.

Which she now looks at and finds interesting rather than reassuring. Three sessions without a change is a plateau, and a plateau is either stability or stasis and those look identical from inside a single appointment.

She would not have seen it if the section were sometimes present and sometimes not. She would have seen three records that happened not to mention the treatment plan, which is a completely different observation and one she would have passed over.

This is the argument for identical structure that she gives to anybody who asks and that she has never heard anybody else make. The value is not that things are easy to find. It is that a gap becomes visible.

She writes one line about the plateau before the patient arrives, in the follow-up section, so that she is holding a question rather than an impression when the conversation starts.

The session goes somewhere she did not anticipate and the question turns out to be the right one.

Afterward she thinks about the fact that this came from a formatting decision she made in her first month, for reasons that were entirely administrative, and which has been producing clinical observations for fifteen years without her having noticed the mechanism.


## Three fifteen

There is a part of the afternoon that does not appear in any account of a workflow because nothing happens in it.

The three o'clock patient has cancelled, which she learned this morning. She has fifty minutes and no obligation.

She does not open anything. She sits with a second chai and looks at the window and thinks about the eleven thirty record, which has been sitting somewhere in her attention since.

This is not a gap in the day's productivity. It is the part of clinical work that most resembles the work itself, and she has become better at protecting it.

What she notices, after about twenty minutes, is that she is thinking clearly rather than trying to. There is nothing she is holding. The eleven patients she is seeing this week are on a screen she is not looking at. The dates she must attend to will present themselves. The four hundred people whose details she used to carry are in four hundred records she can open in seconds.

She has written about this to somebody, once, in a message that took her three attempts. What she wanted to say and could not quite say is that externalising the accounting did not make her a less attentive clinician. It made her a more attentive one, because the attention that had been going into holding things is now available for the thing in front of her.

At the time she was not sure whether that was true or whether it was a justification.

She is more confident now, and the evidence is exactly this fifty minutes: an unplanned interval in which her mind went immediately to the clinical question that mattered rather than to a mental inventory of what she might be forgetting.

She would not have had that fifty minutes eight years ago. She would have had fifty minutes of catching up.


## Six ten

The last patient left at ten past five and the documentation took forty minutes.

She has not saved anything today, deliberately or otherwise. She has not thought about saving.

Before closing the laptop she looks at the small indicator near the top, which says everything is written. She does this perhaps once a week and has never seen it say anything else.

She closes the tab. Nothing objects, which is how she knows there was nothing pending.

The laptop closes. Four hundred patient records, protected, on a machine in a locked office. Nothing has been transmitted anywhere at any point during the day, which she is aware of in the way somebody is aware that their door is locked: not continuously, and completely.

She has thought about her patients today. Eleven separate hours of thinking about specific people and what is happening to them.

She has not thought about the tool at all, except twice: once when the search returned four records and she noticed the operation was useful, and once when reading her own revisions and finding something she had forgotten deciding.

Both of those were thoughts about her work. Neither was a thought about software.


## What she does not put in

There is one more thing about the day and it concerns what is absent from the records rather than from the workflow.

At around four twenty, a patient says something about her brother. It is not clinically relevant. It is context, offered because the person was talking and because talking sometimes goes sideways.

Meera does not write it down.

This is a decision she makes perhaps four times a week and it takes no time at all, which is why it does not appear in any description of a workflow. What she is deciding is that a permanent record about a person who is not her patient, who has consented to nothing, serves no purpose in a document about somebody else's treatment.

Occasionally she is wrong and something omitted turns out to matter and she has to ask again. That costs a minute and she considers it a good trade.

She is aware that nothing in her arrangement helps her with this. There is no mechanism that could. A record will hold whatever she types into it, indefinitely, and the question of whether it should is entirely hers.

She finds this appropriate rather than a deficiency. It is the one part of her practice that cannot be assisted and should not be, and she would be uncomfortable with any tool that offered an opinion about it.

She has thought occasionally about what a colleague would find if they read four hundred of her records. What they would find is a considerable amount about four hundred people and almost nothing about anybody else, which is a property she has maintained deliberately for fifteen years and which nobody has ever noticed or commented on.

That is the kind of professional discipline that leaves no trace when it is working, which is true of most of them.


## What the day did not contain

It is worth stating what was absent, because the absences are the point.

There was no moment at which she moved material from one place to another. The letter a patient produced was photographed into the record where it belongs and did not pass through a folder, a transfer, or an intention to file it later.

There was no moment at which she left one environment to consult another. The medication query, the assessment document, the reading, and the history all occurred in the same place, which is why each of them took seconds rather than minutes.

There was no moment at which a capability was unavailable because of where she was or what she was doing. The search during a session, the lock when somebody knocked, and the reschedule while holding a telephone all happened at exactly the moment they were needed.

There was no reconciliation at the end of the day. Nothing had accumulated anywhere requiring processing, because everything had been recorded where it belonged as it occurred.

And there was no moment at which she considered whether her work was safe.

Each of those absences corresponds to something that would have been present in an arrangement assembled from separate parts, and each would have cost minutes and attention. The aggregate across a day is substantial and across a career is difficult to estimate.

What she experienced instead was a working day, which is the entire objective.


## Twelve capabilities and no transitions

The conventional way of assessing a tool is to examine its capabilities one at a time, which is tidy and measures the wrong thing. What determines whether something is good is how the capabilities behave in sequence, under the conditions of a real day, when somebody is thinking about their work rather than about the tool.

Two constraints govern that. The first is the interval: professional work happens in gaps between other things, and the gaps have shapes. Four minutes before somebody arrives. Fifteen minutes between commitments. Ninety seconds while a question is still open. A capability requiring more time than its gap allows is one that exists and is not used, which is indistinguishable from absence.

The second is switching. Every transition between contexts interrupts whatever state somebody had established, which means a workflow spanning four applications is not four times slower than one spanning a single application but qualitatively different, since part of the person's attention goes to the mechanics of moving rather than to the work.

The day described here contains something like twenty distinct capabilities and no transitions. A preview of the coming days, a record of what was touched yesterday, a deadline indication, and a working set, all encountered in the first four minutes without any of them having been sought. Protection entered once and then absent for the working period. Consistent sections making a five-minute review a lookup rather than a reading exercise. A search reaching inside an attached assessment document, returning a passage in six seconds where opening the document would have taken four minutes.

During the session: brief fragments rather than sustained typing, a resolved question retrieved while somebody was still speaking, an instant screen blanking when somebody knocked, and a photographed letter attached to the section for that date rather than to the record generally. After it: a new section, headings separating report from observation, emphasis placed for a reader who will be herself in eight days, a decision set apart visually because a decision buried in prose is recorded rather than captured, a date attached so the record surfaces when it is needed, and a search across four hundred records to establish whether an impression was a pattern or a resemblance.

Between patients: reading interrupted at the moment an idea took hold rather than finished first, because a reaction has a life of about four minutes and an article completed produces a note about the article. And a figure showing twelve hundred documented sessions, which is the only complete account of her professional output in existence and which she did not construct deliberately.

The most valuable eleven minutes of the day involved none of the day's patients. Reading six consecutive revisions of one treatment plan showed a direction of travel invisible from inside, since each individual revision felt like a small sensible adjustment. She could see herself moving steadily toward something more conservative, across a period when the patient had not become more fragile, and she could not say why. That is a clinical finding produced by a record of her own reasoning rather than by any observation of a patient.

The reschedule took fifteen seconds while she was still listening to somebody on a telephone, against an estimated ninety seconds in her previous arrangement. The difference is not the seventy-five seconds. It is that the shorter version occurs while she is still attending to the person and the longer version does not.

What the day did not contain is as informative as what it did. No moving of material between places. No leaving one environment to consult another. No capability unavailable because of where she was. No reconciliation at the end. No moment of wondering whether her work was safe. Each absence corresponds to something an assembled arrangement would have required, and each would have cost minutes and attention.

She thought about her patients for eleven hours. She thought about the tool twice, and both occasions were thoughts about her work: once when a search told her that a suspected pattern was a coincidence, and once when her own record showed her a drift she had not noticed making. Neither was a thought about software.

That is the standard. Not whether each capability is impressive, but whether the person using them stops noticing that they are capabilities at all.
