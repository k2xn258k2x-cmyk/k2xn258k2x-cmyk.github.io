A small business generates more operational knowledge per person than any other kind of organisation, and almost none of it is recorded anywhere. This is not a failure of diligence. It is arithmetic. In a company of two hundred people, purchasing is a function, bookkeeping is a function, facilities is a function, and each of those functions has somebody whose job includes writing things down in a form that somebody else can use. In a business of one, all of those functions belong to the same person, who is also producing the actual product, and the writing down is the part that has no dedicated hour anywhere in the week.

The result is a specific and expensive kind of concentration. Everything that matters about a supplier relationship, a seasonal pattern, a piece of equipment, or a recurring dispute lives in one person's memory, unrecorded, and is retrieved by that person when needed and by nobody else ever. It works, in the sense that the business runs. It has two well-known failure modes: the person is unavailable, or the person is present and simply cannot recall a number from fourteen months ago that would have changed a negotiation.

![Running a Business on a Single HTML File](/assets/img/feature-screenshot-placeholder.svg)

What such a person needs from a tool is therefore unusual, and most business software is built for the opposite requirement. It must be powerful enough to hold a genuinely complex operation, since the complexity is real even when the headcount is one. And it must be light enough that maintaining it never becomes a task competing with the work, because in a business of this size any tool that requires tending will lose that competition within a month and will lose it permanently.
<!--more-->


Those two requirements pull against each other in almost every product built for the purpose. Systems capable of holding the complexity arrive with configuration, administration, integrations, and a permission model, all of which assume somebody exists whose job is to look after them. Systems light enough to maintain are typically too shallow to hold anything beyond a list. The gap between them is where most small operators live, and it is why so many of them run on a combination of memory, a notebook, a folder of files named by other people, and a spreadsheet that only they understand.

There is a further consideration that people in this position feel strongly and articulate rarely. A subscription is a permanent claim on money that has not been earned yet, and its terms can change in a message nobody reads. Anybody working on thin margins has an entirely rational objection to accumulating such claims, and the objection is sharper for the tool that holds the operational record, because that is the one whose loss would be genuinely serious rather than merely irritating.

Connectivity is the third thing, and it is more often decisive than anybody expects. A commercial building, a basement unit, a delivery bay, a rural site, or an ordinary shop with an unreliable line all produce periods where nothing reaches anything, and those periods are not evenly distributed across the working day. They cluster in exactly the hours when somebody is on their feet dealing with something, which is when the record most needs to be reachable.

Put those together and the requirement looks strange enough that it is worth stating plainly: something capable of holding several years of a complex operation, requiring no administration whatsoever, owned outright rather than rented, and working with no connection at all. That is a narrow specification and it happens to describe a single file.

What follows is a conversation between two people at opposite ends of scale who are running their businesses on exactly that, and who were introduced by somebody who thought the contrast would be instructive.


## Two businesses that have nothing in common

Raj had suggested it, which both of them mentioned separately in the first minute.

‘He sent me a message saying you and Marcus should talk and gave no reason whatsoever,’ Amir said.

‘He sent me the same message with the names reversed,’ said Marcus. ‘Which is his method and which has worked every time so far, so I have stopped asking.’

‘Then let us find out.’ Amir turned his laptop slightly. ‘I have one bakery. My father opened it. I have five people working for me, six from next month, and I bake bread and pastry and I have done for twenty-two years.’

‘Thirty-one properties, about a hundred and forty doors, across two cities and shortly a third.’ Marcus said it flatly. ‘Residential mostly, some commercial. No employees. A property manager who is not an employee and two contractors I use constantly.’

‘Those are not the same business.’

‘Those are not the same business by any measure I can think of,’ Marcus agreed, ‘which is presumably why he did it.’

‘How much is in yours?’

‘Around two thousand entries and a considerable number of attachments. I stopped counting the attachments.’ He paused. ‘Yours?’

‘Nine hundred and eleven, which I only learned recently and which I would have guessed at three hundred.’

Marcus laughed.

‘I did the same thing. I would have said twelve hundred.’

‘So we are both wrong about ourselves in the same direction.’

‘Everybody is, apparently.’ Marcus scrolled something. ‘Can I ask the question I actually had when Raj sent the message?’

‘Go.’

‘Does it behave differently at your size?’ He was direct. ‘Because I have watched things slow down at a few hundred records and I would like to know whether you experience something I do not.’

Amir considered.

‘I have no idea what you mean,’ he said. ‘It opens. I search. Things come back. There is a small pause the first time I search after opening it and then nothing.’

‘That is exactly my experience at two thousand.’

‘Then the answer to your question is no.’

‘The answer is no and it is more interesting than it sounds,’ Marcus said. ‘Because I have paid for two products that were sold to me on the basis of scaling, and both of them became unpleasant somewhere between five hundred and a thousand records, and the reason was always infrastructure I could not see.’

‘And this?’

‘Is a file that opens. There is nothing to scale.’ He shrugged. ‘I have no idea whether that is impressive or whether I have simply not found the edge yet, and after two years I have stopped looking for it.’


## What the money looks like in each of them

‘Start with finance,’ Marcus said, ‘because that is where I would expect us to diverge most and I want to be proved wrong.’

‘You will be proved right. Mine is extremely simple.’

‘Show me anyway.’

Amir shared. A supplier page, entries beneath it, and a series of photographs and documents attached where they belonged.

‘Receipts and invoices go on the supplier entry, in the section for whatever they are,’ he said. ‘My daily entries carry a rough revenue figure and a note about what sold. Anything with money in it carries a label so I can gather them.’

‘That is not simple, that is minimal,’ Marcus said. ‘Which is different and better.’

‘Say what you mean.’

‘Simple would mean you had left things out. Minimal means you have exactly what you need and nothing else.’ He was definite. ‘I have watched people build accounting apparatus inside a notes tool and then abandon it. You have a label and a habit.’

‘My accountant does the accounting.’

‘Mine does too and that is the point neither of us should lose,’ Marcus said. ‘This is not the accounts. It is everything around the accounts, which is the part nobody else can reconstruct.’

He shared his own, which was substantially larger.

‘Rent rolls arrive monthly as workbooks and attach to the property entry.’ He scrolled. ‘Invoices attach to the maintenance entry they belong to. Expense summaries come in as tabular exports.’

‘And you read them?’

‘I read them through the analyser, which takes a tabular file and draws its shape.’ He demonstrated. ‘Which is how I found that one building collects consistently four days later than everything else, every month, for two years.’

‘What did that turn out to be?’

‘A payday alignment across most of the tenants in that building, which is a fact about who lives there and which nobody would ever have told me.’ He shrugged. ‘Not a problem, once I knew. A cash flow assumption I had been getting wrong for two years.’

‘And that came from a chart.’

‘That came from a chart drawn from a file I already had and had never looked at as a shape.’

Amir asked about the annual work.

‘Tax preparation,’ Marcus said. ‘Which used to be four days and is now about six hours, and the reason is filters rather than anything clever.’

‘Explain.’

‘Everything financial carries a label. At the end of the year I filter to that label across the whole portfolio, narrow to a date range, and I have every financial entry from thirty-one properties in one view.’ He counted. ‘Which I then work through, and the attachments are already sitting on the entries they belong to.’

‘And before?’

‘Before, I went building by building through a folder structure, and the invoices were named by whoever produced them, and I lost things.’ He was blunt. ‘Not hypothetically. I have almost certainly failed to claim expenses in past years and I have no way of finding out how much.’

Amir was quiet for a second.

‘Mine is smaller and I have the same thing,’ he said. ‘Before this, my accountant would ask about something from March and I would say I would look for it, and looking meant a box.’

‘And now?’

‘Now it is four seconds, because I photographed the thing and put it on the supplier, and I can find a supplier faster than I can find a box.’ He laughed. ‘Which is not sophisticated and has changed that conversation entirely. She used to send me a list of questions in February. This year she sent me two.’


## The people you buy from

‘Suppliers,’ Amir said. ‘Which is the one part of my vault where I have an actual convention, and I would like to know whether yours looks the same.’

‘Contractors, for me, and I suspect it does.’

Amir opened one.

‘Eleven suppliers. One entry each. Sections for contact and terms, pricing history, delivery arrangements, and anything that has gone wrong.’ He scrolled. ‘Invoices attached to whichever section they concern.’

‘That last section is the one I want to hear about.’

‘Things that have gone wrong is the most useful thing in my entire vault and it is about four hundred words per supplier,’ Amir said. ‘Late deliveries. A quality problem. The time somebody sent semolina that was not what I ordered and argued about it for a fortnight.’

‘Why does it matter so much?’

‘Because I am a pleasant person and I forget grievances, which is good for my life and bad for my business.’ He said it plainly. ‘Twenty-two years of being nice to people means my sense of a supplier is whatever the last conversation was like. The section is the actual record.’

Marcus made a noise of recognition.

‘Mine is called history and it does the same job,’ he said. ‘Every contractor has one. What they bid, what they charged, whether the two matched, whether they finished when they said, and what I had to chase.’

‘Do you read it before a call?’

‘I read it before every call and it takes ninety seconds.’ He was definite. ‘Which is the whole return on the entire arrangement, as far as I am concerned.’

‘Say what it changes.’

‘I called a contractor last month about a roof.’ Marcus described it. ‘He quoted a number. And I had, open in front of me, three previous jobs with the same firm, including one where the final invoice was nineteen percent above the bid and the reason given was access.’

‘Which he had not mentioned.’

‘Which he had not mentioned, and which I raised, in a completely friendly way, before agreeing anything.’ He shrugged. ‘And the conversation we had about access at the beginning is a conversation I would otherwise have had at the end, in a worse mood, with less leverage.’

‘That is exactly my pricing thing,’ Amir said.

‘Say yours.’

‘A supplier calls in the autumn and tells me flour is going up.’ He was already animated. ‘And that is a conversation where I have historically had nothing at all, because I know what I pay now and I have no idea what I paid a year ago beyond a feeling.’

‘And now?’

‘Now I search his name and I have four years of prices, because they are in the pricing section on his entry, written down at the time.’ He counted. ‘And I can say that this is the third increase in eighteen months and that the second one was explained by something that has since resolved.’

‘Does it work?’

‘It worked twice and it did not work once, which is a considerably better record than I had.’ Amir laughed. ‘And the part that matters is not the money. It is that he now knows I keep records, and the conversation is different from the beginning.’

‘That is the most underrated effect,’ Marcus said. ‘Anybody who has been asked one specific question about a job from three years ago behaves differently for the rest of the relationship.’

They compared retrieval, which turned out to be identical.

‘Search rather than navigation,’ Amir said. ‘Even though I know exactly where the entry is.’

‘Same, and it took me a year to stop navigating.’ Marcus nodded. ‘Because when the telephone is ringing I do not want to open a page and then find an entry. I want to type a name and press a key.’

‘And that works even when I get the name slightly wrong,’ Amir said, ‘which I do, because two of my suppliers have similar names and I have never been certain which is which.’


## Writing down the day

‘Now the thing I did not expect to be talking about,’ Amir said, ‘which is four lines at the end of a day.’

‘Your daily entries.’

‘Yasmin made me start them and I complained for about a year.’ He was cheerful about it. ‘Four lines. What sold, what did not, anything anybody said, anything unusual. Ninety seconds.’

‘And it is now a fifth of your vault.’

‘It is now nearly two years of days and I read a year of them in one sitting recently, which changed something significant.’ He did not elaborate and Marcus did not push. ‘But the part that is relevant here is the searching.’

‘Go on.’

‘I searched cardamom.’ He said it as though it were obvious. ‘Every day I had written about cardamom, in one list, going back two years.’

‘What did you find?’

‘That it sells on Fridays.’ Amir spread his hands. ‘Not slightly. Consistently. Which I did not know, and which nobody could have told me, and which is now a production decision.’

‘You bake more on Thursday nights.’

‘I bake more on Thursday nights and I stopped baking it on Tuesdays entirely, and both of those came from typing one word.’

Marcus was nodding slowly.

‘Mine is the same at a different rhythm,’ he said. ‘Every property has an operational log. Maintenance, tenant contact, anything from an inspection that did not need action but was worth noting.’

‘How often?’

‘Not daily. Whenever something happens, which for any individual property is perhaps twice a month.’ He scrolled. ‘Which across thirty-one properties is a couple of entries a day and does not feel like a practice at all.’

‘And you search it.’

‘I search it constantly and I want to give the example that convinced me.’ Marcus pulled something up. ‘I searched a manufacturer's name because I was considering standardising on their equipment across the portfolio.’

‘And?’

‘Eleven results across seven buildings, and four of them were the same fault.’ He was flat about it. ‘Which nobody had noticed, because each one was handled by a different contractor at a different time in a different city, and each of them was an isolated repair.’

‘And together?’

‘Together they are a pattern that cost me somewhere around four thousand dollars over three years and would have cost considerably more if I had standardised.’ He shrugged. ‘I did not standardise. That decision was made by a search box.’

‘That is a considerably more expensive version of my cardamom,’ Amir said.

‘It is the identical mechanism at a different scale, which I think is the whole point of this call.’ Marcus was definite. ‘You wrote four lines a day about pastry and I wrote a paragraph whenever a boiler broke, and both of us built something that answered a question we did not know we would have.’


## Why the shape of the thing matters commercially

‘I want to talk about the file,’ Marcus said, ‘because I have a business case for it and I suspect yours is more emotional and I would like both on the record.’

‘Mine is both and I will give you the money part first, since you are expecting the other one.’

‘Go.’

‘Every subscription is a claim on money I have not earned.’ Amir said it precisely. ‘Which sounds obvious and is not, because most people think about the monthly figure and I think about the commitment.’

‘Say the difference.’

‘A monthly figure is small. A commitment is a thing I have to keep paying during February, when this shop takes about forty percent of what it takes in December.’ He shrugged. ‘My costs are annual and my revenue is seasonal, and every fixed monthly charge is a small tax on my worst months.’

‘That is a cash flow argument and it is exactly right,’ Marcus said. ‘Mine is different and I would put it as concentration of risk.’

‘Explain.’

‘I have counted the services my business genuinely depends on. Not conveniences. Things that would stop me operating.’ He was methodical. ‘There were fourteen, two years ago. Every one of them can change its terms, raise its price, be acquired, or be discontinued, and I would find out in an email.’

‘And that has happened.’

‘That has happened three times in eleven years, and once it was serious.’ He did not elaborate on which. ‘So when I evaluate anything now, I ask what happens to me if the company behind it makes a decision I dislike. And for most things the answer is that I move, with some inconvenience.’

‘And for the operational record?’

‘For the operational record the answer is that I lose eleven years of context that exists nowhere else, and no export will preserve it properly, because exports preserve documents and destroy relationships between them.’ Marcus was blunt. ‘That is not a risk I am willing to hold, and it is the only category where I feel that strongly.’

Amir gave his other argument, which he said was the one people found sentimental.

‘My father owned his tools,’ he said. ‘The proving cabinet is older than I am. The scales were his father's and they are wrong by four grams and I correct for it.’

‘And the difference is ownership.’

‘The difference is that nothing in that room was rented, and the room has outlasted two recessions and a fire.’ He shrugged. ‘I am aware that a file on a laptop is not a proving cabinet. And the relationship is the same relationship, and I have found that it changes what I am willing to put in.’

‘Say more about that, because it is the part I would not have predicted.’

‘There are things in my vault I would not put into somebody else's system.’ Amir was matter of fact. ‘Not scandalous things. Honest assessments of people I work with. What I actually think about a supplier. What went wrong and whose fault it was, including when it was mine.’

‘Which is the material that matters most.’

‘Which is the material that is worth anything at all, and I would write a diplomatic version of it if I thought anybody else could ever reach it.’

They moved to connectivity, which produced immediate agreement.

‘My line goes down about twice a month,’ Amir said. ‘Always during a morning. Never at four in the afternoon when nothing is happening.’

‘Mine is worse and it is structural.’ Marcus described it. ‘I inspect basements, mechanical rooms, and units in buildings with concrete floors. There is no signal in any of those places and there never will be.’

‘And that is where the work is.’

‘That is where the entire inspection happens, and anything I cannot reach in a mechanical room is a thing I will write up afterward from memory, badly, if at all.’ He was definite. ‘I have done that. The write-ups are worse. I can tell which ones they are.’

‘So the offline part is not a feature for either of us.’

‘The offline part is the condition under which my actual work happens,’ Marcus said, ‘and anything describing it as a mode has misunderstood my job.’

Amir asked the practical question.

‘What do you do about backups? Because that is the part that would worry me if I thought about it, and I mostly do not.’

‘Then you should think about it and it takes four minutes to arrange.’ Marcus was brisk. ‘The whole thing is a folder. I copy it to an external drive weekly and to a second location monthly, and that is the entire procedure.’

‘No service.’

‘No service, no account, no restore process, and no possibility of a backup that turns out to be incomplete because something was synchronising.’ He shrugged. ‘I have tested it once, which everybody should do and almost nobody does. I copied the folder to a different machine, opened the file, and it was my business.’

Amir said nothing for a second.

‘I am going to do that this week,’ he said. ‘The testing part. I have the copies and I have never opened one.’

‘Then you have a hope rather than a backup,’ Marcus said, ‘and the difference takes ten minutes to discover.’


## What each of them got wrong first

‘I want to ask about mistakes,’ Amir said, ‘because you have described a system that sounds designed and mine was not designed at all, and I would like to know which of us is lying.’

‘Neither of us,’ said Marcus, ‘and mine took two full rebuilds.’

‘Describe them.’

‘The first version was organised by year.’ He said it with the flatness of a man who has explained a bad idea before. ‘Every property had a page per year underneath it, because that is how my accounts work and it seemed obvious.’

‘And?’

‘And nothing about a building respects a calendar.’ He spread his hands. ‘A tenancy runs across three of them. A dispute runs across two. A boiler installed in one year fails in another, and the record of the installation and the record of the failure were in different branches, and I had to know the dates to find either.’

‘So you had to remember the thing you were looking it up to find out.’

‘Which is the definition of a broken arrangement, and it took me about eight months to say that sentence out loud.’ Marcus shrugged. ‘Now the structure is by property and the years are just dates on entries, which is where dates belong.’

‘And the second rebuild?’

‘Separate entries per document type, which I have described elsewhere and will not bore you with.’ He waved it off. ‘Both mistakes have the same shape, which I did not see at the time. I was organising by a property of the paperwork rather than by the thing the paperwork is about.’

Amir was thinking.

‘Mine is not a rebuild because I never built anything,’ he said. ‘My mistake is the opposite and it cost me about a year.’

‘Go on.’

‘I did not write anything down that felt like it was going well.’ He said it plainly. ‘Only problems. My first six months are almost entirely complaints, because a problem feels like a thing that needs recording and a good day feels like a normal day.’

‘And the consequence?’

‘The consequence is that I have six months of records that describe a bakery I would not want to work in.’ He laughed without much humour. ‘And when I read them back recently it was genuinely upsetting, because it is an inaccurate picture of a period I remember as fine.’

‘That is a bias I have never considered.’

‘It is a bias every one of us has and nobody mentions, because you only find it when you read a year of your own writing.’ Amir was definite. ‘Now I write the good ones deliberately. What worked. What sold out. When somebody said something kind. Not because I need a morale exercise, but because a record of only failures is not a record of a business.’

‘Does it change the usefulness?’

‘It changes it completely,’ he said. ‘Because now when I search a product I get the failures and the successes, and I can see which conditions produced which. Before, I could only ever find out what had gone wrong with something, which is half of an answer and reads like a whole one.’

Marcus was quiet for a second.

‘I am going to check mine for that,’ he said. ‘I suspect my property logs are ninety percent things that broke.’

‘They will be,’ said Amir. ‘Mine were, and nobody told me, and I would have gone on that way indefinitely.’


## The thing Marcus had that Amir did not

‘One more and it is a feature you have never mentioned,’ Marcus said.

‘Then I have never used it.’

‘Open three things at once.’ He shared. ‘Separate tabs, each holding its own view, each keeping whatever filter or page it is on.’

Amir watched.

‘What are those?’

‘Three properties. Same filter applied to each, showing maintenance costs for the year.’ Marcus moved between them. ‘Which is how I do a comparison, because the alternative is opening one, reading it, closing it, opening the next, and holding the first in my head.’

‘And you can move between them without losing anything.’

‘Without losing anything, which is what makes it a comparison rather than a sequence of readings.’ He shrugged. ‘I use it for perhaps ninety minutes a quarter and it is the difference between a review I do and a review I intend to do.’

‘I have no idea what I would use that for,’ Amir said honestly.

‘Then let me suggest one and you can tell me whether it is nonsense.’ Marcus thought. ‘Open your recipes page in one and your suppliers page in another.’

‘Why?’

‘Because when you are costing a recipe, you are looking at ingredients in one place and prices in another, and I would guess you currently do that by switching.’

There was a pause.

‘I do that by switching and it is genuinely irritating and I have never once thought of it as a problem,’ Amir said.

‘Try it now.’

He did, and there was another pause, longer.

‘Ah,’ he said.

‘Say what you are looking at.’

‘I am looking at the ingredient list for something I have been thinking about putting up in price, and I am looking at what I actually pay for three of those ingredients, at the same time.’ He was moving between them. ‘Which is a calculation I have done in my head for twenty-two years.’

‘And in your head it is approximate.’

‘In my head it is approximate and it is optimistic, because I remember the price I want to be paying.’ He laughed without much humour. ‘This is going to change a number and I suspect not in a direction I will enjoy.’

‘That is the general experience,’ Marcus said. ‘Every comparison I have ever set up properly has told me something slightly worse than I believed and something better than I feared.’


## Handing it to somebody who is not you

‘There is a question I want to ask that is not about features,’ Marcus said, ‘and I have been working out how to put it.’

‘Ask it badly, then.’

‘What happens to your business if you are not there for a month?’

Amir did not answer immediately.

‘That is not a badly put question,’ he said. ‘That is the question.’

‘I ask because I have been thinking about my own version and I do not like the answer.’

‘Say yours first.’

‘Thirty-one properties, no employees, and a property manager who handles tenants and knows nothing about anything else.’ Marcus was matter of fact. ‘If I am unavailable for a month, the rent still arrives, because that is automated. Everything else stops. Nobody knows which contractor to call for which building, what any of the disputes are about, or which certificates are about to lapse.’

‘And with the vault?’

‘With the vault, somebody competent could work it out, and that is a completely different answer from the one I would have given three years ago.’ He paused. ‘Every property has the same five sections. Every contractor has a history. The certificates have dates on them and appear in a time view. A stranger with the password would need a week and would not need me.’

‘Have you told anybody the password?’

‘My sister has an envelope, which was an uncomfortable afternoon and which I would recommend to everybody.’ He shrugged. ‘She does not know what is in it and she knows what to do with it.’

Amir was quiet.

‘Mine is worse than yours,’ he said. ‘And it has been improving and it is still worse.’

‘Say it.’

‘Everything about how this bakery actually runs is in my hands.’ He held them up briefly. ‘Not metaphorically. The temperature of the room, the feel of the dough, how long something needs when the weather is like this. None of that is written down and none of it can be.’

‘But that is not the whole business.’

‘That is not the whole business and that is the thing I have only recently understood.’ He leaned forward. ‘The recipes are written. The suppliers are written, with the prices and the history. The daily record is two years long. Who is trained on what is now written, since about a fortnight ago, because somebody pointed out that it was not.’

‘So the part that can be handed over has been.’

‘The part that can be handed over is now written down, and the part that cannot is the part my father could not hand to me either.’ He shrugged. ‘I learned it by standing next to him for four years. Somebody would have to do the same. But they would not have to work out which flour I buy or what happened with the semolina, and that is most of what would actually go wrong.’

‘That is a better answer than mine,’ Marcus said.

‘It is the same answer arrived at from a trade rather than a portfolio,’ Amir said. ‘And I did not build it on purpose. I built it four lines at a time at seven in the evening because my daughter would not stop asking.’


## Two people who do not think of themselves the same way

Near the end, Marcus said something that changed the register of the call.

‘Can I say the thing that has been sitting underneath this for an hour?’

‘Please.’

‘I have spent most of this conversation slightly envious of you,’ he said.

Amir laughed properly.

‘I have four ovens and a hundred and forty doors of debt is what you have.’

‘I have thirty-one properties and I could not tell you what any of them smells like.’ Marcus said it without self-pity. ‘Your business makes a thing. Mine collects a payment for the use of a thing that somebody else made.’

‘That is a very hard way to describe your own work.’

‘It is an accurate way and I made peace with it about a decade ago.’ He shrugged. ‘But I notice that everything in your vault is about how to make something better, and everything in mine is about what has broken. Which may be a fact about our businesses and may be a fact about how we write.’

‘It is both and I want to say the other side,’ Amir said. ‘Because I have spent the same hour being slightly ashamed.’

‘Of what?’

‘Of the fact that you have four years of contractor prices and a filter that produces your year in six hours, and I have photographs of receipts and a label.’ He was frank. ‘You run something considerably more complicated than I do and you run it with more discipline than I have ever applied to anything except dough.’

‘Your discipline is in the dough.’

‘My discipline is in the dough and it does not transfer, which I have known for twenty-two years and have never said to anybody.’ He paused. ‘My father was the same. He could tell you what the room was doing and he could not tell you what he paid for anything.’

Marcus considered that.

‘Then here is what I actually think, having listened to all of it,’ he said. ‘You have built exactly as much system as your business needs and not one thing more, and that is not a failure of discipline. That is judgment.’

‘You are being kind.’

‘I am being precise, which is what I do instead of being kind.’ He was matter of fact. ‘I have watched people with businesses your size build something the size of mine and abandon it in four months, because the system became the second job. You have nine hundred entries, six labels, four pages, and a habit. That is a working system and it took no maintenance to get there.’

‘And yours takes maintenance.’

‘Mine takes about two hours a month and it has to, because a hundred and forty doors will not hold themselves in anybody's head.’ He shrugged. ‘The right amount of system is the amount your business cannot run without. Yours is small. Mine is not. Neither of us is doing it wrong.’

Amir did not say anything for a moment.

‘I am going to remember that,’ he said. ‘Because I have felt behind on this for about eighteen months and I have just realised that I have been comparing myself to people whose businesses require something mine does not.’


## The right things at the right level

Two businesses with nothing in common, running on the same foundation without either of them having configured anything, is the observation worth extracting from that conversation. One is a single bakery with about nine hundred records; the other is thirty-one properties with roughly two thousand. Neither has adjusted anything to accommodate their scale, neither has encountered a point where the arrangement changed character, and both described the same brief pause on a first search and nothing else.

That is not a claim about capacity so much as a consequence of there being no infrastructure to outgrow. Both had previously abandoned products sold on the promise of scaling, in each case because something invisible underneath became unpleasant somewhere in the low hundreds of records. A file that opens has nothing to scale, which removes an entire category of problem rather than solving it.

What they use is nearly identical and applied at different intensities. Documents and photographs attached to the record they concern rather than to a folder. A label carried by anything financial, which converts an annual reconstruction into a filtered view. Supplier and contractor records with a history section that exists specifically because both men are pleasant and therefore forget grievances, and because a sense of a relationship built from the most recent conversation is not a record. Operational logging at whatever rhythm the business produces events, which for one is four lines every evening and for the other a paragraph whenever something breaks.

The retrieval stories are the same story at two scales. One typed the name of an ingredient and discovered a weekly pattern nobody could have told him, which changed production. The other typed the name of a manufacturer and found the same fault four times across seven buildings, each handled by a different contractor in a different city, each an isolated repair until they appeared together. Neither pattern existed in any individual record. Both were produced by consistent writing and a search box, and both changed a decision worth real money.

The architectural arguments they gave were commercial rather than ideological, which is worth noting because these arguments are usually made by people with a philosophical interest in them. One described every subscription as a claim on money not yet earned, falling hardest in the months when a seasonal business can least afford it. ## FAQs

### Why do small businesses generate so much unrecorded knowledge?

Because every function belongs to the same person. In a large organisation, purchasing, bookkeeping, and facilities each have somebody whose job includes writing things down for others to use. In a business of one or two, all of that belongs to whoever is also producing the actual product, and the recording has no dedicated hour anywhere in the week. The result is that everything about suppliers, seasons, equipment, and disputes lives in one memory, retrieved by that person and by nobody else. The failure modes are well known and both expensive: the person is unavailable, or the person is present and cannot recall a figure from fourteen months ago that would have changed a negotiation.

### What would each of these practitioners tell somebody starting?

Start with the records that would be expensive to lose, which is usually suppliers, clients, or assets. Add a history section to each, because the record of what has gone wrong is the part nobody can reconstruct. Establish one label for anything financial before creating many entries. Write something short at the end of each day or after each event, including the good ones. And take a copy of the folder somewhere else, then open it once to confirm that it works. The benefit accumulates rather than arriving at once, which is why the practice is difficult to justify in advance and obvious after a year.


### Why is it worth talking to somebody running a completely different business?

Because the differences expose assumptions that peers share and never question. A conversation between a single-site food business and a multi-property portfolio produced changes in both directions: one adopted a comparison technique he had never considered, the other resolved to check whether his records contained only failures. Neither observation would have arisen between two people in the same trade, since both would have taken the same things for granted. Seeking such conversations out deliberately is worth the effort for anybody running a small operation alone, since the alternative is a peer group that shares every one of your blind spots. The exchange also tends to be genuinely mutual rather than one party instructing the other.

### What do very different businesses turn out to have in common?

The shape of the problem rather than any of its content. Both need a record of relationships with the people they buy from, both benefit from writing down what happens at whatever rate it happens, both find patterns only in aggregate, both work in places without connectivity, and both eventually face the question of what happens if they are unavailable. The vocabulary differs completely and the requirements are close to identical. That convergence is the most informative thing available to somebody deciding whether an approach will suit them, since it suggests the requirements follow from the situation of running one's own operation rather than from any particular trade. A baker and a property investor are about as far apart as two businesses get.

### Does scale change what the tool needs to do?

It changes how much structure is worth building and not what the structure is for. A larger operation needs more levels of hierarchy, more consistent templates, and a few hours of upkeep a month, because a hundred and forty units cannot be held in anybody's memory. A smaller one needs a handful of pages and a habit. What neither needs is configuration, administration, or anything that would have to be maintained for its own sake. Anybody comparing themselves unfavourably to a larger operation's system should note that the larger system exists because the larger business would otherwise fail, not because its owner is more disciplined. The right amount of structure is the amount your own work cannot proceed without.


### What does the operational record eventually become worth?

More than its owner expects, and in a form that is hard to describe until it is needed. Practitioners describe it as the difference between a business that runs and a business that can be explained: to an accountant, to a buyer, to a successor, to a lawyer, or to somebody covering for a month. That capability is invisible while everything is normal and is the entire value of the exercise on the day it stops being normal. Practitioners describe the moment of realisation arriving with an external question they could suddenly answer: a claim, an audit, a valuation, or somebody asking about a decision made three years earlier. Until then the record simply accumulates and feels like a modest habit rather than an asset.

### What is the single sentence to take from this?

That the complexity belongs in the business rather than in the tool holding it. A record that requires configuration, administration, a connection, or a subscription has added its own problems to an operation that already has enough, and small operators reliably abandon such things within months. A folder of files that opens, works anywhere, and belongs to the business asks nothing in return, which is why it survives. A baker with nine hundred entries and an investor with two thousand arrived at that conclusion from opposite ends of scale and neither had set out to make an argument about software. Both were simply describing what had survived contact with their actual working lives.


### Where should somebody begin if they run a business and record almost nothing?

With the single relationship or asset that would be most expensive to lose context about, and nothing else. Create one record, give it a history section, and write down what has actually happened with that supplier, client, or property, from memory, in whatever order it comes. That takes about half an hour and produces something immediately useful. Everything else follows from noticing that the exercise was worth doing, which is a considerably better motivator than any plan to be organised. Practitioners consistently identify the size of the commitment, rather than any amount of resolve, as what determines whether a recording habit survives its first difficult month. Ninety seconds survives; twenty minutes does not.

### How does somebody keep the habit going through a busy period?

By having made it small enough that a busy period does not displace it. A few lines at a fixed point in the day survives almost anything; a structured weekly review does not. Practitioners describe their logs continuing through genuinely difficult stretches precisely because the commitment was ninety seconds, and note that the entries written during those periods are frequently the ones that turn out to matter most when the pattern is eventually read back. The general principle is that a partial record is worth vastly more than an abandoned one, and any rule that makes resuming feel like a debt is a rule that will eventually end the practice entirely.

### What should somebody do when they realise they have been recording nothing for weeks?

Resume without attempting to reconstruct the gap. The instinct to fill in the missing period produces a fortnight of writing that nobody has the appetite for, which reliably ends the practice permanently. Practitioners describe simply starting again on the current day and treating the gap as an ordinary feature of a working life. A record with holes in it is worth enormously more than a record that was abandoned because catching up seemed obligatory. Writing it within minutes rather than at the end of the day is what preserves the specifics, since the wording of an agreement fades considerably faster than the impression that one was reached.

### Is there anything worth recording that most business owners overlook?

Verbal agreements, which are simultaneously the most consequential and the least recorded category. What a supplier said on the telephone about next season, what a contractor agreed about access, what a tenant was told about a repair, and what an employee was promised about hours all have real consequences and exist nowhere. Practitioners describe writing these down within minutes of the conversation as the single highest-return habit they adopted, and the one they had never considered. Anybody weighing this up should note that the cost is measured in seconds and the return arrives at an unpredictable moment, sometimes years later, in a form nobody could have anticipated.
