Most thinking about protecting information assumes an adversary who wants it. Somebody who has decided to obtain material and is prepared to make an effort, whether by defeating a control, obtaining a device, or arriving with a legal instrument. That model produces the whole familiar apparatus of passwords, encryption, and access controls, and it is correct for the situations it describes.

It is entirely silent about the situation that actually occurs in professional life several times a day. Nobody is trying to obtain anything. A person walks into a room, stands at a desk, sits down opposite somebody, or passes behind a chair, and their eyes land on a screen because eyes land on screens. What they see was not sought and cannot be unseen, and in many professions the fact of having seen it is a genuine problem regardless of what anybody intended.

![The Lock Screen as a Professional Habit](/assets/img/feature-screenshot-placeholder.svg)

The consequences of that accidental exposure vary enormously and are rarely trivial. A patient glimpsing another patient's record has learned something about a third party's health. A congregation member seeing a name in a pastoral file has learned that somebody is in difficulty. A client employee reading a security finding has learned about a weakness in their own organisation that they have no authorisation to know about.
<!--more-->


What makes this hard to address is that the exposure occurs in ordinary circumstances, in seconds, with nobody at fault. Standard controls do not engage. A locked device is not locked while somebody is working. Encryption protects material at rest and the material in question is open on a screen because somebody is using it. Access control assumes a person requesting access, and nobody requested anything: they walked in, which is what people do.

The gap is closed by something so simple that it is routinely dismissed. An action that immediately obscures whatever is displayed, blocking both the view and any interaction with it, and which reverses just as immediately when the situation passes. There is nothing sophisticated in the mechanism, which is why it gets underestimated: people evaluating a system look for cryptography and architecture, and a screen that goes opaque does not register as a security measure at all.

Judged against the failure it prevents, however, it is among the most effective controls available, because the exposure it addresses is by far the most frequent. Most professionals will never have a device stolen or receive a legal demand. Most will have somebody walk into their office during a working day, and most will have that happen several times, and the aggregate risk of thousands of such moments across a career is considerably larger than the risk of any single dramatic event.

There is a second effect that people discover only after adopting it, and several describe it as the more valuable one. Somebody who knows their screen can be made blank in a moment stops managing their environment continuously. They stop angling the display, stop tracking footsteps, and stop writing more cautiously than the record deserves because somebody might glance over. The control does not merely prevent an exposure; it removes a background vigilance that was consuming attention nobody had accounted for.

What follows is a conversation between three people whose working environments make this constant, and who use the same single action for three quite different reasons.


## Three rooms, one gesture

Meera had convened it, which she said afterward was unusual for her.

‘I want to talk about something small,’ she said, ‘and I have been slightly embarrassed about proposing it, because it is one button.’

‘Then it is probably worth an hour,’ said Suki. ‘The things people are embarrassed to raise are usually the ones nobody has examined.’

David joined a moment later, apologising for a delay involving a boiler.

‘Say the subject,’ he said.

‘Locking the screen.’

‘Ah.’ He laughed. ‘I use that more than any other single thing and I would not have thought to discuss it.’

‘Which is exactly why I raised it,’ Meera said. ‘I use it perhaps thirty times a day and I have never once talked about it with anybody.’

‘Thirty,’ said Suki.

‘Thirty, and I want to describe when, because I suspect our whens are completely different and the reasons are completely different and the gesture is identical.’


## Between one person and the next

‘Mine is a ritual and I want to describe it precisely,’ Meera said.

‘Go.’

‘An appointment ends. The person stands. I stand.’ She was deliberate about the sequence. ‘And as I stand, my hand goes to the keyboard and the screen locks, and then I walk to the door.’

‘Before the person has left the room.’

‘Before the person has left the room, and that ordering is the whole of it.’ Meera was definite. ‘The moment somebody stands, their eye level changes and their attention comes off me and goes to the room, which includes my desk.’

‘Which is not suspicion of them.’

‘It is not suspicion of anybody. It is a fact about how people behave when a conversation ends.’ She shrugged. ‘Everybody looks around a room when they stand up. I do it. You do it.’

‘Say what is on the screen.’

‘Their own record, which they are entitled to see and which is not the problem.’ Meera paused. ‘The problem is the next twenty seconds, during which I walk to a waiting area and return with somebody else, and my office is empty and my door is open.’

‘And somebody else comes in.’

‘Somebody else comes in and their eyes go to the desk, which is what eyes do, and what they can see is a name and a date and a heading.’ She was precise. ‘Which is enough. A person in this town seeing the name of a neighbour on my screen has learned something about that neighbour, and I have caused it.’

Suki asked the practical question.

‘How long did it take to become automatic?’

‘About a fortnight, and I made it automatic deliberately by attaching it to standing up rather than to a decision.’ Meera was emphatic. ‘Which is the only thing that works. If it is a decision, I will make it correctly ninety-five times and forget on the ninety-sixth, and the ninety-sixth is the one that matters.’

‘So the physical action carries it.’

‘The physical action carries it and I no longer experience it as an action at all.’ She thought about it. ‘I could not tell you whether I did it after my last appointment. I know that I did, because I always do, and I have no memory of it.’

‘That is what a habit is,’ David said.

‘That is what a habit is and it took two weeks to buy and it will last the rest of my career.’


## When somebody knocks

‘Mine is reactive rather than ritual,’ David said, ‘which I think is the difference between our rooms.’

‘Say the room.’

‘A church office with a door that people knock on and then open, because that is what a church office is for.’ He was matter of fact. ‘I could keep it closed and I will not, because a minister with a closed door is a minister nobody comes to.’

‘So the interruption is the point.’

‘The interruption is the entire job. Somebody putting their head round my door because something is wrong is the most important thing that happens in my week.’ David shrugged. ‘Which means I cannot make the room private. I can only make the screen private.’

‘And the sequence?’

‘A knock, and my hand moves before I have looked up.’ He described it. ‘Which took considerably longer than a fortnight to build, because a knock is not a movement I make, it is a sound somebody else produces.’

‘Harder to attach a habit to.’

‘Considerably harder, and what worked in the end was that I practised it.’ He was slightly embarrassed. ‘Alone, in the office, for about a week. I would tap the desk and then lock, tap and lock, until the sound produced the movement.’

Meera made an approving noise.

‘That is a better method than mine.’

‘It is a ridiculous method and it worked, and I have told nobody until now.’

Suki asked what he was protecting.

‘Pastoral notes,’ David said. ‘Which are the most sensitive documents I handle and which look like nothing. Not medical records. A name and a paragraph about a marriage, or a bereavement, or somebody's son.’

‘And the paragraph is not the exposure.’

‘The name is the exposure and the paragraph is not necessary.’ He was definite. ‘Somebody seeing a name on my screen learns that person is talking to me, and in a congregation of four hundred people that is a substantial disclosure on its own.’

‘Say the specific occasion,’ Meera said. ‘Because you mentioned there was one.’

David took a moment.

‘A deacon knocked and came in, and I locked as I always do, and we talked for about twenty minutes about a building matter.’ He said it evenly. ‘And what had been on the screen was a note about a member of his own family, who had come to me a fortnight earlier and asked me not to tell him.’

Nobody said anything.

‘I have thought about that afternoon more than any other,’ David said. ‘Because the habit was eleven months old by then and I did not decide anything. My hand moved and I have no memory of it moving.’

‘And if it had not.’

‘Then he would have seen a name he knew extremely well on a screen in his minister's office, and no explanation I could construct would have repaired it.’ He shrugged. ‘Not the content. The name. That is all it would have taken.’

‘That is the argument,’ Suki said quietly.

‘That one afternoon justified every previous use and every subsequent one, and I would have said the same if it had never happened, and I would not have believed it as firmly.’


## On somebody else's premises

‘Mine is contractual, which makes it the least interesting and the easiest to explain,’ Suki said.

‘Say the situation.’

‘I work on client premises during engagements. An office I do not control, at a desk somebody has found for me, in a room with their staff in it.’ She counted. ‘And on my screen at any moment there may be findings about weaknesses in their systems, diagrams of their network, and code that demonstrates a problem.’

‘Which the client has asked you to produce.’

‘Which the client has asked me to produce and which almost nobody at the client is authorised to see.’ Suki was precise. ‘That is the part people find surprising. My obligation is not primarily to protect the client from outsiders. It is to protect the engagement from the client's own staff.’

‘Say why.’

‘Because a finding about a weakness is a finding about a weakness.’ She shrugged. ‘Somebody who learns about it before it is fixed has information that is dangerous regardless of their intentions, and a substantial proportion of security incidents involve somebody inside the organisation.’

‘So the person walking past your desk.’

‘Is somebody who works there, is entirely legitimate, has no bad intent, and should not read what is on my screen.’ Suki was definite. ‘And my contracts say so explicitly, which means it is not a preference I hold, it is a term I have agreed to.’

David asked how she had built the habit.

‘Locking on movement,’ she said. ‘Any movement away from the keyboard, of any duration, for any reason.’

‘Including four seconds.’

‘Including standing to reach something on the far side of the desk.’ She was matter of fact. ‘Which sounds excessive and is not, because a rule with a duration threshold requires a judgment about duration and I am not going to make that judgment accurately while thinking about something else.’

‘So no threshold at all.’

‘No threshold, which makes it a reflex rather than a decision, and a reflex works when I am tired and a decision does not.’ Suki paused. ‘I lock my car when I walk away from it. I do not assess how long I will be gone. It is the same category of habit and it is easier to build for exactly that reason.’

‘Has it ever mattered?’

‘I do not know, which is the honest answer and which is true of every preventive control.’ She shrugged. ‘Nobody has ever told me they were about to read my screen. What I know is that in eleven years nobody has ever seen a finding before the client received it, and that is the only outcome I can measure.’

Meera raised something.

‘Do clients notice you doing it?’

‘Constantly, and it is professionally useful in a way I did not anticipate.’ Suki almost smiled. ‘Somebody watching me lock my screen to walk four feet has learned something about how I handle their material, and they have learned it by observation rather than from a sentence in a proposal.’

‘Which is more persuasive.’

‘Which is enormously more persuasive, and two clients have mentioned it in conversations about renewing.’ She was matter of fact. ‘Not because I performed it. Because a habit is visible and a claim is not.’


## Why it changes what you write

‘I want to raise something that is not about the exposure at all,’ Meera said. ‘Because it is the effect I actually value most and it took me a year to notice.’

‘Go on.’

‘I write more honestly.’

‘Say what you mean,’ said David.

‘A clinical note is a judgment, and judgments can be phrased carefully or plainly.’ Meera was choosing words. ‘And there is a version of every observation I make that is defensible, hedged, and considerably less useful than the plain version.’

‘And you used to write the hedged one.’

‘I used to write the hedged one about a third of the time, and I told myself it was professional caution.’ She was frank. ‘It was not. It was that a plain observation on a screen is a plain observation somebody might read, and I was writing for an imaginary reader who was not the patient and not me.’

‘And the lock removed the reader.’

‘The lock removed the reader, and my notes became a record of what I actually think, which is the only thing a clinical note is for.’ Meera shrugged. ‘Which is a considerable change produced by a button.’

David was nodding slowly.

‘Mine is different and adjacent,’ he said. ‘I stopped managing my room.’

‘Say what that means.’

‘Before, I had a set of small arrangements.’ He counted them. ‘The screen was angled away from the door. I sat at a particular side of the desk. I would close things when I heard anybody in the corridor, which meant I was listening to the corridor.’

‘While working.’

‘While counselling somebody, occasionally, which is the part I am least proud of.’ David was honest about it. ‘A portion of my attention was on the door, always, and I did not experience it as attention because it had been there for years.’

‘And when it went?’

‘I noticed about a month later that I had stopped noticing the corridor.’ He shrugged. ‘Which sounds trivial and was not, because that attention went back to the person in front of me.’

Suki said hers was the same effect in a different vocabulary.

‘Reduced load,’ she said. ‘Which is the term I would use with a client and which describes exactly what David has just described.’

‘Say it properly.’

‘Monitoring an environment is a continuous process that consumes capacity, and capacity spent on watching a room is capacity not spent on the work.’ She was precise. ‘On a client site I used to know where everybody was. Not deliberately. I simply knew, because part of me was tracking it.’

‘And now?’

‘And now I do not, because it does not matter where anybody is.’ Suki shrugged. ‘Which has made me measurably better at the actual work, and it is the benefit I would put first if I were persuading somebody.’

‘Not the exposure prevented.’

‘The exposure prevented is the reason. The attention recovered is the experience.’ She paused. ‘And people adopt things for the experience rather than for the reason, which is worth knowing when you are trying to change somebody's behaviour.’


## Two boundaries doing different jobs

‘I want to be precise about how this relates to protecting the material itself,’ Suki said, ‘because people conflate them and the conflation causes bad decisions.’

‘Say the distinction.’

‘One of them is about a person being physically present. The other is about the material at rest.’ She was methodical. ‘Locking the screen addresses somebody who is in the room while I am working. Encryption addresses somebody who has the machine while I am not.’

‘Which are entirely different adversaries,’ Meera said.

‘Entirely different, and neither substitutes for the other, and people regularly believe one covers both.’ Suki counted. ‘Somebody with strong encryption and no locking habit is protected against theft and exposed thirty times a day. Somebody who locks constantly and has protected nothing has an unlocked archive on a machine that will eventually be somewhere it should not be.’

‘Say your version,’ David said to Meera.

‘The lock is for the moment. The protection is for the record.’ She said it simply. ‘Which is how I have thought about it for two years and which I have never had to explain until now.’

‘That is better than mine,’ Suki said.

‘Yours is more precise and mine fits in a sentence, and both are correct.’

David said he had never articulated it at all.

‘I use both and I have never thought about them as separate things,’ he admitted. ‘I lock when somebody knocks and I protect the pastoral entries because they are pastoral entries, and I have never noticed that those are two different decisions about two different situations.’

‘Which is fine,’ Suki said. ‘You do not have to have a model to have the right behaviour.’

‘Say why the model helps anyway.’

‘Because it tells you what you are still exposed to.’ She was definite. ‘Somebody who understands the split can ask which situations they have covered. Somebody without it tends to do one thing well, feel protected, and have a gap they cannot see.’

They discussed the interaction between the two, which Meera had encountered and Suki explained.

‘There is a behaviour I want to check,’ Meera said. ‘When I lock and come back, sometimes I have to enter a password again for a protected entry and sometimes I do not.’

‘Which depends on how long you were away and how the caching is configured.’ Suki was precise. ‘A protected entry that has been opened is available for a period. If that period has elapsed while the screen was locked, opening it again asks.’

‘Which I find slightly annoying and I assume is correct.’

‘It is correct and I would keep it.’ Suki was definite. ‘The screen being locked and the material being decrypted are two separate states, and the second one expiring while the first is active is exactly what you want.’

‘Say why.’

‘Because the case you are protecting against is a locked screen that stays locked for two hours because you were called away unexpectedly.’ She counted. ‘If the decryption state persisted, then unlocking would restore access to everything without any further check, which converts a lock into the only barrier.’

‘Whereas.’

‘Whereas with an expiry, a long absence means unlocking gets you back to your workspace and the protected material asks again, which is the correct behaviour for exactly that situation.’

David asked something practical.

‘Should I stop using one of them?’

‘Absolutely not, and that is the point of the distinction.’ Suki was emphatic. ‘Somebody choosing between them has misunderstood the question. They cost nothing together and they cover different halves.’


## Making it automatic

‘Practical closing,’ David said. ‘Because somebody reading about this will agree and will not do it.’

‘Which is the general fate of any advice about habits,’ Meera said.

‘Then say what actually worked.’

‘Attach it to something that already happens.’ She was definite. ‘Not to an intention. To a physical event that occurs reliably whether or not you are thinking.’

‘Yours was standing.’

‘Standing, which happens at the end of every appointment without any decision from me.’ Meera shrugged. ‘I did not have to build a habit of locking. I had to attach locking to a movement I was already making.’

‘Mine was a sound and it was harder,’ David said. ‘Because the trigger came from outside rather than from my own body.’

‘And the practising.’

‘The practising is what solved it and I would recommend it to anybody in the same position.’ He was matter of fact. ‘A week of tapping the desk and locking, alone, in an empty office, until the sound produced the movement without going through my head.’

Suki gave hers.

‘Any movement away from the keyboard, with no threshold.’ She counted. ‘Which is the easiest version to build because it removes the judgment entirely. There is nothing to decide.’

‘How long for each of you?’

‘A fortnight,’ said Meera.

‘About six weeks,’ said David, ‘because of the external trigger.’

‘Three weeks and I had an advantage, which is that I already lock a car and a door and a machine, so it slotted into an existing category.’

‘Say the general shape,’ Meera said.

‘Between two and six weeks depending on how well the trigger fits, and then it becomes invisible.’ Suki was definite. ‘And invisible is the objective rather than reliable, because a reliable habit is one you are still performing and an invisible one is one that survives a bad day.’

David added the thing he said mattered most.

‘Do not attach it to the sensitivity of the material,’ he said.

‘Say why.’

‘Because that requires assessing the material, which requires thinking, which fails.’ He was emphatic. ‘I do not lock when the notes are sensitive. I lock when somebody knocks, regardless of what is on the screen, including when it is a grocery list.’

‘Which sounds excessive.’

‘Which sounds excessive and is the only version that works, because a habit with an exception is a habit with a gap and the gap arrives on the day you are least alert.’


## The three occasions it did not work

‘I want to ask about failures,’ Suki said, ‘because a habit with a hundred percent record is a habit nobody has examined.’

‘I have one and it still bothers me,’ Meera said.

‘Go on.’

‘An appointment ended early, unusually, and the person left while I was still sitting down.’ She described it carefully. ‘My trigger is standing. I had not stood. And the next person was already at my door because the timing had shifted.’

‘So the trigger did not fire.’

‘The trigger did not fire because the situation did not contain the trigger, which is the weakness of attaching a habit to one specific event.’ Meera was frank. ‘I locked it manually, about four seconds later, and I do not know whether anybody saw anything and I never will.’

‘What did you change?’

‘I added a second trigger, which is anybody appearing in my doorway.’ She shrugged. ‘Which is less reliable because it is external, and which covers the case where my own movement does not happen.’

‘Two triggers rather than one.’

‘Two triggers rather than one, and I would recommend it to anybody whose primary trigger is their own body, because bodies do not always do the thing.’

David had a different failure.

‘Mine is that I locked and then unlocked while somebody was still there,’ he said.

‘Say what happened.’

‘Somebody knocked, I locked, we talked, and then they asked me to look up a date.’ He was rueful. ‘So I unlocked, in front of them, and went looking for a calendar, and I had entirely forgotten what was underneath.’

‘Which was?’

‘Nothing sensitive, by luck rather than by anything I did.’ David shrugged. ‘And I now have a rule that I do not unlock while somebody is in the room. If I need to look something up, I ask them to give me a moment, or I do it afterward and telephone them.’

‘That is a considerable inconvenience.’

‘It is about four occasions a month and it is the correct trade, because the alternative is unlocking blind in front of a person and hoping.’

Suki said hers was structural.

‘Mine is that I locked and walked away and the machine did not lock,’ she said.

‘Say the distinction.’

‘I locked the workspace, which hides the material, and I did not lock the machine, which means somebody could have used the computer.’ She was precise. ‘They would not have seen my findings. They could have opened anything else on that machine.’

‘Which is a different exposure.’

‘Which is an entirely different exposure and I had conflated the two for about a year, and I found out during an internal audit of my own practice rather than through an incident.’ Suki shrugged. ‘Which is the best way to find out and I would rather have found out sooner.’


## What it does not cover

‘I want to state the limits,’ Suki said, ‘because we have spent an hour advocating and somebody will over-rely on it.’

‘Say them.’

‘It protects a screen from a person in a room. That is all.’ She counted. ‘It does nothing about a machine somebody takes away, nothing about material somebody has already read, and nothing whatsoever about a photograph taken before it engaged.’

‘Say the last one.’

‘Somebody with a phone can photograph a screen in about two seconds, and locking afterward is too late.’ Suki shrugged. ‘Which is not a realistic threat in a consulting room and is a realistic one on a client site, and the response is positioning rather than software.’

‘Which is what you did previously.’

‘Which is what I do still, for that specific case. I do not sit with my screen facing an open room, and I would not stop doing that because I can lock.’ She was definite. ‘One control does not retire another.’

David asked about the machine itself.

‘Does this replace locking the computer?’

‘No, and conflating them is common.’ Suki was clear. ‘Locking the workspace hides the material. Locking the machine stops somebody using it. If I am leaving a room I do both, and if I am standing up for four seconds I do one.’

‘Which is a threshold after all.’

‘Which is a threshold and I want to be honest about it.’ She laughed. ‘The workspace lock has no threshold. The machine lock has one, which is leaving the room, and that is a threshold I can apply without thinking because it is a physical event rather than a duration.’

Meera raised her own limit.

‘Mine is that it does nothing about paper,’ she said.

‘Say more.’

‘I have a printer, and a fifth of what I handle exists on paper at some point, and a locked screen above an unlocked desk is not a private desk.’ She was frank. ‘Which I did not think about for the first six months, because the screen felt like the whole problem.’

‘And now?’

‘Now the desk is clear when anybody enters, which is a second habit and took considerably longer.’ Meera shrugged. ‘And the two together are what I would actually call the practice, rather than either alone.’

David said his limit was people rather than material.

‘Somebody can read my face,’ he said.

‘Say what you mean.’

‘A deacon who walks in while I am reading something difficult will know I was reading something difficult, because I will look like a man who was reading something difficult.’ He was matter of fact. ‘The screen goes blank and my expression does not.’

‘Which nothing addresses.’

‘Which nothing addresses and which I have simply learned to manage, and which is worth saying because somebody might otherwise believe the screen is the whole of discretion.’ David shrugged. ‘It is a substantial part and it is not all of it.’


## The two people who changed their minds

‘Before we finish I want to ask something,’ Meera said. ‘Did either of you resist this initially?’

‘I did and for about eight months,’ David said immediately.

‘Say why.’

‘Because it felt like distrust.’ He was frank. ‘A minister who conceals his screen when a congregation member walks in is a minister who is hiding something from that person, and that was genuinely how it felt to me.’

‘What changed it?’

‘A conversation with somebody who put it the other way round.’ David paused. ‘She said that I was not concealing my screen from the person entering. I was protecting the person whose name was on it, who was not in the room and could not consent.’

‘Which is the correct framing.’

‘Which is the framing that made it possible, because it moved from an act of suspicion to an act of care, and those feel completely different even though the movement is identical.’ He shrugged. ‘I locked the screen the same afternoon and have not stopped.’

Suki said hers was different.

‘I resisted for about a week and for a stupid reason,’ she said. ‘I thought it was theatre.’

‘Say more.’

‘Somebody in my field performing security is a familiar and slightly embarrassing figure, and locking a screen to walk four feet looked like performance to me.’ She was rueful. ‘Which is a fear about how I appear rather than an argument about anything.’

‘What changed it?’

‘I watched a colleague do it without any self-consciousness at all, and it did not look like theatre, it looked like competence.’ Suki shrugged. ‘Which is the entire content of the change and I am slightly ashamed of how small it was.’

‘And you?’ David asked Meera.

‘I never resisted, and I have an unfair advantage.’ She was matter of fact. ‘My profession trains this. I qualified into an environment where protecting a third party's information is the first thing anybody learns, so a mechanism for doing it faster arrived as an obvious improvement rather than as a new idea.’

‘Which the two of us did not have.’

‘Which neither of you had, and I think that is worth saying to anybody reading.’ Meera was thoughtful. ‘If this feels unnatural, that is not a fact about you. It is a fact about whether your training happened to include it, and most training does not.’


## Teaching it to somebody else

‘Something I have been doing recently,’ David said, ‘and I would like to know whether either of you has tried it.’

‘Say it.’

‘Teaching it. To Michelle, who is doing the same work I am and who is about thirty years younger.’

‘How did that go?’

‘Badly at first and for a reason I did not anticipate.’ He was amused. ‘She adopted it immediately, without argument, and applied it constantly, and after a fortnight I noticed she was locking in situations where it made no sense.’

‘Such as?’

‘Alone in the building. With the door bolted. At eleven at night.’ David laughed. ‘She had built the habit so thoroughly that it had detached from any reason.’

‘Is that a problem?’ Meera asked.

‘I decided it is not, and I want to explain why, because my first instinct was to correct it.’ He paused. ‘A habit that fires when it is unnecessary costs nothing. A habit that has been trimmed to fire only when necessary requires an assessment, and the assessment is the thing that fails.’

‘So over-application is the safe failure.’

‘Over-application is the safe failure and under-application is the dangerous one, and I told her to keep doing exactly what she was doing.’

Suki said something she had noticed with clients.

‘The people who adopt this fastest are the ones who have already had the moment,’ she said.

‘Say what you mean.’

‘Somebody who has once had a screen seen adopts it in an afternoon and never lapses.’ She was matter of fact. ‘Somebody who has not had that moment adopts it slowly, applies it inconsistently, and drops it during a busy fortnight.’

‘Which is a problem, because you cannot arrange the moment.’

‘Which is a genuine problem in any advisory work and the only thing that substitutes is a specific story from somebody they trust.’ Suki nodded at David. ‘Which is why yours is worth telling. An abstract argument about exposure changes nobody. An account of a particular afternoon and a particular deacon changes people.’

Meera was thinking.

‘I have started telling my registrars mine,’ she said. ‘Which is a smaller story and I tell it anyway.’

‘Say it.’

‘A patient once told me she had seen a name on my screen, months earlier, and that she had never mentioned it and had thought about it since.’ Meera was quiet. ‘She was not upset with me. She was worried about the other person, who she knew slightly.’

‘That is before the habit.’

‘That is what produced the habit, and it is the only reason I do this at all,’ she said. ‘I did not read an argument. Somebody told me what she had seen and I have locked my screen every day since.’


## A boundary that costs nothing

Three professions with almost nothing in common, using the same single action, for three different reasons, several times a day. What that convergence describes is not a feature so much as a gap in ordinary security thinking, which is built around adversaries who want something and is silent about the situation that actually recurs: somebody walks into a room and their eyes land on a screen.

The exposure in that moment is frequently smaller than people imagine and more consequential. A name is generally sufficient. A patient's neighbour glimpsed on a clinician's display, a congregation member's name in a minister's file, a security finding read by an employee of the organisation it concerns: in each case the content is unnecessary and the identification is the whole disclosure. None of the standard controls engages, because the material is open precisely because somebody is using it.

The three practices differ in trigger rather than in substance. One attaches to standing up, which happens at the end of every appointment without any decision. One attaches to a knock, which is harder because the trigger is external, and which required a week of deliberately practising until a sound produced a movement. One attaches to any departure from the keyboard with no duration threshold at all, on the grounds that a threshold requires a judgment and judgments fail when somebody is tired. All three describe a period of two to six weeks and then invisibility, which is the objective: a reliable habit is one somebody is still performing, and an invisible one survives a bad day.

The rule that emerged and which all three endorsed is that the habit must not be attached to the sensitivity of the material. Assessing sensitivity requires thinking, thinking fails under load, and a habit with an exception has a gap that arrives on the worst possible day. Locking when a grocery list is displayed is not excessive; it is what makes the behaviour reliable when the display holds something else.

The unanticipated benefit is the one all three named without prompting, and it concerns attention rather than exposure. Somebody who can blank a screen in a moment stops managing their environment: stops angling the display, stops tracking the corridor, stops knowing where everybody in a room is. A clinician described writing more honestly once the imaginary reader disappeared, having previously produced hedged observations about a third of the time and having called it professional caution. A minister described recovering attention he had been spending on a doorway during counselling sessions without knowing he was spending it.

The relationship to encryption is worth stating precisely, since conflating the two produces real gaps. One control addresses a person physically present while somebody is working; the other addresses the material at rest when they are not. Neither substitutes for the other, and each covers a situation the other leaves entirely open. The expiry behaviour follows from the same logic: a locked screen and decrypted material are separate states, and the second lapsing during a long absence is exactly right, since otherwise unlocking would restore everything at once and the lock would become the only barrier.

### What does a fully formed version of this practice look like?

A reflex attached to a physical event, supplemented by a second trigger where the first depends on the person's own movement, applied without regard to what is displayed, never reversed while somebody is present, combined with locking the machine on leaving a room, and paired with a cleared desk where paper is involved. Practitioners describe assembling that set over a year or so, one element at a time, with the screen habit as the first and easiest component. Anybody who reads this and attaches the action to a physical event today will have completed the entire obligation by the end of the month and will not think about it again.

### What is worth taking away above everything else?

That an ordinary, frequent, undramatic exposure is the one most professionals actually face, and that it is closed by a single movement costing a fortnight to establish. ### What is the first thing to do after reading this?

Identify one physical event that happens reliably in your working day: standing at the end of a meeting, hearing a door, or moving away from the desk. Then perform the lock action every time that event occurs, for two weeks, deliberately and with no exceptions. That is the entire method. Practitioners across three unrelated professions describe having done exactly this, describe the fortnight as the only effort involved, and describe never having thought about it again afterward. Choosing the event matters more than anything else in the method, since a trigger that occurs unreliably produces a habit that operates unreliably. Anybody whose working day contains no obvious candidate should use any departure from the keyboard, which happens constantly and requires no judgment at all about duration or circumstances.


### Does anything about this change when working with a colleague present all day?

The trigger has to change, since nobody enters and nobody knocks. Practitioners sharing a permanent workspace describe locking on any departure from the desk and otherwise relying on positioning and on an explicit understanding with the colleague about what each is working on. The habit still applies and its shape differs, because a continuous presence is a different situation from an intermittent one and a trigger built around arrivals will simply never fire. Practitioners in shared offices describe this as the arrangement that works, and describe having initially tried to apply an arrivals-based trigger before realising it was solving a problem their room did not have. Adapting the trigger to the environment is the general principle, and it is worth doing deliberately rather than concluding the habit does not apply.
