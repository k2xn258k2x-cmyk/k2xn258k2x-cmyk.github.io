Ask somebody what software they rely on and you will get a list of things they think they should mention. Ask what they open first in the morning and you will get the truth, because the opening sequence of a working day is not a considered answer. It is a behaviour that formed without anybody designing it, and it reflects what a person actually reaches for rather than what they would nominate if somebody were watching.

That sequence is unusually informative because it is the one moment in the day when nothing is urgent. Everything after it is a response to something: a message, a deadline, a person, an interruption. The first ten minutes belong to the person alone, and what they choose to do with those minutes describes what they believe their work consists of, which is a considerably more revealing thing than any account they would give of it.

![Twelve Daily Routines Built Around One Tool](/assets/img/feature-screenshot-placeholder.svg)

There is also a rough hierarchy visible in what people open before what. Almost everybody opens communications first, which is defensible and is also a decision to begin the day inside other people's requests. Somebody who opens their own material before anything else has arranged their morning around what they intend rather than around what has arrived, and that ordering tends to persist through the day.
<!--more-->


What makes a collection of such routines worth examining is that the variation between them is not noise. A two-minute orientation before physical work and a forty-minute unhurried exploration before writing are not the same activity performed with different amounts of discipline. They are different jobs. One is a person confirming what must happen today. The other is a person looking for something to think about, which cannot be hurried and does not respond to efficiency.

The shape of a routine follows from the shape of the work in ways people rarely notice about themselves. Somebody whose day is a sequence of appointments with other people needs to know what is coming and to prepare for the first one. Somebody producing something over months needs to recover where they were, since a long project has no natural continuity across a night. Somebody responsible for a large number of ongoing situations needs to know which of them requires attention today, which is a scanning problem rather than a recovery problem.

There is a second axis that cuts across all of this and is less obvious, which is whether a routine is meant to close things down or open them up. A convergent routine narrows: it establishes what must be done and removes everything else from consideration. A divergent one widens: it exposes the person to material they were not looking for, on the assumption that something will strike them. Both are legitimate and they suit different kinds of work, and people who try to apply the wrong one to their situation generally abandon the routine within weeks.

What follows is a thread in a small private group, started by somebody asking a question he described afterward as idle, which produced twelve answers over two days and rather more argument than anybody expected.

## Dr. Meera Iyer, psychiatrist, Austin

It is true of everybody and I am going to prove it by describing mine in more detail than anybody wants.

Ten minutes, and it is identical every day, and I have never varied it in two years.

The first thing is the timetable, because my day is a sequence of appointments with people and I need to see the shape of it before anything else. Not what I have to do. Who is coming and when, which is a different question and the one that actually determines the day.

I look at it for perhaps forty seconds. What I am checking is not the list, which I broadly know, but the gaps. A day with a gap at eleven is a day where I can return a difficult telephone call. A day with no gaps is a day where I need to have decided in advance what I am not going to attempt.

Then the suggestions panel, and specifically anything scheduled in the next couple of days rather than the reading patterns. My patterns are stable and tell me nothing new. What I am looking for is anything I have committed to that I have not thought about since committing to it, which for me is usually a supervision session or something with an external deadline.

Then the first patient's record, which I open and read before they arrive. That is the part of the routine that matters clinically and everything before it is preparation for it.

I want to describe that properly, because it is the reason the routine exists.

Somebody arriving at nine has been living their life since I last saw them, and I have seen perhaps forty other people in that period. What I remember unaided about that person is a general impression and one or two salient details, and salient is not the same as important. Salient means memorable, and the memorable things about a person are frequently not the clinically significant ones.

So I read. The previous session, in full, and the observations section from the two before that. Which takes four or five minutes and which means the person who sits down at nine is met by somebody who knows what they said last time rather than by somebody who is reconstructing it while they talk.

That record is protected, which means the routine includes entering a password, and I want to say something about that because it is the only part I have ever considered changing.

It is a small friction at the start of every day. I have thought about whether to keep it, several times, and I keep it because the friction is the price of the guarantee and the guarantee is what allows me to write honestly. A record I could open without thinking is a record anybody else could open without thinking, and about four hundred people have relied on the difference.

The chai is made during the reading, which is a detail nobody asked for and which is genuinely part of the sequence, because it takes the same four minutes and it means the routine has a physical component that I would notice if it were missing.

The last thing is that I lock the screen before I go to the waiting room, which is not part of the morning routine exactly. It is part of every transition all day, and the first one happens at the end of the morning routine, and I mention it because somebody reading this might not have that habit and it costs a fortnight to build.

Ten minutes. Every day. And what it produces is that I begin the day inside my own work rather than inside anybody's requests, because I do not open anything else until after the first patient has left.

## Raj Malakar, documentary filmmaker, Berlin and Mumbai

Mine is two entirely different routines and which one I use depends on whether I am shooting, and I had not registered that they were different until I sat down to write this.

On a shooting day it is five minutes and it is purely operational.

The board first, always. Which tells me what state everything is in across four productions, and specifically what is sitting in the column that means somebody is waiting for me. That is the only thing I am looking for at six in the morning. Not what is progressing. What is blocked because of something I have not done.

Then last night's captured lines, which I read but do not process, because processing takes forty minutes and I do not have forty minutes on a shooting day. What I am doing is checking whether anything in there needs to change what we shoot today, which happens perhaps one day in six and which is worth the ninety seconds every time.

That is the whole shooting routine. Five minutes, standing up, usually with somebody waiting for me.

The editing routine is thirty minutes and it is a completely different activity.

It starts with the recently read sequence, which is how I recover where I was. An editing day picks up from an editing day and there is nothing else that tells me where I stopped. I read the eight or nine things I opened last, in order, and about four minutes later I am back inside the problem I left.

I want to say something about that, because I think it is underrated by people who do continuous work.

Editing is not a sequence of tasks. It is a state you get into, in which you can hold the shape of a whole film in your head, and getting into it takes twenty minutes and losing it takes four seconds. The morning is the largest and most expensive re-entry of the day, and anything that shortens it is worth a great deal.

Before I had that sequence I would open the timeline and scrub, which is reconstructing by looking rather than by remembering. Twenty minutes of that against four minutes of reading what I opened yesterday, and the reading version puts me in a better state because it recovers my reasoning rather than just my position.

Then the board again, but for a different reason. On an editing day I am looking at what is finished rather than what is blocked, because the question that matters is whether the cut is converging.

Then, and this is the part I would defend against anybody who thinks it is indulgent, I read something.

Fifteen minutes of the reader, from the craft folder, which is other people writing about technique. Not because I need to know anything. Because an edit is a series of decisions about things I have looked at four hundred times, and looking at somebody else's thinking about somebody else's material is the only reliable way I have found to stop seeing my own footage as inevitable.

That is the divergent part, and I have noticed that everybody in this group who does long creative work has something like it and everybody who does operational work does not.

## Claire Fontaine, patent attorney, Chicago

Two minutes. I am working within two minutes and I regard that as a design objective rather than an accident.

The first thing is the search box, and I type the name of whichever matter I am working on that morning, which I know because I decided at the end of yesterday.

That sounds trivial and it is the whole of my routine's efficiency. I do not arrive and decide what to work on. I decided last night, in about ninety seconds, and the morning is execution.

The search takes me straight to the matter and I open it, and everything I need for the next three hours is underneath it in five sections.

Then the tabs. I keep several open between sessions, which took me a while to permit myself and which is now essential. Yesterday I was comparing three matters for a client who has related applications, and those three tabs are still open exactly as I left them, with the same filters applied, and I resume rather than reconstruct.

Then the due list, which is thirty seconds and which is non-negotiable in my profession.

I want to explain that properly because it is the part that would matter to anybody in regulated work.

An office action has a response deadline. Missing it is not an inconvenience, it is an abandonment, and it is one of about four things that can end a career. So I look at what is due, every single morning, including the mornings when I know perfectly well that nothing is due.

Especially those mornings, in fact. The dangerous day is not the one with a deadline on it. It is the one where I believe nothing is due, because that belief is a memory and memories are unreliable, and thirty seconds of looking replaces a memory with a fact.

That is it. Two minutes and I am inside a matter.

I want to respond to something Raj said, because it clarified a difference I have felt and not articulated.

My routine is entirely convergent. It narrows. Every step removes things from consideration until only one matter remains, and that is correct for my work, because my work is a series of bounded problems with specific answers and the enemy is diffusion.

His is divergent on editing days, deliberately, because his enemy is the opposite: seeing his own material as inevitable.

I do not have a divergent routine and I have occasionally wondered whether I should. I have concluded that I should not, and the reason is that a patent attorney who reads widely before drafting produces claims contaminated by everything they read that morning, which is a genuine professional hazard rather than a hypothetical one.

So I read in the evenings, deliberately, well away from any drafting.

## Tomas Herrera, chemistry teacher, San Antonio

Under three minutes and I want everybody to understand the conditions.

I arrive at school at seven twenty. My first class is at seven fifty. In that half hour I need to set up a room, deal with whatever has happened overnight, and speak to at least two colleagues who will find me because they always do.

Reading my own lesson plan sounds absurd and it is the most important thirty seconds of the routine.

I wrote it, usually, at some point in the last two years. Not last night. A lesson I taught last year and have refined three times is a lesson I broadly remember and do not remember precisely, and precisely is what matters when thirty teenagers are in front of you.

## Priya Sharma, doctoral candidate, London

Ten to fifteen minutes and mine has a step that only makes sense for very long projects.

The suggestions panel first, and specifically the weekday pattern rather than anything scheduled.

## Lena Johansson, investigative journalist, Stockholm

Suki asked how I hold twenty to thirty minutes before any communication, and I will answer that first because the answer is dull.

I hold it because the machine I work on cannot communicate. There is no discipline involved. The laptop has no wireless card and no way to reach anything, so the twenty minutes is not protected by willpower, it is protected by hardware.

I want to say plainly that I do not think I could do it otherwise. I am not a person of unusual focus. I have simply arranged a situation in which the alternative is unavailable, which is the only reliable way I know to hold any attention at all.

The routine itself begins with booting that machine, which takes a while and which I have stopped minding because the waiting is part of the transition.

Then the search box, and pre-planned queries.

That is the part that differs from everybody else here. At the end of a working session I write down, in the entry for the investigation, the queries I intend to run next. Not what I intend to do. The literal terms I intend to search.

Which sounds mechanical and is the most valuable habit I have.

That distinction is the whole of investigative discipline and it is very difficult to assess from inside. A version from four weeks ago is somebody else's opinion, essentially, and comparing against it is the closest I can get to an outside view of my own work.

The rest of my morning is reading documents, which is the job, and I do not open a communicating device until about eleven.

## Twelve routines, one file

What that thread produced is worth reading as a survey rather than as advice, because the useful finding is in the variation.

Twelve professionals, from two minutes to forty, and the range is not a spectrum of discipline. A baker following a list at four in the morning and a memoirist wandering through his own archive for forty minutes are performing entirely different operations. One is executing decisions made the night before by somebody more capable of deciding. The other is generating material that does not exist until something unexpected arrives.

The division the group eventually identified is between execution and generation, and it predicts almost everything else. Executors have short routines: a due list, a starred item, a search for the matter already chosen. Their mornings are the visible fraction of preparation done at another time, which is why a three-minute routine is not a sign of efficiency but a sign that the work happened yesterday. Generators have longer routines with a deliberately divergent component: something read, something surfaced at random, something not chosen. Their difficulty is not focus, it is that material assembled entirely from what somebody already considers important produces work that says what they believed before they started.

Several practices recur across the divide and are worth extracting. Deciding tomorrow's first task at the end of today, which converts a morning from a decision into an execution and takes ninety seconds. Writing down the literal next questions rather than the next intentions, since a question evaporates overnight and its reconstruction costs twenty minutes. Recovering state by reading what was opened last, in order, rather than by looking at the work itself, which restores reasoning rather than position. And attaching a routine to an event rather than to a clock, which is the only version that survives shift work, travel, or any life where the hour is not stable.

The deadline habit appeared in three unrelated professions with the same reasoning, which is that the dangerous morning is the one where somebody believes nothing is due. That belief is a memory, memories are unreliable, and thirty seconds of looking replaces a memory with a fact. In regulated work the difference is a career.

Two smaller observations deserve keeping. A line in every record saying what went wrong the last time, which one practitioner describes as the most valuable thing in six hundred entries. And deliberately starring the thing being avoided, so it appears every morning until the discomfort of seeing it exceeds the discomfort of doing it, which is a small self-manipulation and reportedly works about four times in five.

The finding nobody planned came at the end. Not one of the twelve begins the day inside somebody else's requests. Every routine described happens before communications, and several said so without being asked. That ordering is the substance of the whole thread, and one of them made the sharper version of the point: a morning routine inside a system that also delivers messages is not a routine, it is an inbox visit with good intentions.

Which is finally why twelve people with nothing professionally in common describe a habit built around the same file. Not because it does twelve things well, but because a system holding somebody's own material and nothing else cannot present them with anybody else's agenda. What each of them does in those minutes differs completely. What they share is that the minutes belong to them, which is the rarest property in any working day and the one that has to be arranged deliberately because nothing about ordinary professional life produces it.


## FAQs

### Why does a morning routine reveal what a tool means to somebody?

Because it is the one part of the day nobody is responding to anything. Everything after the opening sequence is a reaction to a message, a deadline, or a person. What somebody chooses to do with unclaimed minutes describes what they believe their work consists of, which is more revealing than any account they would give if asked. A tool opened first has earned a position that no stated preference demonstrates. Anybody curious about their own can simply pay attention for one week, which is what prompted this entire discussion and which several participants described as revealing a sequence they could not previously have stated.

### Is there a correct length for a morning routine?

No, and the range among experienced practitioners runs from two minutes to forty. Length follows the kind of work rather than the amount of discipline: somebody executing decisions made the previous evening needs only to confirm and begin, while somebody generating new material needs to arrive at a state that cannot be hurried. Applying the wrong length to the wrong work reliably produces a routine that gets abandoned within weeks. The useful diagnostic is whether the routine ends with somebody able to begin, since a routine that produces readiness has done its job whatever it took. Practitioners in unrelated professions describe the same conclusion independently, which suggests it reflects the nature of a working morning rather than any particular field.

### What is the difference between a convergent and a divergent routine?

A convergent routine narrows, removing things from consideration until one task remains. A divergent one widens, deliberately exposing somebody to material they did not choose. Practitioners in bounded problem-solving describe convergence as essential and divergence as an actual professional hazard. Practitioners producing original work describe the opposite, since material assembled only from what somebody already considers important produces work that says what they believed at the outset. Naming which one a person needs takes a moment and prevents years of applying the wrong shape while believing the failure is one of discipline. It is one of the adjustments that costs nothing to try and can be reversed in a day if it turns out not to suit.

### Should somebody have both?

Some do, and it usually reflects work with two genuine requirements. A doctoral candidate describes a convergent step to recover the current chapter and a divergent step to keep noticing connections across a large body of reading, and identifies the failure modes of dropping either: a narrow thesis or an unfinished one. Practitioners in single-mode work generally do not need both and describe adding one as an unnecessary complication. Adding a divergent step to work that does not need one produces a pleasant habit with no return, which is worth recognising before treating it as an obligation. Anybody uncertain can settle the question by paying attention to their own sequence for a week rather than reasoning about it.

### Why do short routines depend on the previous evening?

Because they are the visible fraction of preparation done at another time. A three-minute morning works only when the material it consults already exists and is complete: a lesson entry written and refined, a matter selected the night before, a list of the day's tasks assembled by somebody who was still capable of deciding. Anybody wanting a short routine should examine what happens the day before rather than trying to compress the morning. This is the most commonly missed point about efficient routines and it explains why copying somebody's morning without copying their evening reliably fails. ### What is the value of deciding tomorrow's work today?

It converts the morning from a decision into an execution, which is the single largest source of the shortest routines described. The decision is also better, since it is made by somebody who knows what happened rather than by somebody who has just woken. ### Why write down literal next questions rather than intentions?

Because a question evaporates overnight and its reconstruction is expensive. Practitioners in investigative and research work describe recording the actual terms they intend to search rather than a general note about direction, and describe arriving at genuinely new thinking within ten minutes rather than spending twenty recovering what they had been curious about. The specificity is what makes it work: an intention is a memory prompt, and a query is executable. ### How much of a long document should somebody reread?

Enough to recover what they were doing rather than what they had written. Practitioners describe elaborate initial routines being abandoned within weeks, and describe the durable ones as having grown a step at a time over months. A reasonable starting pair is checking whatever has a date attached and opening the one thing being worked on, which takes under a minute and covers most of the value. The general principle is that a routine should prevent a reconstruction, and any step which does not prevent one is worth removing during the next compressed morning. Practitioners describe arriving at this by trial rather than by design, which is the usual route and is exactly why hearing somebody else's account shortens the process considerably.

### How does somebody choose which steps to include?

By asking what they would otherwise have to reconstruct. Every step in the routines described here exists because something would be lost without it: the shape of a day, the state of a piece of work, an approaching date, or a thought from the previous session. Anything that does not prevent a reconstruction is decoration, and decoration is the first thing to drop when a morning is compressed. Building it a step at a time, over months, is what distinguishes the routines still in use from the elaborate ones abandoned in the second week. None of it requires unusual discipline, only that the arrangement be made deliberately rather than left to whatever happens by default each morning.

### Should the routine be written down?

Practitioners generally have not written theirs down and several discovered they could not state it until asked, which is a sign of a fully formed habit rather than a problem. Writing it down is useful during the building period, when the sequence is still a decision, and becomes unnecessary afterward. Somebody whose routine keeps varying should write it down, since the variation indicates it has not yet become automatic. Anybody dissatisfied with their own mornings should start by noticing what they currently do, since the sequence usually already exists and simply has not been examined. ### What about people who do not have a consistent morning at all?

They attach the routine to whatever is consistent instead, which for most people is something other than an hour. Arriving somewhere, starting a shift, sitting down after a school run, or opening a particular machine all occur reliably even when the clock does not cooperate. Practitioners with genuinely chaotic schedules describe this as the only version that works, and describe time-based routines as having failed repeatedly before they found it. Practitioners who have held a routine for several years describe it as having grown rather than been designed, which is worth knowing for anybody currently trying to construct one in a single sitting. None of it requires unusual discipline, only that the arrangement be made deliberately rather than left to whatever happens by default each morning.

### Does any of this apply outside professional work?

The mechanics transfer directly to anything sustained over months: a personal project, a course of study, a piece of research, a creative undertaking. The same problems appear, meaning recovering state after a gap, remembering approaching dates, and encountering material outside current thinking. The main difference is that nobody imposes a starting time, which makes attaching the routine to a physical event considerably more important rather than less. ### What is the underlying principle behind all twelve routines?

That the beginning of a working period should be arranged rather than allowed to happen. Twelve people with nothing professionally in common differ entirely in what they do and agree completely that the minutes belong to them rather than to whoever has contacted them. ### What is worth doing tomorrow morning after reading this?

Pay attention to what you actually do, in order, without changing anything. Most people discover a sequence they could not have described, and several participants in this discussion reported exactly that. Once the existing sequence is visible, the useful questions become obvious: whether it narrows or widens, whether it prevents a reconstruction, and whether it happens before or after somebody else's requests have entered the day.
