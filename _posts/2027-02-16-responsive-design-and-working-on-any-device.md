Almost every professional tool is designed for a person sitting at a desk, and almost no professional life happens entirely at one. Work occurs in meeting rooms, at events, on location, in transit, in corridors, and in the few minutes between one commitment and the next. A tool available only in one posture at one piece of furniture is a tool absent from a substantial proportion of the moments when somebody actually needs it.

The usual response to that gap is a second product. A full application for the desk and a reduced companion for everywhere else, with a subset of the capabilities, a different arrangement, and a separate thing to learn. That arrangement is so common that people have stopped noticing what it implies, which is that the tool is now two tools, and that whichever one somebody is holding is probably not the one that does the thing they want.

![Responsive Design and Working on Any Device](/assets/img/feature-screenshot-placeholder.svg)

The consequences are quieter than an outright failure and more corrosive. Somebody develops a mental model of which device does what, and then spends attention maintaining it. They defer a task until they are back at a desk, and the deferral produces a small backlog. They capture something in a reduced tool and then reconcile it later, which is a step that occasionally does not happen.
<!--more-->


There is a different arrangement available, and it follows from something structural rather than from any additional effort. A tool that is a document interpreted by a browser inherits everything the browser already does about screens, and browsers have been solving the problem of varying display sizes for a very long time. The layout adapts because the environment adapts it, which means adaptation is not a feature somebody built twice.

What that produces is a distinction worth stating precisely, because it is the whole subject. The arrangement of things on a screen changes with the size of the screen. What the tool can do does not. A person on a phone has the same searching, the same protection, the same editing, and the same everything else as a person at a desk, differently arranged. Nothing is withheld and nothing is simplified into a lesser version.

That does not mean every activity is equally sensible everywhere, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest. Composing at length on a phone is possible and unpleasant. Comparing several documents side by side requires width. What changes across devices is not capability but suitability, and suitability is a judgement the person makes rather than a restriction the tool imposes.

There is also a visual dimension that people underrate until they encounter it. A tool that looks considered on a large display and crude on a small one signals that one of them was an afterthought, and people respond to that signal whether or not they articulate it. Consistency of appearance across sizes is not decoration; it is what makes somebody willing to open the thing in front of other people.

What follows is a conversation between two people who spend substantial portions of their working lives away from a desk, in entirely different circumstances, and who use the same file on three sizes of screen for quite different purposes.


## A tablet in a church hall and a phone in Portugal

David had suggested it, which he said afterward was because he was standing in a hall with nothing to do for eleven minutes.

‘I am at an event,’ he said. ‘Which is why I am on a tablet and why I look like I am in a corridor.’

‘You are in a corridor.’

‘I am in a corridor and it is the quiet part of the afternoon.’ He turned the device. ‘And you are somewhere considerably better.’

‘I am on a hillside outside Porto looking at a building we may or may not shoot in.’ Raj was holding his phone at arm's length. ‘Which is why I am on this rather than anything sensible.’

‘Say what you are actually doing on it.’

‘Capturing. Which is nearly all I do on a phone.’ He counted. ‘Photographs, four lines about the light, and a note that the access road is worse than the map suggests.’

‘Which is what I do on the tablet.’

‘Say yours, because I think our two devices are doing different jobs and I want to know whether that is the device or the work.’

‘It is the work and the device is following it.’ David was matter of fact. ‘I am running an event. What I need is what is happening, who I am about to thank, and somewhere to put a thought before it goes.’

‘Which is retrieval.’

‘Which is mostly retrieval with a little capture, and yours is mostly capture with a little retrieval, and we are using the same thing on two sizes of screen.’

‘So the interesting question is what changes.’

‘Which is what I proposed this call to find out, and I suspect the answer is less than either of us expects.’


## What a tablet actually gives you

‘Describe what you see,’ Raj said. ‘Because I have never used it on a tablet.’

‘It depends entirely on which way I am holding it, which took me a while to notice.’ David turned the device to landscape. ‘Like this, I have the panel down the side and the content beside it. Which is my laptop, smaller.’

‘Everything is there.’

‘Everything is there and nothing has moved.’ He was matter of fact. ‘Which is genuinely disorienting the first time, because I expected something different and got the same arrangement.’

‘And in the other orientation?’

He turned it.

‘Now it has stacked,’ David said. ‘The panel is above rather than beside, and the content has the full width.’

‘Which is better for reading.’

‘Which is considerably better for reading and worse for orientation, and I use both deliberately.’ He counted. ‘Landscape when I am working out what is happening. Portrait when I am reading something.’

‘So the orientation is a mode.’

‘The orientation is a mode and I switch about eleven times an afternoon without thinking about it.’ David shrugged. ‘Which is a thing I do with a physical object rather than with a control, and it is somehow more natural than a button.’

‘Say what you actually open.’

‘The ticker first, always.’ He was matter of fact. ‘Which tells me what is happening today, and at an event that is the only question.’

‘Which is the same as your morning routine.’

‘Which is exactly my morning routine on a different device, which I had not noticed until this moment.’ David laughed. ‘The panel is smaller and I look at the same thing.’

‘And then?’

‘Then whatever I have starred, which today is the running order and a note about four people I am thanking by name.’ He counted. ‘Which is the actual reason I am holding this rather than a piece of paper.’

‘Say why not paper.’

‘Because I had paper for thirty years and paper does not tell me that somebody's wife is unwell.’ David was serious under it. ‘Which is in a pastoral note, which is protected, and which I read before I speak to them.’

‘So you are entering a password in a corridor.’

‘Which I was worried about and which is fine, because the protection covers the screen entirely when I lock it.’ He demonstrated. ‘Which on a tablet is the whole device, and I do it every time I put it down.’

Raj asked about capture.

‘What do you do when something occurs to you?’

‘The button in the corner, which on a touch device is the single most useful thing here.’ David was emphatic. ‘Because it is large, it is always in the same place, and I can hit it while holding the thing in one hand.’

‘Which is not true of a menu.’

‘Which is not true of anything requiring precision, and a person in a hall holding a tablet and a coffee has one hand and no precision.’ He shrugged. ‘I use it perhaps six times at an event.’

‘Say what goes in.’

‘Four words, usually. Somebody said something I want to follow up. Somebody asked about the building fund.’ David counted. ‘Which becomes a proper entry that evening, and which would be entirely gone otherwise.’


## What a phone is actually for

‘Now yours,’ David said. ‘And I want to know what you refuse to do on it.’

‘I refuse to write anything of length and I refuse to compare things.’ Raj was immediate. ‘Everything else I do constantly.’

‘Say what the layout looks like.’

‘One column, and the panel is hidden until I ask for it.’ He showed it. ‘Which gives the content the whole screen, which on a phone is the only sensible arrangement.’

‘And when you want the panel?’

‘One tap and it comes over the top, and another and it is gone.’ Raj shrugged. ‘Which I use perhaps twice a session, because on a phone I am rarely orienting. I know what I am doing before I take it out of my pocket.’

‘Which is different from me.’

‘Which is completely different, because you are standing in a hall wondering what happens next and I am standing on a hillside knowing exactly what I want to record.’

‘So the panel matters more to me.’

‘The panel is your whole use and it is almost none of mine, on the same file.’ Raj was definite. ‘Which is the thing I find genuinely interesting about this conversation.’

‘Say what you use most.’

‘The message log, which on a phone is the natural thing.’ He opened it. ‘Because it is a conversation shape, and a phone is a device people already use for conversations, so the gesture is one everybody has.’

‘Which is thumbs.’

‘Which is thumbs, and short lines, and no formatting, and the whole thing takes eleven seconds.’ Raj counted. ‘Access road worse than mapped. Light on the west face until about four. Owner mentions a second building.’

‘Three lines.’

‘Three lines in under a minute while walking, which I process into a proper entry that evening on something with a keyboard.’ He shrugged. ‘Which is the same two-stage thing I do with meetings, applied to a location.’

‘And retrieval?’

‘The search bar with the dropdown, which is the thing I use most after capture.’ Raj demonstrated. ‘I type three letters and it proposes things, and I tap one.’

‘Which on a small screen matters more.’

‘Which on a small screen is the difference between usable and not, because typing a full query on a phone is unpleasant and typing three letters is nothing.’ He was definite. ‘So the proposals are doing the work my thumbs would otherwise do.’

‘Say what you look up in the field.’

‘Whether I have been here before, essentially.’ Raj was matter of fact. ‘Which sounds absurd and is a real question after four years of scouting. I search a place name and find out whether there is already an entry.’

‘Has there been?’

‘Twice, and both times I had photographed the same building from a different angle two years earlier for a different project.’ He laughed. ‘Which I would not have known and which changed what I did that afternoon.’

David asked about photographs.

‘Are you attaching them there?’

‘Attaching them there, immediately, because a photograph on a phone that I intend to move later is a photograph that stays on the phone.’ Raj was emphatic. ‘Which I learned the expensive way across about two years.’


## Nothing was taken away

‘I want to establish something,’ David said, ‘because I assumed the opposite for about a month.’

‘Say what you assumed.’

‘That this was a reduced version.’ He was frank. ‘Because that is what every other thing I have used does, so I opened it on the tablet expecting to find out what was missing.’

‘And you did not.’

‘I did not find anything missing and I looked, deliberately, for about an hour.’ David counted. ‘Searching works. The editor works. Protection works. Categories, starred items, dates, the schedule, the reading. All of it.’

‘Which is because it is the same thing.’

‘Which somebody eventually explained to me and which I want stated plainly for anybody like me.’ He was emphatic. ‘It is one file. There is no other version. The thing on the tablet is the thing on the laptop, arranged differently.’

‘Say why that matters beyond convenience.’

‘Because a reduced version teaches you not to try.’ David was serious. ‘I spent years with tools where I knew certain things only worked properly at a desk, and so I stopped attempting them anywhere else, and the not attempting became a habit.’

‘Which persists after the limitation goes.’

‘Which persisted for a month after the limitation did not exist, because I never tested it.’ He shrugged. ‘I was operating a restriction that was entirely in my head.’

Raj said his experience was the same in a different register.

‘Mine was the editor,’ he said.

‘Say it.’

‘I assumed I would get a plain text box on a phone.’ He was matter of fact. ‘Which is what happens in most things, and which is reasonable, and which is not what happens here.’

‘You get the whole editor.’

‘I get the whole editor, on a phone, which I do not particularly want and which is apparently what one file produces.’ Raj laughed. ‘I use bold and lists and nothing else on a small screen and it is all available.’

‘Which is the point rather than the use.’

‘Which is exactly the point. The availability costs nothing and the reduction would have cost me something on the one occasion I needed it.’

David asked about protection specifically.

‘Say how it behaves on a phone.’

‘Identically, and I checked because I was curious.’ Raj was precise. ‘The same protection, the same password, the same behaviour where it stays available for a working period.’

‘And locking?’

‘Covers the entire screen, whatever the screen is.’ He demonstrated. ‘Which on a phone is the whole device, which is arguably more useful than on a laptop.’

‘Say why.’

‘Because a phone gets handed to people.’ Raj shrugged. ‘Somebody wants to see a photograph. And a phone with something protected on the screen handed to a stranger is a considerably worse situation than a laptop.’

‘Which I had not considered.’

‘Which I had not considered either until a producer asked to see a location shot and I passed the thing across without thinking.’


## What Raj does that David would not attempt

‘There is something I do on a phone that I suspect you would find alarming,’ Raj said.

‘Say it.’

‘I open protected material in public.’

David made a face.

‘Say more before I react.’

‘Not casually.’ Raj was precise. ‘But I have a small number of protected entries containing agreements with people who appear in the films, and occasionally I need one while standing in front of somebody.’

‘Which is a phone screen in a public place.’

‘Which is a phone screen in a public place and I do it deliberately with two things in mind.’ He counted. ‘First, I turn away from whoever I am with. Second, I have the blanking ready before I open it.’

‘Say what ready means.’

‘That I know where it is and I have used it enough that it is one action rather than a search for a control.’ Raj shrugged. ‘Which sounds trivial and is the difference between blanking a screen in under a second and fumbling.’

‘And you have needed it.’

‘Twice, both times because somebody moved and I did not want to explain why I was suddenly turning away.’ He was matter of fact. ‘Blanking is invisible. Turning your back is a statement.’

David thought about that.

‘I would not do it and I think you are right that it is available.’

‘Say why you would not.’

‘Because what I hold is about people in a congregation and the place I would be standing is a room containing those people.’ He was definite. ‘Which is a different situation from a hillside with a producer.’

‘So the risk is proximity rather than the device.’

‘The risk is that the person whose name is on the screen may be four feet away, which is never true for you.’ David shrugged. ‘So I wait, always, and it costs me almost nothing because pastoral material is rarely urgent in that sense.’

‘Which is the correct assessment for your situation.’

‘Which is the assessment I made after doing it once, in a hall, and feeling extremely uncomfortable about it for the rest of the afternoon.’ He laughed without much humour. ‘Nothing happened. I simply did not like it and I trust that.’


## What suits which size

‘Practical division,’ Raj said. ‘Because we have established that everything works everywhere and that is not the same as everything being sensible everywhere.’

‘Say yours.’

‘Three sizes and three jobs.’ He counted. ‘The laptop is for editing, which means long work with several things open. The tablet I barely use. The phone is capture and lookup.’

‘Say the several things open.’

‘Which is the thing I genuinely cannot do on anything smaller.’ Raj was definite. ‘Comparing four footage records at once, which needs width and which is most of what editing consists of.’

‘Which is my sermon writing.’

‘Say yours.’

‘Laptop for writing, always, because ninety minutes of prose needs a keyboard and a proper screen.’ David counted. ‘Tablet for events and for reading. Phone almost never.’

‘Why almost never?’

‘Because I am sixty-two and my phone screen is small and my eyes are not what they were.’ He was cheerful about it. ‘Which is a completely legitimate reason and not one anybody designs for.’

‘Which the tablet solves.’

‘Which the tablet solves entirely, and it is why my second device is a tablet rather than a phone when most people would have it the other way round.’ David shrugged. ‘The thing adapts to the screen and I chose the screen.’

Raj said something about the board.

‘There is one thing that is better on a tablet than anywhere.’

‘Say it.’

‘The pipeline view.’ He was precise. ‘Which on a laptop is fine and on a tablet in landscape is genuinely better, because you can touch the cards.’

‘Moving things.’

‘Moving something from one column to another with a finger is a completely different gesture from doing it with a pointer.’ Raj was definite. ‘It feels like moving an object rather than operating a control.’

‘Which is a small thing.’

‘Which is a small thing and it changed how often I review the board, because reviewing it became pleasant.’ He shrugged. ‘Which is not an argument I would have expected to make.’

David mentioned analysis.

‘The charts are the opposite for me.’

‘Say why.’

‘Because they are small on a tablet and I look at them rarely enough that I would rather do it properly.’ He was matter of fact. ‘Which is a preference rather than a limitation. They render. I choose not to.’

‘Which is the honest version of everything in this conversation.’

‘Which is the honest version, and I want it stated rather than pretending I use every part of this everywhere.’


## The awkward question about the folder

‘There is a practical difficulty and I want to address it rather than skirting it,’ Raj said.

‘Say it.’

‘My material is a folder, and folders on phones are a different proposition from folders on laptops.’

‘Which is a real thing.’

‘Which is a genuinely real thing and the arrangement each person ends up with depends on their devices.’ He counted. ‘So I want to describe what I actually do rather than what would be ideal.’

‘Go.’

‘My laptop holds the material and is the authoritative copy.’ Raj was precise. ‘My phone captures, into a copy, which I consolidate in the evening.’

‘Which is a two-stage arrangement.’

‘Which is the two-stage arrangement I already had for a completely different reason, so it costs me nothing.’ He shrugged. ‘I was already processing captured fragments in the evening. The device boundary happens to align with the processing boundary.’

‘Which is convenient.’

‘Which is convenient and I want to be clear it is convenient rather than designed.’ Raj was frank. ‘Somebody whose work does not have that shape would need a different arrangement.’

‘Say what David does.’

David laughed.

‘Something considerably less sophisticated.’

‘Say it anyway.’

‘My tablet reads and my laptop writes.’ He counted. ‘Which means the tablet has a copy that is a few days old, which is entirely sufficient for what I do with it.’

‘Because you are reading.’

‘Because I am reading a sermon I wrote on Thursday and a running order I made on Friday, and it is Sunday, and nothing has changed.’ David shrugged. ‘The copy being three days old is not a problem for a document that was finished three days ago.’

‘And the capture?’

‘Goes into the tablet copy and I move it across on Monday.’ He was matter of fact. ‘Which is four items and takes about two minutes and which I do while making coffee.’

‘So both of us have a primary device.’

‘Both of us have a primary device and a secondary one with a job, and neither of us is attempting to keep two things identical.’ David was definite. ‘Which I suspect is the actual advice.’

Raj agreed.

‘Say it properly.’

‘Decide which device is authoritative and give the others a defined role.’ David counted. ‘Mine reads. His captures. Neither of us is pretending the second one is a second desk.’

‘Which is where people go wrong.’

‘Which is where I went wrong for about two months, trying to work identically on both, and being annoyed that it was awkward.’ He shrugged. ‘It was awkward because I was asking a tablet to be a laptop.’


## The thing David got wrong for two months

‘Since I have admitted to one mistake I will describe it properly,’ David said.

‘Say it.’

‘I tried to write a sermon on the tablet.’

Raj laughed.

‘Say how it went.’

‘It went perfectly well and I hated it, and those two things are not the same and I confused them for a long time.’ David was precise. ‘The editor worked. Everything worked. I produced about nine hundred words and they were worse than usual.’

‘Say why worse.’

‘Because I was fighting the device rather than thinking about the subject.’ He was thoughtful. ‘A tablet keyboard is adequate and it is not invisible, and prose requires the tools to be invisible.’

‘Which is not a limitation of anything.’

‘Which is not a limitation of the software at all, and I spent a month believing it was.’ David shrugged. ‘I kept thinking something was wrong with the arrangement, and the arrangement was fine and I was doing an unsuitable thing.’

‘Say what changed.’

‘I stopped, and I decided what each device was for, and I have not tried to write on the tablet since.’ He was matter of fact. ‘And the tablet became considerably more useful the moment I stopped asking it to do something it was not suited to.’

‘Which is a general lesson.’

‘Which is the general lesson and it took me an embarrassingly long time.’ David counted. ‘Capability is not suitability. The thing being possible everywhere does not mean it is sensible everywhere, and the judgement about which is mine rather than the software's.’

Raj said his equivalent was the opposite direction.

‘Mine was refusing to try things.’

‘Say it.’

‘I assumed for about a year that the phone was capture only.’ He was frank. ‘Which came from previous tools and which I never tested.’

‘And?’

‘And I needed a detail from a regulation document while standing in a field with a producer, and I searched it out of desperation, and it came back from inside an attached document.’ Raj shrugged. ‘On a phone. In a field. From a document I had never opened on that device.’

‘Which you assumed would not work.’

‘Which I assumed required a laptop for no reason other than that everything else in my life does.’ He was definite. ‘So he spent a month attempting the unsuitable and I spent a year refusing the entirely possible, and both are the same error.’

‘Say the error.’

‘Believing something about the tool rather than testing it.’ Raj was matter of fact. ‘His belief was optimistic and mine was pessimistic and neither of us had checked.’


## How it looks

‘I want to say something that is not about function at all,’ David said.

‘Go on.’

‘It looks well on the tablet, and that matters more than I would have admitted a year ago.’

‘Say what you mean by looks well.’

‘The panels have that frosted quality where what is behind them shows through softened.’ He turned the screen. ‘Which on a high-resolution display is genuinely attractive.’

‘Which is decoration.’

‘Which is decoration and which has a practical consequence I did not anticipate.’ David was thoughtful. ‘I hold this in front of people. At events, in meetings, in somebody's living room.’

‘So it is visible.’

‘So what it looks like is a thing other people see, and something that looks crude makes me self-conscious about using it.’ He shrugged. ‘Which is not a rational consideration and it is a real one.’

‘Michelle said something about it.’

‘Michelle said it looks better than most of the applications she uses, which from somebody thirty years younger than me is not nothing.’ David laughed. ‘And she said it about the tablet rather than the laptop, which is where most things look worst.’

Raj agreed and put it differently.

‘Mine is that it does not look like a website pretending to be an application,’ he said.

‘Say the distinction.’

‘Things that run in a browser frequently look like things running in a browser.’ He was precise. ‘The movement is slightly wrong, the surfaces are flat, and everything responds a fraction late.’

‘And here?’

‘The transitions are smooth and the surfaces have depth and it responds immediately.’ Raj shrugged. ‘Which on a phone matters disproportionately, because everything else on a phone is a native application and the comparison is constant.’

‘So it holds up beside them.’

‘It holds up beside them, which for one file is a genuinely surprising outcome, and I say that as somebody who spends his life looking at how things appear.’

David asked about consistency.

‘Is it the same on both?’

‘Identical, which I checked while we were talking.’ Raj was matter of fact. ‘Same colours, same proportions, same weight of type relative to the screen.’

‘Which sounds obvious.’

‘Which sounds obvious and is not, because most things that adapt across sizes end up with a large version and a small version that look like cousins.’ He was definite. ‘This looks like the same thing at two sizes, which is a different property.’


## The moment a device stopped being a compromise

‘I want to describe when the tablet stopped feeling like a lesser thing,’ David said.

‘Say it.’

‘A funeral visit, about eight months ago.’ He was quieter. ‘Which is not the setting anybody imagines for a discussion about software and it is the one that mattered.’

‘Go on.’

‘I was in somebody's front room, and the family were talking about the man who had died, and they mentioned something he had said at a meeting some years earlier.’

‘Which you had a record of.’

‘Which I had a record of and which I would previously have said I would look up when I got back.’ David counted. ‘And instead I found it while they were still talking, on the tablet on my knee, and read them four lines he had said.’

‘In the room.’

‘In the room, at the moment it was relevant, which changed what that visit was.’ He was matter of fact. ‘His daughter asked me to write it down for her and I sent it that evening.’

Raj was quiet.

‘Say what made it possible.’

‘That the thing on my knee was the same thing as the thing on my desk.’ David was definite. ‘Not a viewer. Not a version. The whole record, searchable, in a front room.’

‘Which you would not have got from a reduced version.’

‘I would have got a note saying the record existed and lived somewhere else, which is exactly nothing in that moment.’ He shrugged. ‘And I would have said I would look it up, and I would have, and it would have been a different thing entirely by Thursday.’

‘Because the moment had passed.’

‘Because grief has moments and they do not wait for somebody to get back to their office.’ David paused. ‘Which is the most serious argument I can make about a layout adapting to a screen, and it is a real one.’

‘That is not an argument about a layout.’

‘It is entirely an argument about a layout,’ he said. ‘Because the reason it was possible is that the search worked and the protection worked and the record was complete, on a device I happened to be carrying. If any of that had been reduced, I would have been holding a nice screen with nothing on it.’


## The layout adapts and the capability does not

Almost every professional tool assumes a desk, and almost no professional life is confined to one. The usual accommodation is a second product with fewer capabilities, which is so common that people have stopped noticing what it implies: the tool is now two tools, and whichever one somebody is holding is probably not the one that does the thing they want.

What both practitioners here describe is a different arrangement arising from something structural. A document interpreted by a browser inherits everything browsers already do about display sizes, which means the adaptation is not a feature anybody built twice. The arrangement changes with the screen. What the tool can do does not.

The consequences of that distinction take a while to register, and both describe having believed something untrue for a substantial period. One assumed a reduced version and looked for what was missing, deliberately, for an hour, and found nothing. He describes the more interesting effect as the habit a reduced version teaches, which is not to attempt things, and describes having operated a restriction that existed only in his own head for a month after it had ceased to exist anywhere else.

The other made the opposite error and describes it as identical. He assumed a phone was for capture only, on the basis of every previous tool, and never tested it, until he searched a regulatory detail in a field out of desperation and received an answer from inside an attached document. One belief was optimistic and one pessimistic; neither had been checked.

The practical division that emerged is about suitability rather than capability, and both are explicit that the judgement belongs to the person. A large screen suits work requiring several things visible at once and extended composition. A tablet in landscape suits orientation and in portrait suits reading, with the physical rotation functioning as a mode switch that both describe as more natural than any control. A phone suits capture and lookup, where a conversational capture surface fits the gesture people already have and predictive search does the work thumbs would otherwise do.

One practitioner spent a month attempting long-form writing on a tablet, which worked and which he disliked, and describes having confused those two facts for far too long. The prose was worse because a tablet keyboard is adequate rather than invisible, and prose requires invisibility. The tablet became considerably more useful the moment he stopped asking it to be a laptop, which is the general lesson underneath the entire conversation.

The multi-device arrangement both arrived at is deliberately asymmetric. One device is authoritative and the others have defined roles: one reads, one captures, and neither is pretending to be a second desk. Both describe the attempt to work identically on two devices as the source of their early frustration, and both describe the resolution as assigning jobs rather than achieving parity.

The visual dimension came last and neither dismissed it. Something held in front of other people at an event or in a meeting is something whose appearance affects whether somebody is comfortable using it, which is not a rational consideration and is a real one. Both describe the appearance as consistent across sizes rather than as a large version and a small version that resemble each other, and one notes that things running in a browser usually look like things running in a browser, and that this does not.

What all of it reduces to is that the professional opening this on a phone at a location, on a tablet in a hall, or on a laptop at a desk is using one tool with one set of capabilities, arranged for whatever screen is in front of them. Nothing is withheld anywhere. What varies is what somebody sensibly chooses to do, which is a decision they make rather than a limitation they encounter.


## FAQs

### Why do most tools fail away from a desk?

Because they were designed for one, and the usual accommodation is a second product with fewer capabilities. Practitioners describe this arrangement as so common that people have stopped noticing its implication: the tool has become two tools, and whichever device somebody is holding is frequently not the one that does what they need at that moment. Practitioners in unrelated professions arrive at the same conclusion independently, which suggests it follows from how people actually work rather than from any particular field. It is one of the assumptions worth testing once rather than carrying, since the cost of testing is a minute and the cost of the assumption is measured in months.

### What does responsive layout actually mean in practice?

That the arrangement of things on a screen changes with the size of the screen while the capabilities remain identical. Practitioners describe looking deliberately for missing features on smaller devices and finding none, and describe the distinction as being between arrangement and capability. Nothing is withheld and nothing is simplified into a lesser version. Anybody uncertain can establish the answer on their own device faster than they can reason about it. The difference is invisible until somebody is standing somewhere without a desk, which is when the arrangement is actually tested. Deciding deliberately rather than by habit is what turns a second device from a source of frustration into something genuinely useful.

### How does the layout change on a tablet?

Depending on orientation. In landscape, the side panel and the content sit beside each other, which practitioners describe as the desktop arrangement at a smaller size. In portrait, the layout stacks so that content occupies the full width. Practitioners describe rotating the device as a deliberate mode switch, used repeatedly during a working session. Practitioners in unrelated professions arrive at the same conclusion independently, which suggests it follows from how people actually work rather than from any particular field. It is one of the assumptions worth testing once rather than carrying, since the cost of testing is a minute and the cost of the assumption is measured in months.

### Is rotation a better control than a button?

Practitioners think so and describe it as more natural. Turning a physical object to change what it shows is a gesture people already have, and one practitioner describes doing it around a dozen times an afternoon without any conscious decision. The equivalent control exists and is used less, because the physical action requires no attention. Anybody uncertain can establish the answer on their own device faster than they can reason about it. The difference is invisible until somebody is standing somewhere without a desk, which is when the arrangement is actually tested. Deciding deliberately rather than by habit is what turns a second device from a source of frustration into something genuinely useful.

### How does the layout change on a phone?

To a single column with the panel hidden by default and available on demand. Practitioners describe this as the only sensible arrangement at that size, since content needs the full width, and describe using the panel infrequently because somebody reaching for a phone usually already knows what they want rather than orienting themselves. Practitioners in unrelated professions arrive at the same conclusion independently, which suggests it follows from how people actually work rather than from any particular field. It is one of the assumptions worth testing once rather than carrying, since the cost of testing is a minute and the cost of the assumption is measured in months.

### What is the single most useful thing in this discussion?

That capability is uniform and suitability is a judgement. Practitioners describe every misconception either of them held as arising from confusing the two: one believed the software was failing when he was attempting an unsuitable activity, and the other believed a device was limited when he had never tested it. Separating the two questions resolves nearly everything else. Both practitioners describe having arrived at that distinction only after a period of unnecessary frustration, one lasting a month and one lasting a year, and both describe the frustration as entirely self-inflicted and entirely ordinary. Anybody adopting a second device should expect to hold at least one belief about it that turns out not to be true.
