The email from the reviewer arrived on a Tuesday, polite and brief, and it sat unread in an inbox for three days because Tuesdays in this house are not for email. Tuesdays are for the library run that turns into a science discussion in the car park, for the maths lesson that works only if it happens before lunch, for the youngest child who has decided that copywork is an insult and the oldest child who has decided that everything would be easier if she could just read ahead and be done. When the parent in this composite scenario — a mother teaching three children at home, ages seven, ten, and thirteen — finally opened the message on Friday evening, the date inside it stopped her mid-sentence to her partner. The portfolio review was in eleven days. Not in a month. Not at the end of term in some vague, manageable future. Eleven days, with a request to bring attendance records, a log of instructional hours, samples of work across subjects for each child, a reading list, and evidence of progress since the last review.

Nothing about that request was unreasonable. She had taught the work. The children had done the work. The books had been read, argued over, left face-down on the sofa, renewed at the library, and finally returned with a fine she paid without resentment because the extra week had been worth it. The nature walks had happened. The museum trip had happened, twice, the second time because the first visit raised more questions than it answered. The baking that was really fractions, the letter to a grandparent that was really composition, the long project on local birds that started with a feeder outside the kitchen window and ended with a hand-drawn field guide — all of it had happened, in real rooms, on real days, with real learning attached. The problem on that Friday night was not that there was nothing to show. The problem was that showing it meant finding it.

She started, as most parents in this position start, with her phone. The camera roll held the proof, or some of it: a photograph of the ten-year-old's volcano model, taken at an angle that cut off the labels; a photograph of a whiteboard covered in workings for a maths problem, half erased; forty-one photographs from the museum, of which perhaps six showed a child learning anything and the rest showed ceilings, siblings pulling faces, and one very clear image of a gift-shop penguin. Mixed between the school photographs were grocery lists photographed so she would not forget them, a plumber's business card, screenshots of recipes, and a birthday party. There was no order to it except the order in which life had happened, and life, in a house with three children, does not happen in subject categories.

From the phone she moved to the binders, because binders feel like the serious answer. There were three, one per child in theory, bought in a burst of September optimism in three colours. In practice the blue binder held the oldest child's history essays and also, for reasons nobody could reconstruct, the youngest child's phonics sheets. The green binder held attendance sheets printed from a template, filled in faithfully through October and then sporadically, then not at all, with gaps that she knew had been school days — co-op days, field-trip days, days when the work was real but the form was in another room. The third binder held curriculum receipts, a reading list from last year, and a single, excellent map drawn by the ten-year-old that she had been meaning to keep safe and had therefore put somewhere she could not now find on purpose.

Then came the laptop, and Google Drive, and the planner, and the text messages she had sent to herself because sending yourself a text is the filing system of a person who is busy teaching while she files. Drive held folders named by year, by child, by subject, and by at least two naming systems she had abandoned mid-year when they stopped making sense. Inside them were curriculum PDFs she had paid for and downloaded twice because she could not find the first download, scans of work samples made on a day when the scanner app was working, documents titled "final" and "final real" and "portfolio version" that differed from each other in ways she could not tell at midnight. The planner — a good planner, a planner other homeschool parents recommend — held the daily truth for about nine weeks: what was planned, what was actually done, the little notes in margins that are often the most honest record of a homeschool year. After those nine weeks the planner held shopping lists and the occasional date, because writing up the day every day had begun to feel like a second job performed after the first job, for an audience of no one, and she had quietly stopped.

By eleven o'clock the kitchen table was covered. Printouts, binders open, phone face-up with its battery draining, laptop balanced on a stack of library books, sticky notes marking pages she might use. Her partner made tea and said the thing that was both true and unhelpful: it is all here somewhere. It was. That was precisely the problem. The record of a rich, busy, largely successful homeschool year existed in that house in at least six places, in at least four formats, under at least three naming habits, and none of those places talked to any of the others. Reconstructing it for a reviewer was not a matter of opening a folder. It was archaeology, conducted at night, against a deadline, by the only person who held the map in her head — and the map in her head was tired.

This scenario is a composite, drawn from the recurring shape of stories homeschool parents tell each other in community forums and support groups, but every detail in it will be familiar to families who teach at home. The scramble before a portfolio review. The attendance log rebuilt in May from memory, from a wall calendar, from library receipts and text timestamps, by a parent who knows the days happened and hates that she is reconstructing rather than simply showing. The photographs of projects that mean everything in the week they are taken and almost nothing three months later because nobody wrote down what the project was, what the child was trying to do, or what she said about it that made her parent stop and fetch the phone in the first place. The planning system, bought with hope, abandoned by October not because the parent is disorganised but because the system demanded a daily clerical shift on top of a full teaching day. The learning that never made it into any record at all because it did not fit the form: the walk that became a lesson in identifying trees, the baking that became a lesson in scaling quantities, the documentary watched together that became a week of questions. If it did not have a subject box and an assignment line, many systems simply had no place to put it, so it lived only in memory — and memory, at a portfolio review, is not evidence.

It does not have to work that way, and the fix is not a better binder, a stricter planner habit, or a promise to be more consistent next September. The fix is to change where the record lives: one place, private, offline, organised around how a family actually learns rather than how a form expects it to, and forgiving enough that a record takes a minute at the kitchen table rather than a clerical session at a desk. None of that makes a parent a better teacher. Teaching is the walk, the book, the patience, the second explanation pitched differently from the first. What a good record system does is humbler and, in the week before a review, more valuable: it makes the teaching visible without making the parent perform extra work to prove it happened. ## The record problem is not teaching — it is scattering

Ask a room of homeschool parents what the hardest part of the work is and few will say the teaching. Teaching a child to read is demanding, teaching fractions to a reluctant ten-year-old is demanding, but those demands are the chosen ones, the ones parents signed up for and mostly, on good days and even on ordinary days, meet. The demand that wears people down is the shadow work around the teaching: the logging, filing, photographing, scanning, naming, backing up and, above all, retrieving. It is the work that produces no learning for any child, takes time a teaching parent does not have in surplus, and becomes visible only when it has not been done — at a review, at the end of a reporting period, when a grandparent asks what the children have been studying, or when a parent tries to plan next term from what actually happened this term and discovers she cannot reconstruct it.

Scattering is the default state of a homeschool record, and it helps to understand why, because the causes are structural rather than personal. The first cause is that learning happens everywhere and records are made where learning happens. A photograph is taken in the garden because the project is in the garden. A note is made in a text message because the phone is in a pocket and the notebook is upstairs. A worksheet is finished at the kitchen table and stays at the kitchen table, then migrates to a pile, then to a binder if it is lucky. A curriculum file is downloaded on the laptop because that is where the payment was made, while the book list lives in a library app, the attendance mark lives on a wall calendar, and the plan lives in a planner that lives, increasingly, in a drawer. Each individual choice is sensible at the moment it is made. The system they add up to is not a system at all. It is a trail, and trails are for following immediately, not for reconstructing under deadline pressure months later.

The second cause is that each tool a family adopts solves one part of the record and silently refuses the rest. A camera roll is superb at capturing and hopeless at explaining; it stores the image and the date and almost nothing about what the image proves. A cloud drive is good at storing files and indifferent to their meaning; it will hold a PDF forever and never tell you which child used it, in which week, or whether it worked. A paper planner is good at intention and weak at evidence; it records what was meant to happen with more fidelity than what did. A binder is good at holding finished, flat, hole-punchable work and has no answer at all for the audio of a seven-year-old reading aloud in September — the single most persuasive evidence of progress a parent of a young reader owns — or for the video of a science demonstration, or for the transcript of a discussion. So families layer tools, one per format, and each layer adds a place to search, a naming habit to remember, and an account to be signed into on the night it matters.

The third cause is the multi-child problem, which parents of one child underestimate and parents of three feel daily. Work gets mixed up. Not through carelessness — through physics. Three children share a table, a printer, a camera roll, a car, and a parent. The photograph of handwriting practice does not say whose handwriting. The undated essay in the binder could belong to either of the older two. The reading log kept as a single family list is charming in September and useless in May, when a reviewer wants to see what each child read and the list has become an undifferentiated river of titles, some read aloud to everyone, some read alone by one child, some started and abandoned, which is also worth recording and impossible to show. Any record system for a multi-child home has to make the child part of the record at the moment the record is made, with almost no extra effort, or it will not survive contact with a real school day.

The fourth cause is time, and specifically the way record-keeping competes with the rest of a teaching day. Community discussions among homeschool parents return again and again to the same confession: the planning and logging system was abandoned by October. Not the teaching — the system. A daily log that requires opening a laptop, signing in, choosing the right folder or notebook, filling a form with subject, assignment, duration and notes, and repeating that for each child, is asking for perhaps twenty minutes of clerical work at the exact moment of day when the parent is most depleted. Twenty minutes is not much. Twenty minutes every day, forever, for a record nobody may ever ask to see, is a great deal, and human beings ration accordingly. The systems that survive a homeschool year are the ones that ask for a minute, in the room where the learning happened, on the device already in hand — or they are the ones with so little friction that a week's records can be caught up on a Sunday without self-deception. Friction, not motivation, is what kills homeschool records, and any honest recommendation has to start there.

The fifth cause is the one parents mention most quietly: privacy. A homeschool record is, among other things, a detailed archive of children. Photographs of children. Samples of children's handwriting, children's voices reading, children's early essays with their early spelling. Notes a parent makes about a child's struggles — the honest notes, the ones that say reading is still hard, or that maths anxiety arrived in November, or that a child is being assessed, or that a family circumstance changed and school bent around it. Those notes are exactly what make a record useful for planning and persuasive at review, and they are exactly what many parents do not want sitting in a cloud account, synced to services they do not control, under terms they have never read, attached to an account that could be locked, breached, or simply changed by a provider's policy decision. The worry is rarely dramatic. It is a background unease that produces a practical behaviour: parents under-record. They keep the samples but not the honest notes. They keep the photographs on the phone but do not organise them. The record thins out precisely where it should be most candid, because the candid parts feel exposed. A record system for children has to answer that unease with architecture, not reassurance — with local storage, no uploads, and encryption a parent controls — or the best parts of the record will keep being written nowhere.

Put those five causes together and the Friday-night scramble stops looking like a personal failing and starts looking like the predictable output of a scattered design. Photographs without context. Logs reconstructed from memory. Forms that learning does not fit. Systems abandoned by October. Children's work mixed together and, at the same time, held in accounts their parents do not fully trust. The remedy has to address all five at once: one place instead of six, capture that carries its context with it, a structure based on the child and the week rather than the subject form, an entry habit small enough to survive tired days, and custody that keeps a child's archive in the family's hands. That is a demanding list. It is also, not coincidentally, a precise description of what a local-first vault is for.

## What a homeschool record actually has to hold

Before choosing any tool, it is worth listing what a homeschool record genuinely has to hold, because most record-keeping failures are really definition failures: a family keeps some of these things beautifully and discovers, too late, that the review, the next term's plan, or their own memory needed the others. The list is longer than the official requirements of any state or district, and deliberately so. The official record is the floor. The useful record — the one that helps a parent teach better next month and answer questions next year — is broader, messier, and much more human.

First, the compliance layer, because it is non-negotiable where it applies and because it is the layer most often reconstructed in a panic. Depending on where a family lives, this can include attendance records showing the days instruction took place, a count or log of instructional hours, sometimes broken down in ways that matter locally, a record of subjects covered, periodic work samples, assessment or evaluation results, and a reading list. The details vary enormously and this article is not legal advice for any jurisdiction; every family must check its own requirements. But the shape is consistent: dates, hours, subjects, samples, books. Notice what that shape demands of a record system. It demands that ordinary days leave a dated trace, that hours can be totalled without re-reading a year of prose, that samples can be produced per child and per subject, and that books read can be listed per child. A system that holds lovely narrative notes but cannot answer "show me this child's attendance and hours" will fail at review no matter how lovely the notes are. Equally, a system that holds only attendance ticks and hours — the bare compliance skeleton — will fail the parent, because it says nothing about what to teach next.

Second, the work itself: samples that show progress, which means samples that are dated, attributed, and comparable. One essay proves a child wrote an essay. An essay from September beside an essay from February, same child, similar task, proves growth, and growth is what reviewers are actually looking for and what parents most want to see. Work samples in a modern homeschool include far more than paper: photographs of builds, models, artwork and experiments; scans or photographs of written work; audio of reading, recitation, music practice and language work; video of demonstrations, presentations and physical projects; and files — the story typed, the presentation built, the code written. Each format arrives through a different door and, in a scattered system, stays behind that door. In one vault, an attachment is indexed and searchable alongside the note that explains it, so the sample and its context travel together. That last point matters more than it first appears. A photograph of a bridge made of spaghetti is a curiosity. A photograph attached to an entry that says what the challenge was, what failed first, what the child changed, and what she said when it held weight, is evidence of engineering thinking. The difference is two sentences, written on the day, and the whole problem is arranging your tools so those two sentences are easy to write and impossible to lose.

Third, the plan and its honest shadow, what actually happened. Homeschool planning is famously a genre of optimistic fiction. The plan says the science unit takes two weeks; it takes four, because it was interesting. The plan says a book a week; one book takes three because the child fell in love with it and another takes two days because she did not. A record that keeps only the plan teaches a parent nothing and misleads a reviewer politely. A record that keeps only outcomes makes next term's planning a fresh guess. The valuable record holds both, close together: this is what we intended this week, this is what we did, this is why they differ. That comparison, accumulated over a year, is how a parent learns her own family's real pace — how long a writing project actually takes, which subjects expand, which curricula earn their shelf space — and it is the first thing sacrificed when logging feels like a second job, because it requires honesty in writing at the end of a long day. Keep the entry small and private, and honesty gets easier. Privacy is not only about keeping children's data away from strangers. It is about a parent being able to write "this curriculum is not working and I am changing it in January" without feeling she is publishing an admission.

Fourth, the learning that does not fit forms, which in many homes is a large share of the best learning. Nature walks where identification, observation, drawing and patient looking are the curriculum. Baking, where scaling a recipe is arithmetic, following it is reading comprehension, and timing it is applied common sense. Museum and gallery trips, farm visits, historical sites, a grandparent's stories recorded at a kitchen table, a documentary that reorganises a child's picture of the world, a long conversation in the car that becomes the real history lesson of the week. Rigid systems built on subject-and-assignment forms have no shelf for these, so they go unrecorded, and then a parent at review finds herself describing a year that looks thinner on paper than it was in life — because the paper only admitted the learning that arrived in a workbook. A record system for homeschooling needs free-form entries with labels, not forms with compulsory fields: an entry can be tagged as a field trip, as nature study, as life skills, as a project, and cross-labelled with the subjects it genuinely touched, without pretending it was an assignment. VaultBook's entries, labels, wiki pages and notes are built that way — the structure is the label you choose and the links you make, not a template you must satisfy — and that looseness is not a lack of rigour. It is what allows the record to match the education.

Fifth, the resources layer: curriculum files, book lists, and the practical library of a teaching home. Curriculum PDFs bought and downloaded, sample schedules, answer keys, printable maps and timelines, lists of books owned, borrowed, requested and recommended, links replaced here by local notes because a vault makes no network requests, ideas for next term collected in the middle of this one. In scattered homes this layer lives across devices and downloads folders, and parents rebuy or re-download what they already own, lose the list of what to read next, and cannot answer the simple question of what they used for a subject last time and whether it worked. A single vault with a file and folder analyser, attachment indexing and full search turns that layer into something queryable: which resources do we hold for this child's next maths stage, what did we think of the last one, where is the PDF. The Reader, covered in detail later in this article, matters here too, because a book list that connects to actually reading, annotating and returning to texts is worth more than a list that is only a list.

Sixth, the child layer: observations, milestones and the sensitive notes. First chapter book finished. Reading fluency turning a corner in a specific month, audible if you compare two recordings. A concept suddenly landing after weeks of circling it. A friendship difficulty at co-op affecting Mondays. A health appointment, an assessment, a change at home that explains a change at the desk. This is the layer that makes a record a family's own history rather than an inspector's file, and it is the layer with the strongest claim to encryption and local custody. In VaultBook, encryption is per entry: a parent can encrypt the sensitive entries — the assessment notes, the honest reflections, the records she keeps because they matter and not because anyone should browse them — with AES-256-GCM, while leaving ordinary logs and lists open for quick daily use. That granularity fits how families actually feel about their records. Not everything is secret. Some things are private. A good system lets a parent draw that line entry by entry instead of forcing a choice between locking everything away behind friction or leaving everything exposed by default.

Seventh, the forward layer: what comes next. Expiring library loans and their dates, co-op terms, review deadlines, the book to request before the current one ends, the project to finish before a grandparent visits, the curriculum decision to make before a sale ends. Homeschool parents hold an unusual number of small deadlines in their heads, and the ones that slip are rarely the big ones — they are the loan that becomes a fine, the review date that arrives eleven days after an unread email. A record system that only looks backwards is half a system. VaultBook's built-in calendar and its Recent, Due and Expiring sidebar exist for exactly this forward layer, and the attendance section below uses them in earnest: deadlines and due items surface alongside the record instead of living in a separate app the parent must remember to check.

Hold those seven layers together — compliance, work, plan and reality, informal learning, resources, child observations, and what comes next — and the shape of the tool a homeschool family needs comes into focus. It must accept paper, photo, audio, video and file with equal ease. It must attribute everything to a child with a label, not a filing expedition. It must search across all of it, including inside scanned and photographed text, because a record you cannot search is a record you will re-read at midnight before a review. It must keep versions, because logs grow. It must work in a kitchen with poor signal, in a car, at a museum, entirely offline, because that is where the learning is. And it must keep the whole archive in the family's custody, encrypted where it counts, in one portable file rather than spread across accounts. That is a high bar, and no tool clears every part of it equally — the honest comparisons later in this article will say where the big, familiar apps are stronger. But the bar itself is not optional. It is simply what the record is.

## Setting up one vault per family, one entry per child-week

Setup is where good intentions go to be tested, so the setup described here is deliberately small. It can be done in one sitting, it asks for no reorganisation of past years, and it is designed around a single decision that does most of the work: one vault for the family, structured by child and by week, with labels doing the organising that folders and binders used to attempt. Families who prefer a vault per child can adapt it, but one family vault has real advantages for a teaching parent — shared days are recorded once, siblings' work can be compared, a field trip that taught all three children needs one entry rather than three copies, and there is exactly one file to keep, back up, and open on the night before a review.

Begin with the vault itself. VaultBook is a single-file HTML application; there is no account to create and no server waiting to receive your children's records, because the application makes zero network requests and works locally. You open it, you create your vault, and the record of your homeschool lives in that local file under your control. Sit with what that changes for a moment, because it changes the setup conversation entirely. There is no importing your life into someone else's system, no folder permissions to configure so a partner can see what a reviewer should not, no sharing settings to audit. There is a file, in your house, on your devices, that you copy when you want a backup and encrypt where you want privacy. For a parent who has spent years scattering records across accounts, that simplicity is not a limitation. It is the point.

Inside the vault, create the skeleton before you record a single lesson. The skeleton has three parts. First, one standing entry per child — call it the child's home entry — holding the basics that otherwise get retyped and re-lost all year: full name as it should appear on official records, date of birth if your reporting needs it, the year or level being studied, the requirements that apply to this child this year, and links onward to that child's weeks. Second, a small set of standing reference entries for the family: one for this year's requirements and deadlines, one for curriculum in use per subject, one for the master book list, one for contacts and arrangements such as co-op and reviewers. Third, the rhythm that will carry the year: a weekly entry per child. That weekly entry is the heart of the whole setup, and it deserves a careful description.

A child-week entry is a single entry, created at the start of the week or caught up at the end of it, titled plainly with the child's name and the week — and then filled, lightly, as the week goes along. It holds the plan at the top, a few lines only. It holds dated notes as days happen: what was done, what was read, what was made, with photographs, scans, audio or files attached at the moment they are created, while context is still in the parent's head and costs two sentences instead of twenty minutes of reconstruction. It holds the honest shadow too — what did not happen, what took longer, what will carry forward — because the entry is private and the habit is small. And it is labelled, consistently, with the child's name, and with subject or activity labels as they genuinely apply. Labels in VaultBook do the work that folders promise and rarely deliver, because one entry can carry several labels at once: a museum trip entry can be labelled for each child who went, and for history, and for art, and as a field trip, and be found under any of them. A folder forces a choice about where a thing lives. A label simply tells the truth about what a thing is, several truths at once, which is what homeschool learning is like.

Families teaching children of very different ages will feel the value of this in the shared activities. The baking afternoon that is fractions for the thirteen-year-old, measuring and counting for the seven-year-old, and a first solo reading of a recipe for the ten-year-old is one event in life. In the weekly structure it can be recorded once, in a shared entry or in the youngest child's week, labelled for all three children and for the subjects each child actually practised, with a sentence per child on what each took from it. At review time, searching or filtering by a child's label gathers that child's year — including the shared learning that a per-child binder would have forced into one child's pages and hidden from the others. The mixing-up problem does not disappear because a parent tries harder. It disappears because attribution is a label applied in seconds at the moment of recording, and because search can reassemble any one child's record on demand.

Now the daily habit, because setup without a survivable habit is just September optimism in a new tool. The rule is: record in the room, in the minute, at the lowest quality that is still true. A photograph attached to this week's entry with one sentence beats a polished write-up planned for the evening and never written. An audio note of a child reading, captured because the entry was open, beats a termly assessment nobody schedules. VaultBook's full search, its attachment indexing and its OCR — which reads text inside scanned pages and photographed work using tesseract — forgive rough capture, because what matters is that the material and its context are in the vault and findable, not that they are beautifully presented. Presentation is a review-week task, and it is a pleasant one when the raw material is gathered; it is a nightmare when the raw material must first be hunted across six places. Parents who have abandoned planners by October should notice the difference in what is being asked. A planner asks for a composed daily account, in handwriting, in a book that is somewhere specific. A weekly vault entry asks for fragments, in the moment, on whatever device is in hand, in any order, because search and labels will impose the order later. Fragments are survivable. Composed accounts, on tired days, are not.

Two more setup choices pay for themselves all year. The first is dynamic tabs and related notes, which sound like small interface features and behave like a teaching assistant's memory. Keep tabs open for the current week of each child and for the standing requirements entry, and the vault opens each day already arranged around the work. Related notes surface connections a tired parent would miss — last term's entry on the same topic, the earlier work sample that makes today's sample a progress story, the curriculum note from when this approach was chosen. At review time those connections are gold: progress is a comparison, and a system that proposes the comparison saves the midnight archaeology entirely. The second choice is to use version history deliberately for the few entries that live all year. The attendance log, the reading log, the requirements entry — these grow and change, and version history means an edit never destroys what the entry said before. If a parent corrects an attendance record in May, the earlier version still exists, and the correction is honest bookkeeping rather than a silent rewrite. Reviewers, like auditors everywhere, trust records that show their workings more than records that appear, immaculate and unrevisable, at the deadline.

Finally, decide the encryption line at setup, while you are calm, rather than entry by entry under stress. A workable family rule: encrypt entries that contain assessments, health-related notes, honest reflections on a child's difficulties, and any official correspondence you store; leave plans, logs, book lists and work samples unencrypted for frictionless daily use. Because VaultBook encrypts per entry with AES-256-GCM and a PBKDF2-derived key via Web Crypto, that rule is enforceable in the tool itself, not just intended. And decide the backup habit at the same sitting: one file is wonderfully portable — it can be copied to a drive, kept with family documents, handed to a partner — and portability only helps if the copy actually happens, so tie it to something that already happens, the end of the month, the end of a term. One vault, copied monthly, encrypted where it counts, beats six accounts, none of them fully trusted and none of them complete, every single time.

## Attendance, hours and the calendar that does not need a signal

Attendance is the least interesting record a homeschool parent keeps and the one most likely to be demanded, in full, on a date certain. That combination — dull, compulsory, and reconstructed in a panic by so many families every spring — makes it the perfect test of a record system. If attendance is easy, the system is working. If attendance requires a May evening with a wall calendar, a phone full of photographs used as date evidence, library receipts and a partner saying "that was the week your sister visited, so we must have done school on the Thursday," the system has failed at its simplest job, no matter how beautiful its other features are.

Start with why attendance goes wrong in otherwise well-run homes. It goes wrong because a school day at home does not announce itself the way a school day in a building does. There is no register taken by someone else, no bus, no uniform, no bell. There are days that are unmistakably school: lessons at the table, work in books, a plan followed. There are days that are school in every way that matters and look like something else: the museum day, the co-op day, the day of the long walk and the long conversation, the day a project consumed the house. There are half days bent around appointments, days when one child is ill and the others learn independently, days in the school holidays of local schools when this family is working because it takes its breaks at other times, and days in term time when this family is not working because its year is arranged differently. A parent knows, on the day, which of these it was. Three months later, faced with a blank attendance grid, she is doing inference, not record-keeping, and she knows it, and the inference feels uncomfortably like invention even when it is accurate. The emotional core of the attendance problem is not laziness. It is that honest people dislike certifying what they can no longer verify, and a scattered record forces them to do exactly that.

The remedy is to make the attendance mark a by-product of records already being made, and then to let a calendar do the remembering. In the setup described above, the child-week entry already creates a dated trace of learning: if there is a weekly entry with dated notes, work attached and reading logged, the week was a school week, and the days can be marked from evidence rather than memory. On top of that trace, keep one standing attendance entry per child per term — plain, factual, updated in seconds at the end of a week while the week is still known. Days present, days of instruction, hours where hours are required, with a word where a day needs one: co-op, field trip, independent work, sick sibling, assessment morning. That word matters. A grid of ticks invites a reviewer's questions; a grid of ticks with brief, contemporaneous reasons answers them before they are asked, and it protects the parent too, because the unusual days are explained at the time rather than defended later.

VaultBook's built-in calendar is where this becomes a habit rather than a chore. School days, co-op sessions, review deadlines, library due dates, term boundaries and the family's own holidays can live on the same calendar that sits inside the vault with the records themselves, rather than in a separate calendar account that knows nothing about the entries it should be pointing to. And because VaultBook works entirely offline — it is a local, single-file application making zero network requests — the calendar does not need a signal to be truthful. This sounds like a small thing until you live the homeschool week as it is actually lived: in cars between activities, in museum halls with thick walls, in rural homes where connectivity is a suggestion, on trips where the learning is the point and the connection is absent. A record system that sulks without a network is a record system that misses exactly the days most worth recording, the out-of-the-house days, the field days, the days that later need a word of explanation on an attendance log. Offline-first is not a technical preference here. It is the difference between a record made on the day, in the place, and a record reconstructed at home from memory — the very failure this whole setup exists to end.

Hours deserve a separate word, because families required to log instructional hours often suffer more from the arithmetic than from the teaching. Hours are scattered by nature: a morning of lessons, an afternoon project, twenty minutes of reading before bed that absolutely counts and absolutely gets forgotten, a documentary in the evening, practice on an instrument, the long baking session that was three subjects wearing an apron. Reconstructing hours in May is hopeless in detail and stressful in aggregate. The survivable method is rough honesty at short intervals: note hours in the weekly entry, in round numbers, at the end of each week, while the shape of the week is still felt rather than researched. A parent knows whether this was a full week, a short week, a week of deep project work with fewer but longer sessions. Weekly approximations recorded contemporaneously are far more truthful than daily precision invented retrospectively, and any parent who has done both knows it. The weekly rhythm also produces totals almost for free: a term of weekly entries can be searched, read through and totalled in an hour at review time, with the underlying dated evidence sitting in the same entries if anyone asks how a number was reached. Compare that with the alternative most families describe — a spreadsheet started in September, a paper log abandoned in November, and a final number assembled from both plus memory — and the advantage is not elegance. It is that the number can be shown, step by step, to be grounded in a record made at the time.

The forward-looking half of the calendar matters as much as the record-keeping half. The Recent, Due and Expiring sidebar in VaultBook surfaces what is coming due and what is about to expire alongside the record itself, and for a homeschool parent that sidebar quietly prevents the small disasters: the library book that becomes a fine and then a blocked card on the day twenty books are needed; the review deadline that arrives by unread email; the co-op registration window that closes; the borrowed curriculum that must go back to its owner. Parents hold these dates in their heads because their systems scatter them — a due date in a library app, a deadline in an email, a term date in a planner — and heads, in busy houses, drop things. Putting the dates in the vault, where they surface as due and expiring items next to the entries they relate to, moves them from memory to system. It also closes the loop with attendance: a term planned on the calendar, recorded in weekly entries, and checked against due dates in the sidebar is a term whose attendance record writes most of itself. The parent is no longer keeping three systems — a plan, a log and a memory — and reconciling them under pressure. She is keeping one record, in one place, that looks both ways in time.

There is an honesty point to make here about what VaultBook does not do for attendance. It does not report to any authority for you, it does not know your jurisdiction's rules, and it will not warn you that your particular state, province or district counts hours in a specific way or wants a particular form signed by a particular person. Families must still learn their own requirements and still produce whatever official document is demanded, in whatever format is demanded. What the vault changes is the position you produce that document from: from evidence, calmly, with dates and samples attached, instead of from reconstruction, at midnight, with a draining phone. Nor is a digital log automatically superior to paper for every family — a parent who genuinely loves a paper planner, keeps it daily, and files it safely has a working system and should keep it. The argument in this article is for the far larger group whose paper and cloud systems have quietly stopped working by October, whose attendance is a May reconstruction, and who need the record to live where the photographs, the audio, the PDFs and the honest notes already need to live: together, offline, searchable, and theirs.

## Reading logs, book lists and the Reader

If attendance is the record parents keep for other people, the reading log is the record they keep for themselves — and it is, year after year, the one they most regret losing. Ask a homeschool parent what her children read two years ago and she will give you the beloved titles instantly and the rest in a haze. The series that carried a reluctant reader through a winter. The non-fiction detour into volcanoes, or codes, or queens, that lasted a month and redirected a child's interests for good. The read-aloud that the whole family still quotes. The book that was too hard in September and easy by spring, which is one of the purest measures of progress a home can produce. These are not decorative memories. They are planning data — what to seek more of, what to avoid repeating, when a child is ready for the next step — and they are review gold, because a reading list shows breadth, appetite and growth in a single page. Yet reading logs die faster than almost any other homeschool record, and the reasons are worth naming, because the design that saves them follows directly from the reasons.

Reading logs die, first, because reading happens in the wrong places for paperwork: in bed, in cars, on sofas, in waiting rooms, under blankets with a torch in defiance of lights-out. The book is finished wherever the child happens to be, and the log is wherever it happens to be, and the two rarely meet. They die, second, because a family reads in several modes at once and a simple list flattens them: books a child reads alone, books read aloud to everyone, books read aloud to one child, audiobooks in the car that the whole family absorbs, books started and abandoned — which are data too, about taste and level — and books re-read, sometimes five times, which is also data and which a tidy log tends to hide. They die, third, because the book itself keeps leaving the house: library books go back, borrowed books go home, and with them goes the physical reminder of what was read, leaving a parent trying to recall in May the title of a book that left in November. And they die, fourth, because curriculum book lists and pleasure reading live in different places — one in the curriculum file, one in the library app, one in a parent's memory of what to request next — so no single list ever shows the whole reading life of a child.

The vault approach treats the reading log as part of the weekly rhythm rather than as a separate clerical project. In practice that means three small habits. First, log the book where the week is logged: a line in the child-week entry when a book is finished, started, abandoned or re-read, with a sentence — from the child where possible — about what it was like. A sentence is not a burden, and over a year those sentences become the most reread pages in the whole record, because they hold voices, not just titles. Second, keep one standing master book list per child in the vault, updated from the weekly entries at the end of each term, so the review-ready list is assembled from contemporaneous notes rather than recalled under pressure; version history on that standing entry means each term's list is preserved even as the list grows, so last year's reading is never overwritten by this year's tidying. Third, label reading entries usefully — the child's name, read-aloud or independent, fiction or non-fiction, the subject a non-fiction book served — so that search can answer real questions in seconds: what has she read about the natural world this year, which books did we read together as a family, what did he read alone in the spring term. Full search across the vault, including semantic question-and-answer search, changes the log from a list you scroll into a record you can interrogate, and attachment indexing and OCR mean that even a photographed library receipt or a scanned reading certificate becomes part of the searchable evidence rather than a picture of evidence.

Then there is the difference between logging reading and supporting it, and that is where VaultBook's built-in Reader earns its place in a homeschool home. A book list tells you what a child has read. A reading environment shapes what happens next: keeping texts together, returning to them, reading closely, noticing. For the many homeschool texts that live as files — public-domain classics, curriculum extracts, poems to be studied, a child's own growing collection of written work, reference material gathered for a project — the Reader gives the family a place inside the same vault where those texts are opened and read, rather than scattered across downloads folders and forgotten apps. The practical value is continuity. The poem studied in October is in the vault with the entry about studying it. The classic being read across a term sits alongside the weekly notes on how it is landing, which chapters caused arguments, which passages a child copied out because she loved them. When a reviewer asks about a title on the reading list, the parent is not producing a bare list and a memory; she can open the record around the book — when it was read, by whom, in what mode, what came of it. That surrounding record is what turns a reading log from a claim into a story with evidence, and stories with evidence are what portfolio reviews, at their best, are for.

The Reader and the log also serve the most delicate reading task in a multi-age home: pitching the next book. Every teaching parent knows the narrow bridge between too easy, which bores, and too hard, which discourages, and knows that the bridge moves at a different speed for each child and at an uneven pace across a year. A searchable record of what each child actually read — with the parent's sentences about how it landed, the abandoned titles as well as the triumphs — is the best instrument a parent has for judging that pitch. It beats memory, which remembers the highlights and loses the pattern. It beats a library app's history, which shows borrowing rather than reading and mixes three children's cards and tastes into one stream. And it beats a curriculum's suggested sequence, which knows nothing about this child. The log in the vault knows this child, because the parent wrote it, a line at a time, in the weeks when the reading happened. Add the private analytics sidebar — VaultBook's local view of your own material and activity, kept private inside the vault rather than reported anywhere — and a parent can see the shape of a term's record at a glance: what has been gathered, what is growing, where the gaps are. Used lightly, that kind of overview helps a parent notice, in November rather than May, that one child's non-fiction reading has thinned, or that read-alouds have been crowded out for a fortnight, while there is still time to do something about it.

A last word on privacy belongs in this section too, because reading records are quietly sensitive. A child's reading history reveals her level, her pace, her interests and sometimes her worries; the books a family chooses can reflect its faith, its politics, its circumstances and its hopes. Many parents are content for a library to know what they borrowed — that is the bargain of borrowing — but feel differently about a detailed, years-long, per-child reading profile, with a parent's candid notes attached, sitting in a commercial cloud account, synced, analysed under terms that change, and retrievable by anyone who gains access to that account. In the vault, the reading log is local, makes no network journey, and can be encrypted entry by entry where a parent wants that protection. The list that a reviewer sees is the list the parent chooses to show, exported from a record that remains the family's own. That distinction — between showing a record and surrendering it — runs through every part of the homeschool case for a private offline vault, and it will return in the parts of this article that follow, which take up work samples and photographs, projects and informal learning, and the full, honest comparison with the familiar apps most families start with. The reading log is simply where the distinction is easiest to feel: these are your children's inner lives, taking shape one book at a time, and the record of that deserves to live at home.

## Photos that prove learning — Photo and Video Explorer with EXIF in the homeschool year

A homeschool portfolio review does not fail because learning did not happen. It fails, when it fails, because the evidence of learning is scattered, undated, and impossible to reconstruct under pressure in the week before the reviewer arrives. For a parent teaching three children of different ages at the same kitchen table, that scattering is almost structural. The youngest dictates a story while standing on a chair. The middle child finally understands long division on a whiteboard that will be wiped clean by dinner. The oldest builds a bridge from balsa wood in the garage, tests it until it breaks, rebuilds it stronger, and breaks it again on purpose to record the load. All of that is real education. Almost none of it arrives in a neat folder.

In the year before this family moved their records into VaultBook, the photographic record lived in four places at once. There were phone photos mixed in with grocery lists photographed for convenience, screenshots of recipes, and pictures of a leaking tap sent to a plumber. There were a partner's phone photos that were promised but never transferred. There were short videos buried in a messaging app thread, compressed beyond usefulness. And there were the good intentional photos, taken on a proper camera during a nature walk, sitting on a memory card in a drawer because importing them felt like a separate project. When the parent sat down in spring to assemble evidence, the problem was not a lack of images. The problem was that none of the images could answer the simplest reviewer questions reliably: when was this taken, which child is this work by, what subject does it show, and what happened next?

The Photo and Video Explorer inside VaultBook changes the nature of that problem, not by taking better photographs, but by treating photographs already taken as records with context rather than as pictures in a stream. Because VaultBook is a single-file HTML vault that runs locally with zero network activity, importing a term's worth of images does not mean uploading a family's private life to a photo service, accepting facial recognition grouping, or granting an app permission to scan a camera roll in the background. The parent chooses the files, copies them into the vault deliberately, and works with them offline on the family computer. That deliberateness matters for homeschooling families, many of whom chose home education partly to keep tighter control over their children's digital footprint. A system that requires a child's face and daily work to be processed on a distant server in order to be organised is, for those families, not a neutral convenience. It is a trade they did not want to make.

Once inside the vault, the Explorer's most unglamorous feature becomes its most valuable: EXIF data. Most phone and camera images carry embedded metadata — the date and time the shutter fired, sometimes the device, sometimes location if location services were enabled, and technical details like orientation. In a consumer photo app that data is mostly invisible, used quietly to sort a timeline. In a portfolio context, that date stamp is provenance. The parent preparing for review can sort the year's science work chronologically and see, without relying on memory, that the pond-water microscope photographs belong to late September, that the leaf-identification walk happened three weeks later after the first frost, and that the snow-crystal photographs the youngest is so proud of were taken in January, not December as everyone had been remembering. That correction is small, but reviewers notice coherence. A portfolio that places work in the right sequence tells a story of progression. A portfolio that guesses at dates undermines confidence in everything else, even when the underlying learning was strong.

Progression is, in fact, the whole argument a portfolio has to make, and photographs show progression in ways a worksheet cannot. Consider handwriting. The youngest child in this family began the year forming letters with intense concentration, tongue out, grip too tight, producing three wobbly lines before tiring. By spring the same child was writing full sentences for a letter to a grandparent. Two photographs of the child's own hand at work, dated months apart by their EXIF timestamps, make that growth visible in seconds. No test score carries the same immediate credibility. The same is true for the middle child's art: early attempts at drawing a horse that look mostly like a table with a head, and then, after a term of slow looking and a library book on animal anatomy, a horse with joints that bend the right way. Or for the oldest child's woodwork, where the first dovetail joint gapes and the fourth sits tight enough to need a mallet tap. The Explorer lets the parent gather those sequences deliberately — filtering by date range, grouping by the note or entry each image is attached to, and viewing video alongside stills rather than in a separate app — so that progression can be shown rather than merely asserted in a written summary.

Video deserves its own honest accounting, because it is both the richest evidence and the easiest to mismanage. A ninety-second clip of a child explaining, in their own words, why the moon changes shape teaches a reviewer more about understanding than a completed diagram with the phases labelled correctly, because the explanation reveals the reasoning, including the moment the child catches their own mistake and corrects it. VaultBook's inline audio and video players mean those clips can be attached to the relevant learning entry and played in place during preparation, without opening another application or hunting through a downloads folder for a file named with a string of numbers. The parent can watch the clip where it belongs, next to the written observation from that day, and decide whether it earns a place in the review set or is better kept as a family record only. That distinction — between the full archive and the curated portfolio drawn from it — is one homeschooling parents return to again and again in community discussions. The anxiety is rarely about documenting too little. It is about being unable to select calmly from too much, late at night, with a deadline approaching.

There are limits here that should be stated plainly. The Photo and Video Explorer does not improve a blurred photograph, does not invent metadata that a device never recorded, and does not automatically know which child is in a picture or what subject a picture demonstrates. If location data was disabled on the phone — as it is in this family, deliberately — then EXIF will not supply a place, and the parent must add that context in the accompanying entry while memory is fresh. If images are imported in a single large batch at the end of the year, the organisational work is still real work: attaching, captioning, and linking the keepers to entries takes evenings, not minutes. What the vault removes is not effort but chaos and exposure. The images stay in one encrypted, portable file rather than spread across services; the dates embedded in the files themselves do the heavy chronological lifting; and search across the vault means a half-remembered picture — the one of the middle child holding a frog — can be found through the entry it was attached to, rather than by scrolling a camera roll backwards through thousands of unrelated images.

The workflow that emerges over a year, once the parent stops treating photographs as an afterthought, is modest and repeatable. Once a week, usually on a Sunday evening, the week's intentional learning photos are copied from phone and camera into the vault and attached to that week's entries, while the context is still alive: who made this, what surprised them, what they want to try next. Casual duplicates and near-identical burst shots are left out entirely, which keeps the single file leaner and the future selection task smaller. Once a term, the parent uses the Explorer to walk the date range and check for orphan images — pictures imported but not yet attached to anything — and either links them or deletes them. By the time portfolio season arrives, there is no excavation project. There is a chronological, captioned visual record from which a reviewer set can be chosen in an afternoon, and behind that set, if a reviewer asks a follow-up question, sits the fuller archive that can answer it on the spot.

For three children learning at different levels in the same house, that shared visual archive also does something a set of separate binders never did well. It shows the life of the household as a learning environment: the oldest reading aloud to the youngest in a photograph attached to both children's entries, the middle child teaching the youngest a card trick that is really a lesson in probability, all three around a map on the floor planning a field trip. State requirements are usually framed per child, and the portfolio must ultimately be presented per child, but the education itself is not separate in that way, and a record that preserves the overlap is truer to what actually happened. When the reviewer asks, as good reviewers do, how the parent differentiates instruction across ages, those shared images, properly dated and captioned, are evidence of differentiation in practice — the same activity, entered at three levels — rather than a claim in a planning document that was written in August and never quite matched reality.


## Voices of the year — recording narration, recitation and music practice, transcribed locally

If photographs are the most visible evidence in a homeschool portfolio, voices are the most revealing, and the most often lost. A child's narration — retelling a story, a historical event, or a science observation in their own words immediately after encountering it — is one of the oldest assessment tools in home education, borrowed from the Charlotte Mason tradition and used far beyond it. It shows comprehension, vocabulary, sequencing, and confidence in a single unscripted minute. It also, traditionally, evaporates. The parent listens, nods, perhaps scribbles a sentence in a planner, and the actual voice, with its pauses and self-corrections and sudden enthusiasms, is gone. Multiply that loss by three children narrating different books at different levels across a school year, and a family ends up with a portfolio full of finished written work and almost nothing that shows how the children think aloud, which is where much of their learning actually lives.

Before VaultBook, this family tried to keep audio the way most families try: voice memos on a phone. The recordings worked, in the narrow sense that files were created. Everything after creation failed. Voice memo apps name files by date or by location, not by child or book. Recordings sat unlabelled until the parent could no longer remember whether a particular four-minute file was the oldest child narrating a chapter on the Roman roads or the middle child retelling a myth. Sharing a recording to the computer required a cable, a cloud link, or messaging it to oneself. Transcription, which would have made the narrations searchable and quotable in the written portfolio summary, meant either typing them out by hand — an hour of typing for ten minutes of a young child's halting speech — or uploading a child's voice to a transcription service, which the parent was unwilling to do. So the memos accumulated, unheard and unlabelled, and were quietly excluded from the portfolio altogether. The evidence existed and was unusable, which is functionally the same as it not existing.

VaultBook's combination of tools changes that equation at three points: capture, transcription, and trimming, all of it local. Recordings can be attached to the entry for the lesson they belong to, so context is established at the moment of capture rather than reconstructed months later: this narration belongs to this child, this book, this chapter, this date. The MP3 cutter and joiner, which sound like modest utilities, solve the practical problem that real recordings are messy. A narration file usually contains the parent's prompt at the start, a sibling interruption in the middle, a long silence while the child thinks, and then the best ninety seconds the child has produced all term. Cutting is not vanity editing; it is how a parent prepares a reviewer-ready excerpt without falsifying the record, because the original remains in the vault's version history while the trimmed excerpt is what gets presented. Joining matters in the opposite direction: a poem recited in two sittings, or a piano piece recorded movement by movement across a practice week, can be assembled into the single continuous performance the portfolio needs.

The decisive capability, though, is local Whisper transcription. VaultBook transcribes audio on the device itself, with no network involved, which means a child's voice never leaves the family computer in order to become text. For this family that single fact is the difference between using transcription and refusing it. Once a narration is transcribed inside the vault, it becomes part of the searchable record. The parent can search across a year of narrations for a word, a character, a concept — every time the middle child mentioned Athena, every time the oldest used the word erosion before being formally taught it — and can use the vault's semantic question-and-answer search to ask, in plain language, what a child said about a topic months ago and be pointed to the entry that contains it. For portfolio preparation this is quietly transformative. The written summary for each child can quote a sentence or two from the child's own narration, accurately, because the transcript is there to check against, instead of paraphrasing from a parent's fond and unreliable memory. Reviewers respond to children's actual language. A summary that says a nine-year-old explained photosynthesis as the plant cooking its dinner using light, and can show the dated transcript behind the quotation, carries a weight that adjectives about enthusiasm never do.

Music practice shows the same pattern in a different register. Two of the three children in this family learn instruments, and music is the subject parents most often describe, in community conversations, as impossible to document honestly. Practice happens in fragments: scales in the morning, a difficult passage repeated twenty times, a duet attempted with a parent and abandoned in laughter. A practice log that says thirty minutes, four times a week, documents attendance, not progress. Short recordings change that. A monthly recording of the same piece — halting in October, fluent in February, expressive in May — attached to the music entry for each month, is progress a reviewer can hear in three minutes. The parent does not record everything; that way lies an archive nobody can face. The rhythm that works is a single benchmark recording per child per month, plus the occasional capture of a breakthrough moment, trimmed to its essential minute. The transcripts matter less for music itself, but the parent's spoken notes recorded after a lesson — what the teacher assigned, what finally clicked about hand position — transcribe into the searchable record alongside everything else, so a question in March about what was advised in November has an answer.

Recitation and memory work form the third strand. Poems, speeches, scripture or passages in a second language, times tables sung rather than said, the oldest child's carefully rehearsed presentation for the co-op science fair — all of these are performances that exist only in the air unless recorded at the moment they are ready. Families often record the polished final version on a phone for grandparents and lose the developmental sequence that a portfolio actually needs: the first stumbling attempt, which shows where the child started. VaultBook's version history and dated entries make it natural to keep both, because storage inside a single file rewards keeping the sequence together rather than scattering attempts across a camera roll. When the child themselves listens back — and children should, because hearing their own improvement is a powerful motivator that no parental praise quite replicates — the inline player means playback happens inside the same record where the text of the poem sits in the Reader, so the child can follow the words while listening, and notice where their recitation drifted from the text.

Honesty requires naming what local transcription cannot do. Whisper, running locally, is good but not perfect, and it is least perfect exactly where homeschool audio is hardest: young children, overlapping voices, background household noise, proper names from history and mythology, and words in languages other than the family's main one. Transcripts in this vault are treated as drafts to be corrected while listening, not as court records, and the parent corrects names and key terms as part of the weekly review rather than trusting the raw output. Long recordings also take real processing time on an ordinary family computer; transcribing a forty-minute co-op presentation is an overnight or mealtime task, not an instant one, and very long files are better cut into sections first. Nor does any of this remove the human judgement about what is worth recording at all. A family that records every narration will drown in audio just as surely as a family that photographs every worksheet drowns in images. The tool rewards a sampling discipline — regular, dated, attached to context — and punishes hoarding exactly as much as a shoebox of unlabelled tapes once did, only faster.

What the parent reports valuing most, by spring, is not any single feature but the way the voices accumulate into something the family did not set out to build: an audible record of three children growing up inside their education. The youngest's narrations lengthen audibly across the year, from three sentences to a full retold chapter with voices for the characters. The middle child's music recordings show a practice-room patience that report cards would never mention. The oldest, listening to a September narration in May, winces and laughs at how unsure they sounded, and then, unprompted, records a new one to show the difference. That last act — a child choosing to document their own growth — is the moment the portfolio stops being a compliance exercise assembled by a parent and becomes a shared family record the children themselves have a stake in keeping well.


## Paper without the paper pile — scanning worksheets and building the portfolio PDF inside the vault

Every homeschool household contains a paper problem that its occupants have agreed, tacitly, not to discuss. It might be a crate in the corner of the dining room. It might be a stack of binders whose rings no longer close, a filing cabinet drawer that sticks because it is overfilled, or a series of supermarket bags, one per term, each containing everything from a carefully completed map of the Roman Empire to a maths worksheet with a jam stain. Paper accumulates in home education for good reasons. Young children learn with their hands, on paper. Workbooks are paper. Art is paper, often paper far too large for any folder. A parent who has been told, correctly, to keep evidence of work across subjects and across the year learns to keep everything, because discarding the wrong page in October feels riskier than keeping a thousand unnecessary pages until June. The result, by portfolio season, is an archaeological dig conducted in a mood of low-grade panic.

The scanning workflow in this family's VaultBook year did not begin with a grand digitisation project, and that is precisely why it worked. Community discussions among homeschooling parents are full of cautionary tales about the big scan weekend: a parent hires or borrows a fast scanner, feeds hundreds of pages through it, produces a folder of image files named by the scanner's numbering system, and then never labels, sorts, or looks at any of them again, while also feeling unable to throw away the originals because the scans are effectively unfindable. A digital pile is not an improvement over a paper pile. It is the same pile with worse visibility. What worked here instead was a small, continuous habit built on VaultBook's combination of full search, OCR, and PDF tools inside the single vault file, so that each scanned page entered a system where it could be found again by its content rather than by its filename.

The habit ran like this. At the end of each week, the parent selected the week's paper worth keeping — and selection, again, is the skill the whole system depends on. Not every completed worksheet earns a scan. The pages that earn it are the representative and the revelatory: a typical maths page showing the current level of work, the first page where a new concept appears, a page with a revealing mistake and the child's own correction beside it, a piece of writing in the child's hand, a test or quiz where the state or the family's chosen curriculum expects one, and anything the child is proud of. Those pages, perhaps fifteen or twenty in a week across three children, were photographed or scanned and brought into the relevant entries in the vault. VaultBook's OCR then made the printed and, within limits, handwritten content of those pages searchable, which changes their status entirely. A scanned worksheet in a folder is a picture of a worksheet. A scanned worksheet whose text is recognised and indexed inside a searchable vault is a document that can answer questions: when did the middle child first work with fractions as parts of a set rather than parts of a shape, which spelling list contained the word the youngest kept missing, where is the map the oldest drew before the field trip to compare with the one drawn after it.

That searchability is what allows the paper originals to leave the house, and for this family that departure mattered more than any single feature. Once a term's representative pages were scanned, OCR-checked, attached to dated entries, and backed up as part of the single vault file, the crate for that term could be honestly emptied — most of it recycled, a small selection of original art and sentimental pieces kept in one slim folder per child. The dining room corner came back. More importantly, the parent's relationship to incoming paper changed. Knowing there is a reliable weekly route from paper to searchable record removes the fear that drives hoarding. A worksheet can be assessed on its merits, kept digitally if it evidences something, and let go physically without the nagging sense that a future reviewer might demand exactly that page.

The second half of the workflow arrives at portfolio time, when the selected evidence must become a document a reviewer can actually receive, open, and read in order. This is where VaultBook's PDF merge, split, and compress tools earn their place, and where the contrast with the family's previous method is sharpest. Previously, assembling the portfolio PDF meant exporting and downloading pieces from several places — scans from one app, a summary written in a document in cloud storage, photographs exported from a phone, a reading list from a spreadsheet — and then combining them with whatever free online PDF merger a search turned up, which meant uploading a child's educational records, including in some years health and evaluation documents, to an unknown server run by an unknown company in order to rearrange pages. Many parents do this without a second thought because no alternative is visible at midnight before a deadline. The privacy problem is real even when nothing bad happens: the records left the family's control, copies may persist on that server under terms nobody read, and the whole transaction happened because merging pages is a trivial operation that should never have required an upload in the first place.

Inside VaultBook, the assembly stays home. The parent exports or generates the pieces from the vault — term summaries, selected scans, reading lists, attendance and activity logs — and then uses the PDF tools within the same local environment to merge them into the portfolio in the order the state guidance suggests: identifying information first, then per-child sections moving through subjects, with the strongest evidence early in each section. Splitting matters when a single large export, such as a workbook's scanned term, needs only its representative pages in the final document; the parent splits out the needed pages rather than bloating the portfolio with forty near-identical exercises. Compressing matters at the practical end of the process, because a portfolio rich in scanned pages and photographs can grow into a file too large to email to a reviewer or upload to a district portal with a size cap, and compressing it locally, with a check afterwards that the scanned handwriting is still legible at the reduced size, avoids the desperate last-minute resort to an online compressor and its upload.

There are genuine limits to state. OCR is excellent on clean printed text and good on neat handwriting, but a six-year-old's exuberant pencil work, faint photocopies, and pages photographed at an angle in poor light will defeat it in places, and the parent learned to check OCR results on any page they intended to rely on for search, correcting the recognised text for the most important pages. Scanning itself remains physical work: pages must be flattened, fed or photographed one by one, and checked for the classic failures — a thumb in the corner, a cut-off margin where the answer lived, two pages scanned as one. Very large artwork still does not belong in a scanner at all; it is photographed for the Explorer and the original is kept or released on its own terms. And a single-file vault containing a year's scanned evidence is a file that must be backed up with genuine discipline — copied to an external drive and to a second location the family controls — because concentrating records in one portable file is a strength only when that file is not sitting in exactly one place. VaultBook makes backup simple, since one file is trivially easier to copy than a scattered system, but simple is not the same as automatic, and the family keeps a standing end-of-term backup routine for exactly that reason.

By review day, what the parent carried was not a crate but a single, ordered, legible PDF per child, assembled entirely on the family computer, with the full vault behind it if deeper evidence was requested. The reviewer could be shown, live, how a scanned page in the PDF connected back to the dated entry, the surrounding weeks, the photographs of the same project, and the child's narration about it. That connectedness is the real product of the scanning habit. Paper, in a homeschool, is where much of the learning physically happens. The point of scanning into VaultBook was never to pretend otherwise, or to chase a paperless ideal the children themselves would find sterile. The point was to give paper a way to be found, selected, combined, and finally released — so the house keeps the learning and loses the pile.


## Planning without the October abandonment — Kanban for units, terms and the work that moves

There is a joke homeschooling parents make about planners, and like most durable jokes it is a confession. The confession goes: in August, the parent buys a beautiful planner, or builds a beautiful planning spreadsheet, or prints a beautiful set of colour-coded term charts, and plans the year in loving detail. In September, the plan meets the children. By October, the beautiful system has been abandoned, not because the parent is disorganised, but because the plan was built as a fixed script in a household that runs on weather, illness, curiosity, library due dates, a grandparent's visit, a broken washing machine, and the discovery that one child needs three more weeks on fractions while another has raced ahead and is bored. The plan did not bend, so the plan broke, and the parent planned the rest of the year in their head and on sticky notes, feeling vaguely guilty whenever the abandoned planner surfaced in a drawer.

This family had abandoned three systems in three years before moving planning into VaultBook's Kanban board, and the reasons for those abandonments are worth naming because they explain what a board has to do differently. The paper planner failed on rescheduling: moving a unit meant erasing, crossing out, and rewriting until the page was unreadable, so changes simply stopped being recorded. The shared cloud planning app failed on friction and exposure: it wanted an account, a sync, a subscription-tier decision about boards, and it placed the family's detailed daily life — including notes about a child's struggles — on a service whose business is hosting other people's plans, while also being unreachable on the rural co-op's unreliable connection exactly when the parent wanted to check it. The spreadsheet failed on perspective: it could hold dates and subjects in a grid, but it could not show, at a glance, what was stuck, what was nearly done, and what nobody had touched, and it made moving work between weeks an exercise in cutting and pasting rows that nobody sustained past September.

A Kanban board fits homeschool planning for a reason that has little to do with software fashion and everything to do with how the work actually behaves. Homeschool work moves. A unit study on local rivers is planned, then waits two weeks for the rain to stop, then happens in a rush of field trips and readings, then produces follow-on work — a map, an essay, a water-quality test — that nobody scheduled in August. A Kanban board represents that honestly with columns that describe states rather than dates: ideas and resources gathered, planned and ready, in progress this week, waiting on something, done and documented. A card for a unit, a book, a project, or a course module sits in a column that tells the truth about it today, and moving the card when the truth changes takes a drag rather than a rewrite. The plan stops being a promise the family keeps breaking and becomes a picture of the work that the family keeps updating, which is a profound psychological difference for the parent. A board with many cards in progress and a healthy flow into done is evidence of a working system. A planner full of crossed-out August intentions is evidence only of optimism.

In this family's vault, the board carries three distinct planning horizons at once, and keeping them distinct is what prevents the October collapse. At the longest horizon, one set of cards represents the year's major units and commitments per child and for the family together: the terms are visible as groupings, the state-required subjects are each represented, and the big fixed points — the portfolio review itself, a co-op production, a music examination, a multi-week trip — sit on the board from the start of the year so nothing else is planned in ignorance of them. At the middle horizon, cards represent the current term's units in preparation: resources being gathered, library books on hold, a museum membership about to be used, the oldest child's research project moving from question to outline to draft. At the shortest horizon, the in-progress column is deliberately kept small — a rule the parent borrowed from watching their own overwhelm — because a column showing nine simultaneous units across three children is not a plan, it is an admission that nothing has been prioritised. The board makes that visible in a way a list never did. When the in-progress column grows, the family finishes or parks something before starting the next thing, and parking a card by moving it back to waiting is recorded as a decision, not experienced as a failure.

Cards earn their keep by what they connect to. In VaultBook, a Kanban card lives in the same single file as the entries, photographs, recordings, and scans that the work produces, so a card for the rivers unit can be related to the notes gathered during planning, the field-trip entries, the photographs with their dates, the water-test results charted later, and eventually the portfolio summary that draws on all of it. Related notes and dynamic tabs in the vault reinforce this: the parent can move from the board's overview into the underlying material and back without changing applications, exporting anything, or wondering which folder the resources went to. Planning and evidence, which in most homeschool systems live in different tools and drift apart by November, here occupy the same ground. When the parent plans the next term, they are looking at the same record that shows what the last term actually contained — not what August hoped it would contain — and planning improves accordingly. The middle child needs more time on written composition, not as a resolution but as a visible fact: the board shows composition cards repeatedly waiting, and the entries show why.

The calendar and the Due and Expiring sidebar supply the time dimension that a board alone deliberately lacks. Kanban shows state; it does not nag about dates, and some things in a homeschool year are genuinely date-bound: library books come due, co-op fees must be paid, a museum's homeschool day happens once, state paperwork has a filing deadline, borrowed curriculum must be returned, a child's evaluation appointment sits on a particular morning. Those live in the vault's calendar, and the sidebar surfaces what is due and what is expiring soon each time the parent opens the file, which converts deadline management from a separate act of anxious remembering into a glance. The combination matters because the two failure modes of homeschool planning are opposite: rigid date-plans that shatter on contact with life, and fluid no-date systems in which a filing deadline quietly passes. Board for flow, calendar and sidebar for the fixed points — together they cover both.

Honest limits apply here as much as anywhere. A Kanban board does not plan for you. It will faithfully display a bad curriculum choice, an overloaded term, and a subject the parent is avoiding, and its clarity about avoidance can be uncomfortable before it is useful. It is also, in VaultBook, a local tool: a co-parent or co-op coordinator who needs live, simultaneous access to the same board from their own device, editing at the same moment, is better served by a collaborative cloud board, and this family handles that by exporting or reading out the relevant plan for co-op coordination rather than pretending the vault does live collaboration. It does not. Similarly, a parent whose planning style is genuinely a detailed daily timetable, lesson by lesson and hour by hour, may find a board's state-based view too loose on its own and will pair it with the calendar and daily entries, as this family does for the oldest child's structured coursework. The board replaced the script, not the schedule.

What it replaced, most importantly, was the annual guilt cycle. This year there was no October abandonment because there was nothing rigid enough to abandon. The board in October looked different from the board in August — units reordered, one abandoned unit card moved to a someday grouping without ceremony after the children's interest genuinely died, two unplanned units added when a local exhibition and a family conversation opened doors nobody had predicted — and every one of those changes was recorded as ordinary movement rather than confessed as deviation. When the parent later wrote the year's summary for the portfolio, the board's history of movement, alongside the dated entries, let them describe the year truthfully: here is what we planned, here is what changed and why, here is what the change produced. Reviewers, who have seen many rigid plans and many abandoned planners, tend to trust that account. It sounds like education as it is actually lived, because for once the planning tool was shaped like the life it was planning.


## The private annex — health notes, evaluations and family matters under per-entry encryption

A homeschool record is never only an educational record. Woven through any honest year of home education are documents and notes of an entirely different sensitivity: a child's evaluation identifying a reading difficulty and recommending support, a therapist's letter, notes from a difficult meeting with a district official, a record of a custody arrangement that affects who may collect a child from co-op, a parent's private journal entries about a child's anxiety, a family's financial notes about what curriculum they can and cannot afford this term, medical information relevant to field trips, allergy and medication details, and the frank assessments a parent writes at midnight that are essential for planning honestly and wholly unsuitable for any other reader. In most families' systems, these materials live wherever they happened to land — an email inbox, a notes app synced to a cloud account, a folder on a shared family drive, a paper file in the same binder as the spelling tests. The sensitivity of the material and the casualness of its storage have almost nothing to do with each other, and parents know it, and worry about it in the background, and mostly do not act, because acting would mean running a second, separate, secure system alongside the everyday one, and second systems are where records go to be forgotten.

VaultBook's answer to this is architectural rather than procedural, and it is the feature this family describes as the one that finally let them keep one record instead of two: per-entry AES-256-GCM encryption inside a vault that is already local-first and makes zero network connections. Per-entry is the operative phrase. The parent does not have to choose between encrypting everything — which would make daily use cumbersome — and encrypting nothing. Individual entries can be encrypted individually, with their own protection, while the ordinary life of the vault — spelling lists, unit plans, field-trip logs — remains quick to open and work with. The evaluation report is an encrypted entry. The notes from the meeting about it are an encrypted entry. The parent's planning notes that reference it in frank terms are encrypted. The reading log that the evaluation influenced, the book lists and the progress photographs, are ordinary entries that can be shown to a reviewer without any risk that the neighbouring private material is exposed in the same motion. That granularity mirrors how the family actually thinks about the material: not secret versus public as two worlds, but a spectrum of audiences, handled entry by entry.

The local-first, zero-network design matters as much as the cipher, because encryption at rest is only part of the exposure picture. A note stored in a conventional cloud notes app may be encrypted in transit and on the provider's disks, but it exists on systems the family does not control, under terms that can change, accessible through an account that can be compromised, reset, or locked, and in many services it is processed — indexed, scanned, used to train or improve features — in ways the family cannot observe. For material like a child's evaluation, the relevant question is not whether a large provider is probably careful. It is whether the document needs to be there at all. In VaultBook it is not there. It is in a single file on the family's own computer, copied only where the family copies it for backup, and never transmitted by the application because the application does not transmit. When the parent opens the vault on a laptop at a co-op, in a library, or at the review meeting itself, no sync occurs, no account is signed into, and no network call carries anything anywhere. The privacy of the private annex is a property of the design, not a promise in a policy document that a future acquisition could revise.

In practice, the annex in this family's vault holds several distinct categories, and describing them shows why a single everyday system with strong per-entry protection beats a separate secrets system that drifts out of date. First, health and developmental records: evaluations, support plans, therapy summaries, and the parent's dated observations that give those professional documents their context across time — what was tried after the evaluation, what changed, what did not. Because those observations sit, encrypted, in the same timeline as the ordinary educational record, the parent can prepare for a follow-up appointment with a complete picture, and can also demonstrate, if a reviewer properly asks how identified needs are being addressed, that a coherent plan exists and is being followed — disclosing the summary the parent chooses to share, not the underlying file. Second, family and administrative sensitivities: correspondence about requirements and deadlines where a dispute exists, notes on family circumstances that explain a disrupted term, financial planning for curriculum and activities, and copies of identity and eligibility documents the family is periodically asked to produce. Third, the children's own private material, which grows in importance as the oldest child ages: a journal kept as part of a writing programme, drafts not ready to be read, reflections written for an audience of one. Teaching a child that their private writing will actually be kept private — encrypted even within the family record, opened only by arrangement — is itself an educational act, and one that a synced family notes account, readable by whoever holds the account credentials, cannot honestly offer.

A password generator inside the vault sounds like an unrelated utility until the annex makes its purpose obvious. Per-entry encryption is only as strong as the passphrases protecting it, and human beings asked to invent strong, unique passphrases unaided produce weak, reused ones, especially tired parents at midnight. Generating a strong passphrase within the same local tool, and following the family's own arrangement for keeping recovery information safely — written, sealed, and stored physically in the home rather than in another cloud account — turns the annex from a feature into a practice. The parent is candid about the corresponding danger, and any honest account must be: encryption that cannot be bypassed also cannot be rescued. If the passphrase for an encrypted entry is lost and no recovery note exists, that entry is gone, and no support desk anywhere can restore it, because there is no server copy and no back door. That is not a defect in the design; it is the design, stated plainly. It means the family's backup discipline has two parts, not one: back up the vault file itself in more than one physical location, and keep the means of opening its encrypted entries recorded somewhere the family can reach in an emergency and an intruder cannot. Families unwilling to maintain that second discipline should encrypt less, not more, and keep only what truly needs it under per-entry protection.

Two further limits deserve the same plainness. Encryption inside the vault protects entries when the vault is closed and when the file is copied or stolen; it does not protect what is on the screen while the parent is working, it does not protect a computer already compromised by malware, and it does not substitute for ordinary device hygiene — a logged-in, unattended laptop at a co-op is a risk no cipher in a file can answer. Nor does per-entry encryption organise the material by itself: an encrypted entry with a vague title and no links to related notes is a small safe whose contents the owner has forgotten, so the family names annex entries clearly for their own future selves and relates them, where safe, to the ordinary entries they affect. Finally, judgement about what belongs in the annex remains human. Over-encrypting everyday material creates friction that tempts a parent to stop using the system; under-encrypting an evaluation recreates the original problem. The family's working rule — if reading this aloud to a stranger would feel like a betrayal of a child, encrypt it — has held up well across a year of edge cases.

The effect on the portfolio process is subtle but real. Because the private material has a proper home inside the same record, the parent never faces the old choice between bringing the whole messy binder, private pages included, or spending an evening extracting the shareable pages and hoping nothing sensitive slipped between them. The reviewer sees the educational record, complete and coherent. The annex stays closed, in the same file, under the parent's hand. And in the family's own life, beyond any review, the annex has become the place where the hardest parts of the year are recorded truthfully — the evaluation that was difficult to read, the term that illness dismantled, the worries that preceded the breakthrough — which means the record of this homeschool year is not a sanitised success story. It is the whole story, with the private chapters locked by their author, which is exactly what a family archive ought to be.


## Field trips, co-ops and learning far from Wi-Fi

Ask homeschooling parents where the best learning of the year happened and very few of them point at the kitchen table. They point at a wetland in October rain, at a working farm, at the back room of a small museum where a volunteer let the children hold a printing press tool, at a co-op science morning that turned into an afternoon because nobody wanted to stop, at a historical site three counties away where the oldest child suddenly understood a book they had resisted for months. Learning away from home is not enrichment on the margins of this family's education; it is a substantial share of the education itself. It is also, from a record-keeping point of view, the hardest learning to capture, because it happens away from the desk, away from the planner, and very often away from any reliable internet connection — in rural nature centres, in the basements of old buildings, in car parks, on trains, in the particular dead zones that seem to surround every interesting place in the countryside.

The failure pattern before VaultBook will be familiar to any parent who has tried to document a field trip with cloud-based tools. The parent takes photographs that will not upload until the family is home, by which time they join the undifferentiated camera roll. Notes are typed into a phone app that spins, complains about connectivity, and occasionally loses the paragraph written in a field while a child was asking a question. A co-op coordinator shares resources through a platform that requires a login the parent cannot complete on the venue's captive Wi-Fi. The worksheet or activity sheet from the venue goes into a bag and emerges, crumpled, a fortnight later, detached from the photographs and the memory of what the youngest child said about the heron. Back home, reconstructing the day into a record is a small research project, and on busy weeks it simply does not happen, so the richest days of the term are the thinnest entries in the record. Parents describe this gap constantly in community conversations: the portfolio ends up over-representing worksheets, because worksheets document themselves, and under-representing the expeditions that actually changed what the children understand.

A local-first vault that makes zero network connections inverts that experience, because nothing about using VaultBook depends on a signal. The single-file HTML vault opens on the parent's laptop or tablet in a farm car park exactly as it opens at the kitchen table. During the trip itself, the parent and, increasingly, the older children add to the record in the moment: a dated entry started in the morning with the plan and the questions each child wanted to answer, observations added as they occur, the middle child's measurement of a pond's edge, the oldest child's sketch photographed and attached, a short recording of a ranger answering a question — captured with permission — and of the children's own immediate narrations while the experience is still vivid, which are invariably better than anything they produce at home that evening. Those recordings can be transcribed later, back home, with the local transcription described earlier, but the capture itself needs nothing but the device in hand. The Reader view serves the other direction of the same day: texts the family brought with them — a poem about the coast, a chapter of the history they are visiting the site to understand, identification guides saved into the vault beforehand — are readable offline in the same place where the day's notes are being written, so reading and recording are not split between a synced library app that may or may not have downloaded the book and a notes app that may or may not save.

Preparation is where the offline design quietly earns its keep before anyone leaves the house. In the days before a trip, the parent gathers the relevant material into the vault while connectivity is plentiful: venue information, tickets or booking confirmations saved as entries, the map, the relevant past entries through related notes — last spring's visit to the same wetland, the book the middle child read in preparation, the questions left unanswered last time. The calendar holds the day's fixed points and the Due and Expiring sidebar has already surfaced the practical deadlines in the run-up: the booking that had to be confirmed, the library guide due back the day after the trip, the co-op fee. Because everything needed is inside one file, there is no departure-morning audit of which app holds what, and no discovery, at a venue gate, that a confirmation email is trapped behind a login on a network that will not load. The file is either on the device or it is not, and the family checks once.

Co-op life adds a second dimension, because co-ops are social institutions with their own records: shared classes taught in rotation by parents, attendance that some co-ops track carefully, resources lent and borrowed, a term's schedule negotiated among a dozen families, and children's work produced collaboratively that belongs in each family's own portfolio. Here VaultBook's strengths and its honest limits sit side by side, and the family is clear-eyed about both. The vault is an excellent personal record of co-op participation: the parent's own teaching plans for the sessions they lead, dated entries and photographs for each session, the resources they prepared in the Reader, the follow-up work each of their three children did at home at their own level, and the calendar's record of the term's rhythm. It is not a live collaboration platform, and it does not pretend to be. The co-op's shared scheduling and messaging happen in whatever tools the group has chosen, and the parent transfers what matters into the family vault rather than expecting other families to adopt it. Where a cloud notes or board tool is genuinely stronger — many editors at once, instant shared visibility — the family says so and uses the shared tool for the shared purpose, keeping the vault as the record of their own children's learning within it. That division of labour is not a compromise that needs apologising for. A portfolio is a family's record, presented to a reviewer; it was never the co-op's group chat.

Two practicalities of learning away from home deserve mention because they decide whether a system survives contact with real trips. The first is the import routes back into the record. Material gathered elsewhere — a resource a co-op parent recommends, a curriculum document begun in another tool, an entire set of notes from an earlier year kept in Obsidian — needs a way in that does not involve retyping, and VaultBook's Obsidian import serves exactly that purpose when the family adopted a resource collection another parent had built, bringing it into the same searchable vault where the trip records live. The second is data visualisation, which finds an unexpected home in field learning: the children's own collected data — temperatures across a day outdoors, bird counts across repeated visits to the same site, rainfall measured in the garden between trips, the oldest child's water-quality readings — becomes charts inside the vault, attached to the entries that explain how the data was gathered. A reviewer shown a child's own chart of counts across four visits, rising in accuracy and ambition, is being shown scientific practice developing, not a worksheet about it. And the parent, searching the full vault on the evening after a trip, can connect today's chart to last term's with a plain-language question, because search, transcription, and semantic question-and-answer all work on the local record without sending a child's data anywhere to be analysed.

There are constraints the family plans around rather than denies. A single-file vault on one device means the device matters: a dead battery ends the day's recording, so a charged power bank travels with the same reliability as the packed lunches, and the vault file is copied to the parent's phone as a read-and-add backup before longer trips. Photographs and video from a long expedition make the file grow, so the weekly selection discipline matters even more for trip media than for table work. And working offline means working without instant answers — a question that arises in a field and is not answered by the material in the vault waits until home, which the parent has come to regard, after initial irritation, as a feature of the pedagogy rather than a lack: the children record the question in the entry, argue about it in the car, and look it up together later, and the question's journey becomes part of the record too.

By the end of the year, the field-trip and co-op entries are, as the parent expected, the sections of the portfolio the reviewer lingered over longest — not because they were polished, but because they were complete: dated, photographed, narrated in the children's own voices, connected to the reading that preceded them and the work that followed, and assembled without a single moment in which a child's record depended on a signal in a field. The kitchen table is where this family's homeschool is administered, but the vault's offline design means the record travels to where the education actually happens, and comes home with it, whole.

Taken together, the strands of this second half of the family's year — the dated photographs that turned a camera roll into evidence of progression, the voices preserved and transcribed without ever leaving the house, the paper that was scanned, searched, assembled into a portfolio at home and then released, the plan that bent instead of breaking, the private annex that let one record hold both the shareable and the sensitive truthfully, and the learning captured far from any network — describe a single underlying change rather than six separate improvements. The record stopped being a set of fragments held in services, bags, binders, and memory, each with its own login, its own failure mode, and its own claim on the children's privacy, and became one file the family owns outright, works with anywhere, and can present from with confidence. The portfolio review, when it came, was almost an anticlimax: an afternoon conversation over a well-ordered record, with the evidence for every claim within reach. The parent is careful to say that VaultBook did not do the teaching, did not choose the books, run the experiments, or sit through the difficult weeks, and no tool could. What it did was ensure that the teaching which happened — in the rain, at the table, in the car, in the hard weeks as well as the golden ones — was not lost between the living of it and the telling of it, and for a homeschooling family, that keeping of the whole story is the difference between a year they can prove and a year they can actually remember.

## When the school document cloud went dark — the PowerSchool outage of November 2026

There is a particular kind of quiet that falls over a kitchen table when a parent clicks a link that is supposed to open a document and nothing useful happens. Not a dramatic crash. Not a blue screen. Just a spinner, a grey placeholder, a message that says the service is being investigated, and underneath it the ordinary, pressing reality that the document was needed now — for a co-op coordinator waiting on a form, for an umbrella programme administrator closing out a term, for a district office that holds records a family depends on to prove attendance, progress, or compliance.

In late November 2026, that quiet was not hypothetical. PowerSchool, one of the largest education technology providers in North America, listed its Document Cloud services in Major Outage on its official status page. According to [PowerSchool's status page](https://status.powerschool.com/), the Document Management component was first posted as "Investigating" on November 24, 2026 at 21:33 PST. Updates followed on November 25 at 10:38 PST, and again on November 26 at 05:24 and 06:41 PST, each confirming in substance that Document Management remained offline while teams continued working — through the US holiday period — to restore it. The affected components were not a minor feature tucked in a corner of the product. They were the Document Cloud for the United States and Canada: Document Delivery, Document Signature, Document Management, and Document Library. Taken together, those four names describe almost everything a school organisation does with a formal document after it is created: deliver it, get it signed, manage it, and store it where others can find it again.

It matters to frame this precisely, because precision is the whole point of this section. This was not a story about every homeschool family in America suddenly losing its own records. A family that keeps its attendance logs, reading lists, work samples, and evaluations in a folder on its own shelf, or in a file on its own computer, was not locked out of anything by PowerSchool's outage. The families exposed to this kind of disruption are the ones whose records pass through an institution that uses such a system — a school district that provides services to homeschool students, a charter or umbrella programme that holds the official file, a co-operative that collects signed agreements, medical forms, and portfolio submissions through a managed document platform, or a private programme that requires parents to retrieve and return documents through a portal. When the portal's document layer goes dark, the family's copy — if the family's only copy lives inside that portal — goes dark with it. The parent has done nothing wrong. The password still works. The account is still in good standing. The document is simply on the other side of a service that is, for the moment, not serving.

That distinction is not a technicality. It is the difference between custody and access, and custody is the subject of this entire article. Access is what a service grants you when it is running, when your account is recognised, when the network cooperates, and when the company's engineers have not declared an incident. Custody is what you have when none of those conditions are required. A homeschool family lives closer to this distinction than almost any other kind of record-keeper, because the family's records are simultaneously personal and official. A third-grade reading log is a memory. It is also, in many states and under many umbrella programmes, evidence. A high school transcript built at a kitchen table is a source of pride. It is also the document a college admissions office, an employer, or a state reviewer will treat as the formal record of four years of work. When a single set of files must be both keepsake and compliance record, the question of who physically holds them stops being an abstract technology preference.

The PowerSchool incident is useful precisely because it was undramatic in its causes and ordinary in its shape. Services have incidents. Large, competent, well-funded services have incidents. The status page language — investigating, working to restore, teams engaged over a holiday — is the language of people doing a difficult job under pressure, and there is no reason to read it as anything else. No family's planning should rest on the assumption that a vendor is careless. It should rest on the simpler observation that any system reachable only through someone else's running service will, at some point, be unreachable for reasons that have nothing to do with the family. An outage measured in days, spanning a holiday when offices are closed and deadlines do not move, is enough. A portfolio review does not reschedule itself because a document library is offline. A signature deadline for a co-op agreement does not pause. A state reporting window, where one applies, opens and closes on the calendar the state published, not on the calendar a vendor's incident bridge prefers.

Consider how this plays out in the specific, unglamorous situations homeschool families actually face. A mother in a district-partnership programme needs the signed dual-enrollment form that lives in the document library — the form the college requires before it will release a course seat for January. She can describe the form. She signed it. She remembers the date. None of that produces the form. A father coordinating a co-op's spring term needs the signed facility-use agreements and the background-check acknowledgements that parents returned through document delivery; without them, he cannot confirm to the church hall that lends the rooms that the paperwork is complete, and the hall, reasonably, wants paper before it hands over keys. An umbrella programme administrator, herself a homeschool parent of twenty years' standing, fields calls from families who need copies of evaluations she filed for them — evaluations that are, at that moment, inside a document management system she cannot open either. She is not withholding anything. She is locked out of the same room her families are locked out of, which is the defining feature of shared cloud custody: when it fails, it fails for everyone in the same way at the same time, including the people trying to help.

Against that background, set the contrasting scene — not a triumphant one, just a boring one, which is exactly what good record-keeping should feel like. In the family this article has followed, the records that matter most do not live in a portal. They live in a VaultBook vault: a single-file HTML document stored on the family's own computer, with copies the family itself makes on media it controls. When the mother in our central scenario needs the immunisation record, the evaluation letter, the signed agreement, or the transcript draft, she opens the vault. There is no service to be down. VaultBook makes zero network requests; there is no server on the other end whose status page could carry bad news, no account session to expire mid-task, no document library component to enter a Major Outage state. The file opens in a browser, locally, the way it opened the day before and the way it will open during an internet outage, a vendor incident, a move to a house where the broadband has not yet been connected, or a week at a grandparent's home with no connection worth the name. The per-entry AES-256-GCM encryption, derived through PBKDF2 and executed with the Web Crypto API in the browser, protects the contents at rest without changing this essential fact: decryption happens on the family's machine, under the family's passphrase, and depends on no third party being awake, solvent, unbreached, or finished with its incident review.

It is worth pausing on what this does and does not prove. It does not prove that institutional document systems are useless; districts and umbrella programmes adopt them for real reasons — standardisation, multi-staff access, audit trails across hundreds of families — that a single family's vault does not attempt to replicate. It does not prove that every family should refuse to use a portal its programme requires; in many arrangements, submitting through the portal is a condition of participation, and the sensible response is to comply fully while keeping one's own complete copy. And it does not prove that a local file can never be lost: drives fail, laptops are stolen, houses have fires, and a later section of this article takes that responsibility seriously. What the November 2026 outage does prove, in the modest and sufficient way that real incidents prove things, is that the two copies are not equivalent. The portal copy is available when the portal is available. The vault copy is available when the family is present with its file and its passphrase. For records that must serve as both memory and evidence across eighteen years of childhood, that difference is not a feature comparison. It is the architecture of the family's peace of mind.

There is also a longer, quieter lesson in the dates on that status page. The incident began on the evening of November 24 and was still being worked on the morning of November 26, with the holiday in between — which is to say, it occupied exactly the stretch of the year when homeschool families are least able to chase paperwork and most likely to need it settled: end-of-term submissions, mid-year reviews, spring enrollment forms, and the annual attempt to close the year's files before December. Timing is a property of outages that no service-level promise fully removes. A family's own vault has no timing. It does not observe holidays. It does not schedule maintenance windows. It does not merge with another company, retire a product line, or change the terms under which old documents can be exported. It sits where the family put it, and it opens when the family opens it. In a month when a major provider's document cloud could not say the same, that plain, almost dull reliability is the news — and it is reason enough, on its own, to keep the family's irreplaceable records in the family's own hands.

## Why VaultBook beats the market leaders for this family's records

Every homeschool family that has not yet chosen a system has already, in practice, been offered several. The market-leading note and document apps are present in the family's life before anyone makes a deliberate decision: a phone ships with one, a co-op shares through another, a tutor sends a link from a third, a grandparent keeps a list in a fourth. The question is never whether these tools are capable — the leaders lead because they are genuinely good at what they were built to do. The question is whether what they were built to do matches the specific job of holding a child's educational record, privately, completely, and under the family's sole control, for years at a time. This section takes each leader in turn, states the concrete mechanism by which VaultBook serves this scenario better, and states just as plainly where the leader remains stronger. The argument is not that these apps are poor tools. It is that custody, encryption, and independence — the properties this scenario turns on — are architectural properties, and on architecture the leaders and VaultBook simply made different choices.

**Notion.** Notion is a cloud workspace: pages, databases, and shared workspaces that live on Notion's servers and are reached through an account. For a homeschool family, that means the portfolio, the planner, and the record of the year exist as content inside a service — editable from anywhere, certainly, but held in custody by the provider, dependent on the account remaining in good standing and the service remaining reachable, and not protected by per-entry end-to-end encryption of the kind VaultBook applies. In VaultBook, each sensitive entry — the evaluation letter, the medical form, the transcript notes — is individually encrypted with AES-256-GCM, so the vault file can sit on a family computer, a backup drive, or a relative's machine for safekeeping without its protected contents being readable to whoever holds the storage. The honest limit: Evernote's capture pipeline, especially its web clipping and long-refined forwarding and scanning workflows, remains strong, and families who gather material from across the web all day will find its intake smoother. Its notebooks are held through that account, typically in OneDrive — which is to say, custody sits with Microsoft, access runs through account authentication, and the record's availability is bound to that ecosystem's health and the family's standing within it. VaultBook's advantage for this scenario is independence from any such account layer: no Microsoft account, no OneDrive, no sign-in event stands between a parent and the portfolio on the morning of a review. For a record that must outlast any particular device — and a homeschool record must outlast several — that portability is not a convenience feature; it is preservation policy. The honest limit: on-device capture in Apple Notes, for a family entirely within Apple's world, is superb — the quick note from the lock screen, the scanned document straight from the camera, the note that is simply there. **Samsung Notes.** Where a family lives in the Galaxy ecosystem — a parent with a Galaxy phone or tablet, perhaps a child with a Galaxy tablet for lessons — Samsung Notes plays the role Apple Notes plays elsewhere, with the same structural outline: notes held within the vendor's ecosystem and account framework, most at home on Samsung hardware. VaultBook's advantage is the same mechanism stated once more, because the scenario keeps posing the same question: when the tablet is replaced by a different brand, when the reviewer has a different machine, when the record must be produced in a room with no accounts signed in, the single local file still opens. The honest limit deserves emphasis, because it is the strongest in this section: Samsung's S Pen handwriting experience is excellent — pressure, feel, and integration that generations of note-takers have chosen deliberately. A student whose primary work is handwritten on a Galaxy tablet is well served there. **Goodnotes.** Goodnotes is, fairly, the handwriting leader in education: the digital paper against which other handwriting apps are judged, beloved by students and teachers for the feel of writing, the organisation of notebooks, and the classroom workflows built around it. Nothing in this section disputes that crown, because the vault is not competing for it. The custody point is narrower. A Goodnotes-centred record is a record of handwritten notebooks, typically synchronised through a cloud account, optimised for the act of writing rather than for the long, mixed, evidentiary archive a homeschool portfolio becomes: evaluations from outside teachers, medical and legal forms, photographs with EXIF data, PDFs to merge, split, and annotate, audio of a child's reading, correspondence, transcripts. The honest limit is the mirror image of the claim: for the handwritten work itself — the daily maths, the copywork, the annotated worksheet — Goodnotes is better, and a family that loves it should keep it for exactly that. Step back from the seven comparisons and a pattern emerges that is more useful than any single verdict. Every leader named here is, by default, cloud-based and account-based; that is not an accusation but a description of how mainstream software is built, and for most purposes it serves people well. VaultBook was built for exactly that worst day, and built so that the worst day feels no different from any other. That is the whole of the Rule 6 case, and it needs no exaggeration: name the leaders, honour what they do better, and keep the family's record where the family can always reach it — at home.

## A year in the vault — September setup to June portfolio review, month by month

Abstract systems persuade nobody for long. What persuades a homeschool parent is a year — the actual, cluttered, interruption-filled year — and whether the system is still standing, still findable, still calm in June. So here is the year, month by month, in the life of the family this article has followed: two children, one in the middle elementary years and one entering the high school stretch, an umbrella programme that requires a mid-year check and an end-of-year portfolio, a co-op that meets weekly, and two parents who between them hold the roles of teacher, administrator, registrar, and archivist. The vault does not make the year easy. It makes the year's evidence impossible to lose track of, which turns out to be most of the battle.

**September — setup, and the discipline of beginnings.** The year starts before the first lesson, in the unglamorous week when forms multiply. The parents create the year's structure in the vault in a single sitting: a section per child, a section per subject, a section for the administrative spine of the year — attendance, medical forms, the umbrella programme agreement, the co-op commitments, the year's calendar of deadlines. Last year's vault is not overwritten or merged into confusion; the new year begins fresh in its own file, and the old file is copied, untouched, to the family's backup places. That separation matters more than it sounds. A portfolio reviewer wants a year, not a lifetime; a parent in March wants this year's reading list, not three years of reading lists interleaved. September is also when the calendar earns its keep: every fixed point of the year goes in at once — the umbrella programme's submission dates, the co-op's term boundaries, the evaluation booking window, the dates by which the medical forms expire. The Due/Expiring sidebar will spend the rest of the year quietly counting down to these, so that no deadline has to be carried in a parent's head for nine months. The children's first work goes in during the first fortnight, photographed as it comes off the table: the Photo/Video Explorer reads the EXIF data from each photograph, so the date and device information ride along with the image of the handwriting sample or the science project, and a parent sorting in June can trust the chronology without having typed a single date. September's lesson, learned by every organised family sooner or later, is that systems fail in June because of what was skipped in September. The vault makes September's work take an evening rather than a week — and so, crucially, it actually gets done.

**October — the rhythm of capture.** By October the novelty is gone and the rhythm is everything. Work flows into the vault in small, unremarkable batches: photographs of finished pages at the end of the school day, the older child's essays written directly into entries, the younger child's narrations captured as audio and transcribed locally with Whisper — the words becoming searchable text without the recording of a child's voice ever leaving the house, a point the parents feel more strongly as the year goes on. October is when full search stops being a specification and becomes a habit. A co-op leader asks whether the family has covered a particular topic yet; a parent searches the vault and answers in the room, from evidence, rather than from memory. A grandparent asks what the children are reading; the reading log is thirty seconds away. October also brings the year's first small crisis, as it always does: a form the umbrella programme insists it never received. In the old arrangement — a folder, an email thread, a portal — this would have meant an evening of archaeology. In the vault it means finding the entry, checking its version history to show when the record was created, and resending the copy with the calm of people who keep their own originals. Nothing about the moment is dramatic, and that is precisely the point the family will repeat to other parents all year: the vault does not create triumphs, it deletes emergencies.

**November — depth, and the month the cloud went dark.** November is the year's first deep water: the work gets harder, the days get shorter, and the mid-year requirements begin to cast shadows. The parents start a Kanban board for the older child's first substantial research project — columns for sources gathered, notes drafted, outline, first draft, revision — and discover that planning in the same place as the record removes a whole category of friction: the board's cards link naturally to the notes and sources themselves, because everything lives in one file. The younger child's board is simpler and more cheerful: this week's lessons, this week's nature study, the library books due back. Then, in the month's final week, comes the incident described earlier in this article: the document systems used by programmes around the family enter their difficult days around the holiday, coordinators send apologetic messages, and a few families in the co-op discover that documents they need are documents they cannot reach. Our family's experience of that week is almost embarrassingly plain. Everything the family itself must produce, prove, or sign from its own records is already in the vault, and the vault opens. The parents do the one useful thing available to them: they export copies for a co-op friend whose signed forms are stuck on the wrong side of the outage — copies of the family's own agreements, which they hold — and they add a line to their own mental ledger, not smug but settled: copies you hold are the only copies you have. November ends with the mid-year portfolio half-assembled almost by accident, because a year of steady capture means assembly is mostly selection.

**December — the honest month.** December is short, festive, and educationally lumpy, and the vault's contribution is permission to be honest about it. Attendance is recorded as it actually was — full weeks, broken weeks, the days given over to family and celebration — because a record that pretends otherwise is worthless as evidence and faintly corrosive as a family document. The parents photograph the term's best work before it is given away as gifts or recycled in the New Year clear-out, a small ritual that takes ten minutes and saves the portfolio from the classic December loss: the finest project of the autumn, proudly displayed, then gone. December is also when the family makes its first full backup review of the year: the vault file copied to the external drive, a copy to the encrypted stick that lives with a trusted relative, a check that the passphrase is written down where the parents agreed it would be written down, and nowhere else. Custody, December teaches, is a practice, not a purchase.

**January — the mid-year check.** January is the umbrella programme's mid-year moment: a progress submission, an attendance summary, evidence that the plan of September is recognisably the year being lived. Families who assemble this from scratch lose a weekend to it. This family loses an afternoon, most of it spent choosing rather than hunting: the search finds the term's work by subject and date; the related-notes suggestions surface connections a parent had forgotten — the nature journal entries that belong with the science summary, the narration transcripts that evidence the younger child's reading progress better than any test; the PDF tools assemble the submission into a single tidy document, merging the evaluation form, the samples, and the summary, and the family submits through the programme's channel while the originals never leave the vault. January also brings the new-term reset: the Kanban boards are cleared and re-cut for the spring, the calendar gains the term's new fixed points, and the Due/Expiring sidebar quietly flags that one medical form expires in the spring — months early, which is exactly when such a warning is useful rather than alarming.

**February — the deep-work month.** February is where homeschool years are quietly won: no major deadlines, no festivals of paperwork, just the steady accumulation of work. The vault's role is retrieval and connection. The older child's research project, dormant since November, resumes; every source, note, and draft is where it was left, and the version history shows the argument's evolution — a gift in itself when a parent wants to evidence process, not just product, for a reviewer who values it. The semantic Q&A earns its keep in February in a way keyword search never quite did: a parent asks the vault, in plain words, where in the year's material the younger child's understanding of fractions visibly turned a corner, and the answer gathers the October struggle, the November breakthrough narration, and the January fluency into one view. This is the month the parents stop describing the vault to friends as storage. It is, they have started saying, the family's memory of the year — indexed, dated, and honest.

**March — trips, disruptions, and working from anywhere.** March takes the family away from home for two stretches — a family obligation in another state, and the co-op's spring field programme — and the vault travels the way a single file travels: on the laptop, whole and entire, opening in a motel room with indifferent Wi-Fi because it needs no Wi-Fi at all. Lessons continue in fragments; the fragments are captured in fragments; the record stays continuous. A relative asks, with genuine curiosity, to see what the children have been learning, and the parents can show a curated view from the vault on their own machine, sharing exactly what they choose to share, entry by entry, rather than handing over an account or a device logged into everything. March is also when the first standardised-test logistics of the year appear on the horizon, and the calendar absorbs them without fuss. The month's quiet revelation is geographical: a record that lives in a file has the family's own mobility, while a record that lives in accounts has the family's mobility only where logins, connections, and sessions cooperate.

**April — testing, evaluations, and evidence under mild pressure.** April brings the year's formal instruments: the standardised testing many programmes require or recommend, the booking of the end-of-year evaluation, the co-op's spring showcase. The vault holds the test registration documents, the previous years' results filed in their own vaults for comparison, and the growing body of work from which evaluation samples will be drawn. When the evaluator — a certified teacher who reviews homeschool portfolios — asks in advance what format she will receive the portfolio in, the parents can answer with unusual precision, because they have been building it all year: a single assembled PDF for the formal submission, drawn from the vault, with the complete underlying record available if she wishes to see more. April is also, in many families, the month of doubt, when parents look at the year and wonder whether it adds up. There is a particular comfort, in that month, in a record that can simply be searched, browsed, and read back: the year, whole, in the family's own words and the children's own work. Doubt thrives on vagueness. The vault is specific.

**May — assembly, almost anticlimactic.** May is portfolio assembly month, and in this family it is — the word the parents use, with some wonder — boring. The Reader view lets them review the year's documents as documents, page by page, the way a reviewer will. The PDF tools do the mechanical work: merging the final selections, splitting out the sections the umbrella programme wants separately, reordering pages without rescanning anything. Photographs chosen for the portfolio carry their EXIF dates into the captions the parents write, so the visual chronology of the younger child's projects is accurate to the day. The older child's transcript draft, maintained in the vault since September and revised under version history all year, gets its final subjects, grades, and credit decisions — each change preserved, so that if a question arises next year about how a grade was determined, the record of the decision and its predecessors exists. May's boringness is the year's verdict: the portfolio was not assembled in May. It was accumulated from September, and May merely harvested it.

**June — the review, and the closing of the file.** June is the review itself: the evaluator's meeting, the umbrella programme's acceptance of the portfolio, the co-op's end-of-year celebration, and the family's own private ceremony of closing the year. The review scene that ends this article returns to this month, so here it is enough to record the administrative facts. The portfolio is accepted. The evaluation letter — the single most important document of the year — goes into the vault the day it arrives, scanned, OCR'd, indexed, and filed alongside the transcript it validates. And then the parents perform the year's most important ten minutes: they close the vault. The file is finalised, copied to every backup place the family maintains, and left complete and untouched, the permanent record of a year of a child's life and education. Next September will have its own file. This one is history now — findable, readable, encrypted, and theirs. A year in the vault ends the way it began, with an evening of deliberate, unhurried order — and with the deep, unglamorous satisfaction of a family that can prove, show, and remember exactly what it did.

## What VaultBook does not do for a homeschool family

An honest account of any tool must include the shape of its edges, and VaultBook's edges are not accidental shortcomings — most of them are the direct price of the properties this article has praised. Custody and collaboration pull in opposite directions. Independence and automation pull in opposite directions. A family choosing the vault should choose it with its eyes open about what it is not getting, and this section exists so that no reader can say the case was made by omission.

First and largest: VaultBook offers no live co-teaching collaboration. There is no shared cursor, no simultaneous editing, no comment thread updating in real time between a parent and a tutor, no co-op planning page that thirty families refine together over an evening. When this family's co-op builds its shared resource library or a tutor annotates a draft with a student in real time, they use other tools — the leaders named in the previous section do this well, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest. What the vault provides instead is the deliberate, asynchronous pattern: work is copied out for collaboration and the finished result is copied back into the record. Many families find that pattern not merely acceptable but preferable for the formal record — the archive stays clean, single-authored in its custody, and free of other people's edits — but a family whose educational model is built around live, multi-adult collaboration all day will experience the vault as a complement to its real workspace, not a replacement for it. That is a legitimate architecture. It is simply not the architecture for the archive itself.

Second: VaultBook does not file anything with the state, the district, or the umbrella programme. It will not submit the notice of intent, transmit the portfolio, renew the medical form, or chase the programme's portal on the family's behalf. The calendar and the Due/Expiring sidebar will warn, clearly and early, that a deadline approaches and a document expires; the PDF tools will assemble exactly the submission required; the vault will hold the proof of what was sent and when the family sent it. But the act of filing remains the family's act, performed through whatever channel the authority requires. This is, again, the shadow of a virtue: a tool that makes zero network requests cannot also transact with government portals, and a family should be clear-eyed that the vault is the record room, not the courier. Families who want maximum automation of compliance will need to supply the diligence themselves — the vault makes that diligence dramatically easier to exercise, and no more automatic than that.

Third: handwriting is not the vault's studio. VaultBook will faithfully keep photographs and scans of handwritten work, index them, OCR them into searchability, and preserve them as portfolio evidence for decades — but the act of handwriting itself, with a pen on a surface that feels like paper, belongs to the tools that specialise in it. Goodnotes and Samsung Notes, named earlier, are better at being written in, and a child who loves writing by hand should write by hand in the environment built for it. The vault is where the term's handwritten work comes to be kept, found, and presented — the archive, not the desk. Families should also note the honest corollary: photographed handwriting is only as legible to OCR as the handwriting and the photograph allow, and the family's own titles and summaries on entries remain the most reliable finding aids for a child's most idiosyncratic pages.

Fourth, and most important to state without flinching: VaultBook provides no cloud backup, because there is no cloud. The single file that constitutes the family's record exists where the family puts it — and if the family puts it in exactly one place, and that place fails, the record is gone with a completeness no vendor outage could match. Custody is responsibility. The discipline this article's family practises — the working copy on the main machine, the external drive copy refreshed monthly, the copy held by a trusted relative, the December and June backup reviews, the passphrase recorded once, carefully, where both parents agreed — is not an optional garnish on the vault approach. It is half of the approach. Families unwilling to maintain even that modest routine should think carefully: a reputable cloud service's redundancy is a real good, honestly weighed, and for some households it will outweigh custody. The vault's claim is for families who accept the trade knowingly and prefer their record's survival to depend on their own habits rather than on a company's continuity — habits that, once built in September, take minutes a month to keep.

Fifth, a cluster of smaller honesties that deserve naming in one breath. The vault will not teach: it contains no curriculum, no lessons, no adaptive exercises, and the education in this article happened at the table, not in the software. It will not communicate: messages to the co-op, the evaluator, and the programme still travel by the family's ordinary channels. Its search, however capable — full-text, OCR, attachment indexing, semantic questions — is only as good as what the family actually put in, and a term of unphotographed work is a term the vault cannot conjure back. Its encryption protects entries at rest under the family's passphrase, which means a weak passphrase weakens it and a lost passphrase, for protected entries, is final — there is no provider holding a reset key, because there is no provider, and that finality is the same property, viewed from the other side, as the privacy the family chose. None of these limits is hidden in fine print. They are the load-bearing walls of the design, and a family that can look at them and nod — collaboration elsewhere, filing by hand, handwriting at its own desk, backups as a habit, passphrase guarded like a house key — is exactly the family the vault was built for.

## The portfolio review, revisited — closing scene + practical checklist to start this week

Return, finally, to the room where this article began: a portfolio review, a reviewer with limited time and a professional eye, and a family presenting the evidence of its year. But it is June now, not some anxious rehearsal of June, and the scene plays differently from the way such scenes play in the households that assembled their evidence in a panic the week before.

The evaluator arrives with her notebook and her questions, and the first thing she asks for is the shape of the year: attendance, subjects, the plan and how it changed. The parents do not produce a folder of loose certainties. They open the vault — on their own laptop, offline, in a room where nobody has asked for a Wi-Fi password — and the year is simply there. Attendance, complete and honest, including December's broken weeks. The subjects, each with its samples. The plan of September, preserved in version history alongside the revisions of January and March, so the reviewer can see not a family pretending the year went as scripted, but a family that planned, adapted, and recorded the adapting. She asks about the younger child's reading, and instead of an assurance she gets a progression: the October narration transcripts, halting and charming; the February turning point the semantic search surfaces in seconds; the spring fluency, in the child's own recorded voice and its local transcription. She asks about the older child's research project, and the Kanban board's finished columns, the drafts under their versions, and the final paper tell the story of a piece of work, not just its conclusion. She asks — evaluators always ask — about the one awkward gap, the subject that thinned out in the spring, and the parents can answer her from the record rather than around it, because the vault made honesty cheap: the gap is visible, its reasons are visible, and the plan to remedy it next year is already an entry with a date on it.

What she does not see is almost as important as what she does. She does not see the family's medical forms, because those entries sit encrypted and unopened, present in the record's structure without being displayed to a reviewer who has no need of them — per-entry encryption proving, in a real room, that privacy in the vault is not all-or-nothing but chosen document by document. She does not see other families' children, other years' records, or anything beyond the scope of the review, because the family shares by showing, on its own screen, exactly what the occasion requires. And when she asks for the formal submission to take away — the portfolio document her programme files — the parents hand her the PDF assembled in May, merged and ordered, identical in content to the record she has just inspected, while the complete original stays where it has always stayed: in the family's custody, in a single file, in their home. She signs the evaluation. The letter is scanned into the vault before the parents have finished their tea, OCR'd and filed beside the transcript it validates, and the year is complete in the only sense that finally matters: it can be shown, proved, and remembered, in full, by the people to whom it belongs.

If this scene is the one a family wants, the distance between here and there is not talent, money, or a free summer. It is a week of small, deliberate beginnings — and the checklist below is written to be started this week, in the middle of whatever term the family is currently in, because the vault rewards starting now far more than it rewards starting perfectly.

**This week's checklist — starting the family vault.**

*Day one: create the vault and set the passphrase properly.* Create the VaultBook vault file, name it for the family and the school year, and choose a passphrase with the seriousness the next eighteen years deserve — long, unique, memorable to the parents, written down once and stored where the family keeps its essential documents. Decide, in the same sitting, which entries will be encrypted as a matter of routine: medical and legal documents, evaluations, anything carrying sensitive personal detail. Make the decision once, as policy, so it never has to be improvised per document.

*Day two: build the year's skeleton.* Create the structure before filling it: a section per child; within each, the subjects actually being studied; and one administrative section for the year's spine — attendance, agreements, forms, correspondence. Add the standing documents that already exist, wherever they currently live: the notice of intent or programme agreement, current medical forms, last year's evaluation letter. Photograph or scan what exists only on paper. Ten documents, properly filed, beat a hundred documents in a pile.

*Day three: load the calendar with every fixed point.* Enter every deadline and expiry the family already knows: programme submissions, evaluation windows, co-op term dates, form expirations, testing registrations. Then look at the Due/Expiring sidebar and enjoy, possibly for the first time, the sight of the whole year's obligations visible at once, counting down without any parent having to remember them. Every future deadline enters the calendar the day it is learned; that single habit is worth half the system.

*Day four: start the capture rhythm, small and daily.* Photograph the day's finished work before it leaves the table — a minute, no more. Write the day's attendance entry. Capture one narration, one reading session, one piece of work in progress. The rhythm matters more than the volume: a family that captures a little every day arrives in June with a portfolio; a family that plans to capture everything in May arrives in May with a project. Use the Photo/Video Explorer as the pictures accumulate, and let the EXIF dates carry the chronology so no one has to reconstruct it later.

*Day five: set up planning where the record lives.* Create one Kanban board per child for the current term — columns as simple as "this week," "in progress," and "done" for the younger child; a project-shaped board for the older one. Move one real piece of work across it. Planning inside the vault, beside the evidence, is what turns the file from an archive of the past into an instrument for the coming week.

*Day six: make the copies, and write down where they are.* Copy the vault file to the family's external drive. Make the second copy that will live outside the house — with a trusted relative, in the family's safe place — and record, in the vault's administrative section itself, where every copy lives and when each was last refreshed. Put a monthly ten-minute backup appointment on the calendar, next to the other fixed points. Custody is responsibility, and this is the hour of the week where the family accepts it, concretely and calmly.

*Day seven: rehearse the worst day.* Open the vault on a different machine — a borrowed laptop, the other parent's computer — with the network off. Find, by search alone, three things: a document from the administrative section, a piece of work from October or whenever the year began, and the next deadline. Ask the vault one plain-language question about the children's work and read what it gathers. If the rehearsal takes five minutes and everything is found, the system is real. If something is missing, it is missing in a rehearsal, in a quiet week, where it can be fixed — which is the entire philosophy of the vault in a single exercise.

A family that finishes this week will not have finished the work; it will have started the year — any year, begun in any month — the way the family in this article learned to start it: with the record in its own hands, encrypted where it should be, searchable to the last photographed page, indifferent to outages, accounts, and ecosystems, and quietly accumulating the evidence of an education. The portfolio review, when it comes, will not be a performance assembled for an audience. It will be what it was in that June room: a family opening its own book, on its own machine, and turning the pages of a year it can stand behind — every page present, every date true, every document exactly where the family put it, in the only custody that has never once gone dark.
