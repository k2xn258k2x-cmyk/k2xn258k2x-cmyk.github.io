The enemy of any record is friction, and friction is measured in seconds rather than in effort. A thought arriving in the middle of something else has a working life of perhaps a minute before it changes shape or disappears entirely, and every action standing between the thought and the record is an opportunity for it to escape. Open the application. Find the right place. Compose a title. Type the content. Decide where it belongs. Each of those is trivial in isolation and together they are considerably longer than the thought will survive.

That arithmetic is why so much professional knowledge never gets written down at all, and why the people it happens to almost always blame their memory. Somebody who loses an observation four times a week for twenty years concludes they are forgetful. What is actually happening is an economic decision made below the level of awareness: the cost of recording exceeded what the thought seemed worth in that moment, so the thought lost. Lower the cost and the same person captures constantly, with no change to their memory and no additional discipline.

![Threads: The Fastest Way to Capture a Thought](/assets/img/feature-screenshot-placeholder.svg)

Most tools for keeping notes are designed for composition, and this is not a criticism. A note with a title, a body, a location in some structure, and perhaps a category is exactly right for deliberate work: sitting down to write something, thinking about where it belongs, and producing a record intended to be read. That model assumes a person with attention available and a decision to make, and for a substantial part of anybody's writing it is precisely correct.
<!--more-->


It is entirely wrong for the other situation, which occurs constantly in professional life and is rarely designed for. Somebody is doing something else. Their hands may be occupied, their attention is committed elsewhere, and something has just happened or occurred to them that will matter later. They have a few seconds, not a few minutes, and the few seconds are borrowed from an activity that has not paused. Asking that person to choose a location and compose a title is asking them to perform an act of organisation while they are in no position to organise anything.

What such a moment needs is a surface with no decisions in it. Somewhere to type a line, immediately, with no question about where it goes or what it is called, and with the time recorded automatically because a person in that state cannot reliably supply it and frequently does not know it. The form that produces this most naturally is one everybody already understands: a message. Nobody has ever had to learn how to send one, nobody titles a message, and nobody decides which folder it belongs in.

The second property that matters is sequence. A single quick note is a familiar thing and adequate for a thought that stands alone. A great deal of professional observation does not stand alone: it accumulates across a shift, a session, a day of shooting, or a period of concentrated activity, and the relationship between the third observation and the first is frequently the information. Capturing them as a series, in order, with times attached, preserves something that eight separate notes would destroy.

The third is that this only works if the material is processed. A capture surface with nothing downstream of it becomes a second inbox, which is the failure mode of every quick-entry mechanism ever built. The captured line is not the record; it is the raw material from which the record gets made later, when somebody has attention available, and the discipline that makes the whole arrangement work is a fixed point at which that happens.

What follows is a conversation between two people whose working conditions make this unusually stark, one of whom is at sea and one of whom is standing in an agave field.


## Messages sent across eleven hours

The exchange began in the group, in fragments, because Nkechi was in the South China Sea and Raj was in Oaxaca and their waking hours had almost no overlap.

Raj posted first, at the end of a shooting day: I have just typed four things into a chat window while a man waited for me to stop typing so he could show me something, and it occurred to me that this is now how I work and I have never examined it.

Nkechi replied about nine hours later: I have been working this way for two years and I would find it difficult to explain to somebody why it matters. Say what your four things were.

He listed them. A shot that was underexposed and would need to be redone from a different angle. An interview that had gone somewhere unplanned at about the seven minute mark and had become the best material of the week. A note about light in a field at a particular hour. And one line reading simply ask about the grandmother, which he said he could not now reconstruct the reasoning behind and which had turned out to matter enormously.

Nkechi wrote: three of those are observations and one is a question you asked yourself. Mine are the same mixture and I had not noticed until you listed yours.

They went back and forth like that for two days before their hours aligned sufficiently for a call, which Nkechi warned would be poor and which was.

‘I can hear you and I can see approximately a third of you,’ Raj said.

‘That is a better connection than I had last month.’ There was a sound behind her that he did not ask about. ‘I have about forty minutes and then I am on.’

‘Then let us not do the polite part.’

‘I would prefer that. Say what you actually want to know.’

‘I want to know whether you use it the way I use it,’ Raj said, ‘because I have a suspicion that we do the same thing for completely different reasons and I would like to find out which parts are the reasons and which parts are the thing.’


## Four hours on a bridge

‘Describe a watch,’ Raj said. ‘Because I have no picture of it at all.’

‘Four hours, at night, on the bridge, and I am responsible for the vessel during them.’ Nkechi said it plainly. ‘Which means monitoring instruments, watching the radar, maintaining a lookout, and being the person who decides things if anything requires deciding.’

‘And things happen.’

‘Things happen constantly and almost none of them are events.’ She paused. ‘That is the part people misunderstand. A watch is not a sequence of incidents. It is four hours of ordinary observation with perhaps three things in it that will matter later, and I do not know which three until afterward.’

‘Say what one looks like.’

‘A contact appears on the radar at an unusual bearing and behaves in a way I would not expect.’ She counted. ‘A reading on a piece of equipment sits slightly outside where it usually sits. The weather does something a little before the forecast said it would.’

‘None of which is an emergency.’

‘None of which is an emergency and every one of which I will want in six weeks if something develops.’ Nkechi was definite. ‘An equipment reading that is slightly off on three consecutive watches is a maintenance issue. The same reading once is nothing. And I cannot tell which it is at the moment I see it.’

‘So you record everything.’

‘I record everything notable, which is perhaps eight or nine lines in four hours, and the discipline is that I do not decide whether something is worth recording.’

Raj asked the question he had come with.

‘How long does it take you?’

‘Eleven seconds,’ she said, without hesitation. ‘I have counted, because I wanted to know.’

‘Say what happens in those eleven seconds.’

‘I open the overlay, which appears in front of whatever I was looking at, and I type a line, and I close it.’ She described the gesture. ‘There is no location to choose. There is no title. There is no decision of any kind, and that is the entire reason it works.’

‘And the time?’

‘The time is recorded and I did not supply it, which matters more than it sounds.’ Nkechi was emphatic about this. ‘Ask me what time it is right now.’

‘What time is it?’

‘I would have to look, and I have been awake and working for three hours.’ She laughed slightly. ‘A person on a rotating watch has a genuinely unreliable relationship with the clock, and a person writing something up afterward supplies an approximate time and believes it is accurate.’

‘And the difference between accurate and approximate?’

‘Is the difference between a record and a recollection, and only one of those is any use in an inquiry.’ She said it without drama. ‘I have needed the actual times twice in nine years. Both times it mattered and both times I would have supplied something wrong from memory.’

Raj was quiet for a second.

‘Say what the end of the watch looks like.’

‘Twenty minutes, in my cabin, before I sleep.’ She described it. ‘I read back what is there, which is eight or nine lines with times on them, and I turn perhaps two of them into proper entries.’

‘Only two?’

‘Only two, and the other seven are correct to have captured and correct to leave.’ Nkechi shrugged. ‘An observation that turns out to be nothing is not a failure. It is the cost of not having had to judge at the moment I saw it.’

‘What proportion becomes something eventually?’

‘Perhaps a quarter, across everything.’ She thought about it. ‘Which sounds wasteful and is the correct ratio, because the alternative is judging at the point of observation and I have already told you I cannot.’

‘And the two that become entries.’

‘Become proper records with sections, in the right place, with anything attached that should be attached.’ She was definite. ‘An equipment anomaly becomes an inspection entry. A weather observation becomes part of the voyage record. And they are considerably better entries than they would have been if I had written them at the time, because I am sitting down and I know what happened next.’


## Thirty seconds between takes

‘Now yours,’ Nkechi said. ‘Because I imagine a film set as calm and I suspect that is wrong.’

‘A film set is the least calm environment I have ever worked in and everybody on it is pretending otherwise,’ Raj said.

‘Describe the window.’

‘Thirty seconds, sometimes less.’ He counted. ‘Between one setup and the next, while somebody is moving a light or an interviewee is having water. That is when I have my hands free and my head available, and it ends when somebody says they are ready.’

‘And what arrives in that window?’

‘Three kinds of thing and I only worked out recently that they are different.’ He listed them. ‘Technical notes, which are about what we just did and what is wrong with it. Editorial notes, which are about what something means and where it might go. And questions to myself, which are the most valuable and which look like nothing at the time.’

‘Say the technical version.’

‘Shot fourteen was underexposed and needs redoing from a different angle.’ He shrugged. ‘Which I will absolutely have forgotten by the wrap, because between now and the wrap there will be forty more shots.’

‘And the editorial.’

‘An interview went somewhere unplanned at about seven minutes and became the best material of the fortnight.’ Raj was animated. ‘Which I know at the time, standing there, and which I will remember imprecisely by the evening. Was it seven minutes or was it eleven. Was it after the question about her mother or before it.’

‘Whereas a line typed at the moment.’

‘Whereas a line typed at the moment says seven and says which question, and the timecode on that interview is a thing I will be looking for in eight months in an edit suite in another country.’

Nkechi asked about the third kind.

‘Say what a question to yourself looks like.’

‘Ask about the grandmother.’ He said it plainly. ‘Four words. I typed them on the second day of the shoot and I could not tell you now what prompted it.’

‘And?’

‘And I asked, three weeks later, because it was sitting in the log, and the answer restructured the entire film.’ Raj was quiet for a second. ‘She had a story about her grandmother and a palenque that closed in the sixties, and it is now the spine of the whole thing.’

‘You would not have asked.’

‘I would absolutely not have asked, because whatever produced that instinct on day two was gone by day three.’ He shook his head. ‘That is the case I use when anybody asks me why I bother, and it is one line of four words that I did not understand when I wrote it.’

‘Do you type these while people are waiting?’

‘Constantly, and I have stopped apologising for it.’ Raj laughed. ‘I used to say sorry, one second, and now I just do it, because eleven seconds of somebody waiting is not a cost and losing the observation is.’

‘How many in a day?’

‘Thirty or forty on a shooting day, which sounds enormous and is about eight minutes of total typing.’

‘And in the evening?’

‘In the evening, at the guesthouse, I go through them and turn them into things.’ He described it. ‘Scene entries. Interview summaries with the timecodes in them. A catalogue of what we have shot that is not people talking. And a much shorter list of things I am now going to do differently.’

‘Which takes how long?’

‘Forty minutes on a good day and I do it every single day without exception, because the one time I skipped it I lost most of a day's context.’ He was definite. ‘Not the lines. The lines were there. But I could not remember what half of them referred to, forty-eight hours later, and a line reading the light in the second field is meaningless once you have been in six fields.’


## What each of them writes in a line

‘I want to ask something specific,’ Nkechi said, ‘because I have read your four lines and mine look different and I want to know whether that is the profession or the person.’

‘Say what yours look like.’

‘Almost telegraphic.’ She read a few. ‘Contact, port bow, unusual bearing, not responding. Number two generator, temperature high side of normal. Wind backing earlier than forecast.’

‘Those are considerably shorter than mine.’

‘They are shorter because I am writing to a version of myself who will read them in ninety minutes,’ Nkechi said. ‘Not in a year. In ninety minutes, in my cabin, when I still remember the shape of the watch.’

‘Whereas mine.’

‘Yours read as though you might come back in a month.’

Raj thought about it.

‘That is true and I had not noticed,’ he said. ‘Because I do come back in a month. The evening processing produces the entries, and the entries are what I use during the shoot, and then the whole log gets reread in the edit, which is eight months later.’

‘So you have two readers.’

‘Two readers with completely different amounts of context, and I have been writing for the second one without deciding to.’ He was working it out. ‘Which is why my lines are longer and why they contain things that are obvious to me right now.’

‘Give me an example of an obvious thing you include.’

‘Which field. Which interview. Whose house.’ He counted. ‘All of which I know perfectly well while typing and none of which I will know in the edit suite, because there will have been eleven fields and forty interviews.’

‘Then that is not padding, that is addressing.’

‘Say more.’

‘Every line is addressed to somebody, and the amount of context you include is a function of who that is.’ Nkechi was precise. ‘Mine go to a person ninety minutes away who was there. Yours go to a person eight months away who has forgotten.’

Raj wrote something down.

‘Then I have been doing this correctly by accident,’ he said, ‘and I would like to do it deliberately, because there are lines in my logs that fail the test.’

‘Which ones?’

‘The technical ones.’ He winced. ‘Shot fourteen underexposed. Which is perfectly clear on the day and completely opaque in an edit, because there is a shot fourteen on every one of forty shooting days.’

‘So they need the day.’

‘They need the day or the setup or something, and the timestamp gives me the day if I go looking, which is a step I will not take when I am scanning three hundred lines.’ He shrugged. ‘Four more characters at the moment of typing. That is the fix.’

Nkechi was quiet for a moment.

‘I have the reverse problem and I have just recognised it,’ she said.

‘Say it.’

‘My lines are written for a reader ninety minutes away, and my logs are kept for years, and occasionally somebody asks me about a voyage from two contracts ago.’ She was frank. ‘And I open a log and it says number two generator, temperature high side of normal, and I do not know which vessel that was on.’

‘Because you never needed to write it.’

‘Because at the time it was the only vessel I could possibly have meant.’ She laughed without much humour. ‘Which is a fact about the moment rather than about the record, and I have been treating a permanent thing as though it were temporary for two years.’


## Three ways in, and when each is right

‘I want to compare this against the other ways of getting something in,’ Nkechi said, ‘because I use all three and I have never articulated the boundaries.’

‘Say what you use.’

‘The full editor, the round button, and this.’ She counted. ‘And I use them in completely different situations without thinking about it, which means there is a rule I have not written down.’

‘Then let us find it.’

‘The full editor is for when I am composing.’ Nkechi described it. ‘A formal inspection report. A certification study note that is going to be substantial. Anything where I am deciding the structure as part of writing it, with sections and files and a place in the hierarchy.’

‘Which requires attention.’

‘Which requires attention and a table and no alarms, and which I do perhaps four times a week.’

‘And the button?’

‘One thing, standing alone, when I have thirty seconds and no sequence.’ She was thinking as she spoke. ‘Something occurs to me about the master mariner examination while I am walking to the mess. One press, write it, close it. It becomes an entry immediately.’

‘Whereas mine would go in the log.’

‘Whereas yours would go in the log because you are in the middle of something that will produce more of them.’ Nkechi paused. ‘That is the distinction and I have just heard myself say it. The button is for one thing. The log is for one of several things.’

Raj sat back.

‘Say that again, because I think you have just solved something I have been circling for a year.’

‘The button produces an entry. The log produces a line in a sequence.’ She was definite now. ‘If the thing I am capturing is complete in itself, it should be an entry, and making it one immediately saves me processing it later. If it is one observation among several arriving over a period, it belongs in the sequence, because the sequence is part of the information.’

‘Which is exactly why I stopped using the button on shoots.’

‘Say what happened.’

‘I used it for the first two productions and it produced forty separate entries a day, each with a title I had invented in three seconds, in no order, all of which I then had to gather.’ Raj was rueful. ‘Which is not capture, it is fragmentation, and it took me a whole production to work out that the problem was not the button.’

‘The problem was that your observations were not standalone.’

‘The problem was that my observations were a sequence and I was destroying the sequence at the moment of capture.’ He nodded. ‘And I still use the button constantly during editing, because during an edit an idea genuinely is one thing.’

They compared the third case, which both said they had got wrong at some point.

‘I tried to use the log for composition once,’ Nkechi said.

‘How did that go?’

‘I wrote an inspection report as eleven consecutive lines in a chat window, on the reasonable grounds that I was already there.’ She laughed at herself. ‘And it is a terrible inspection report, because a report has a structure and eleven messages have an order, and those are not the same thing.’

‘Order against structure.’

‘Order is when things happened. Structure is what kind of thing each part is.’ She was precise. ‘A report needs the second and the log gives you the first, and I was trying to make one do the other because I could not be bothered to move.’

‘That is a rule as well,’ Raj said. ‘If the thing needs a shape, it needs the editor. If it needs a time, it needs the log.’


## The half that nobody talks about

‘Now the part where this fails,’ Raj said, ‘because we have both been enthusiastic for half an hour and I want to be honest.’

‘Processing.’

‘Processing, and I want to say plainly that this whole arrangement is worthless without it.’

‘Say why.’

‘Because captured lines are not knowledge, they are raw material.’ He was definite. ‘A log with four hundred unprocessed lines in it is a second inbox, and a second inbox is worse than no inbox, because you now feel guilty about a place you never open.’

‘I have watched somebody do exactly that,’ Nkechi said. ‘A colleague who adopted this and captured beautifully for about six weeks and never once went back.’

‘And?’

‘And he stopped capturing, because it had become a place where things went to be forgotten in a slightly more organised way.’ She shrugged. ‘Which is worse than his previous arrangement, because at least when he was forgetting things he knew he was forgetting them.’

‘Then say your discipline, precisely.’

‘End of every watch. Twenty minutes. Before I sleep, without exception, including the watches where nothing happened.’ Nkechi counted. ‘And the exception-free part is the whole of it, because the moment I allow myself one skipped session the next one is easier to skip.’

‘Mine is every evening and I have skipped it once in two years.’

‘What happened?’

‘I lost about a day of context, which I have already described, and it was sufficiently annoying that it has never happened again.’ Raj shrugged. ‘Which is a more effective enforcement mechanism than any resolution.’

They discussed what processing actually involves, which Nkechi said was less than people expect.

‘I am not writing everything up,’ she said. ‘I am reading eight lines and deciding about each one, and most of the decisions are no.’

‘So it is triage.’

‘It is triage performed at the right moment, which is the entire point.’ She was emphatic. ‘I could not triage at the moment of observation. I can triage twenty minutes later, when I know what happened for the rest of the watch, and the judgment is considerably better.’

‘That is the argument I have never been able to make to people.’

‘Say it as you would to them.’

‘The capture is deliberately indiscriminate so that the judgment can happen when you are competent to make it.’ Raj was working it out. ‘Which means the low ratio is not waste. It is the mechanism.’

‘And the lines that become nothing?’

‘Stay in the log, which I keep, because occasionally I go back to a shooting day months later and the seven lines I did not use turn out to explain something.’ He shrugged. ‘They cost nothing. They are lines of text.’

Nkechi raised the thing she said she had got wrong for a year.

‘I used to delete the ones I had processed.’

‘Why?’

‘Tidiness, which is not a reason.’ She was blunt about it. ‘And then I needed a sequence once, for a report, and I had the two entries I had made and no record of the six observations around them, which is exactly the context that would have answered the question.’

‘So you keep everything now.’

‘I keep everything, and the log for a voyage is a complete record of every watch, and the entries are the things that needed to be findable on their own.’ She paused. ‘Which is two layers and I only need the second one most of the time, and the first one has saved me twice.’


## Who else this would suit

‘Can we speculate about the others?’ Raj said. ‘Because I have been thinking about who in the group would use this and I suspect several of them would and do not know it.’

‘Start with the one you are confident about.’

‘Tomas.’ Raj was immediate. ‘A laboratory session with thirty teenagers in it, and a student asks something genuinely good, and he cannot stop to write it down because he is supervising something that can burn somebody.’

‘And by the end of the period?’

‘By the end of the period there have been six good questions and he remembers two, and the two he remembers are the ones asked most recently.’ He shrugged. ‘Which is a recency effect rather than a quality judgment.’

‘Whereas eleven seconds.’

‘Whereas eleven seconds while the class is working, six times in a period, and he has all six and can decide which of them changes how he teaches it next year.’

Nkechi offered one.

‘Meera,’ she said. ‘And this one is more delicate and I want to be careful about it.’

‘Go on.’

‘She is in a room with somebody who is telling her something difficult, and she is maintaining eye contact, and something occurs to her that is clinically significant.’ Nkechi described it. ‘She cannot compose a note. She cannot look away for a minute. And if she waits until the end of the appointment she has forty-five more minutes of material on top of it.’

‘So a phrase.’

‘A phrase, typed in a few seconds, at a moment when she is already writing something anyway, which is normal in that room and does not break anything.’ She paused. ‘And the full record gets written afterward, properly, from a memory that has been given three anchors.’

‘That is a considerably more careful use than mine.’

‘It is the same use with higher stakes,’ Nkechi said. ‘She is not recording an observation. She is preserving the thing she noticed before her own attention overwrites it.’

Raj offered two more.

‘Amir,’ he said. ‘Behind a counter, with somebody waiting, and a customer has just said something about a product that he will absolutely have forgotten by the afternoon.’

‘He writes at the end of the day.’

‘He writes four lines at the end of the day, which I know because he has told the group about it at some length.’ Raj grinned. ‘And I would bet a considerable amount that at least half of what he intends to write in the evening is gone by four in the afternoon, and he has no idea, because you cannot notice what you have forgotten.’

‘And the fourth?’

‘David, and his is the one I find most affecting.’ Raj was more careful. ‘He visits people in hospitals and sits with families, and the details of those conversations are the entire substance of his work with that person for the next several years.’

‘And he writes them afterward.’

‘He writes them afterward, in a car park, on a phone, which he has described.’ He paused. ‘And the difference between writing them in a car park and writing them in an office three hours later is enormous, and he found that out by accident rather than by design.’

Nkechi was quiet for a moment.

‘There is something common to all four,’ she said.

‘Say it.’

‘None of them is at a desk, and none of them can pause what they are doing, and all four are in a situation where the thing worth recording is produced by another person rather than by them.’ She was working it out. ‘Which is a different problem from having an idea. An idea will come back. Something somebody said will not.’


## Nothing is waiting to be sent

‘One more thing and it is the one that makes the whole arrangement possible for me,’ Nkechi said.

‘Connectivity.’

‘I am in the South China Sea. There is no connection, and there will not be one until we are alongside, and that is nine days.’ She said it as an ordinary fact. ‘Which means anything that needs to reach something else is not a tool I can consider.’

‘Say what happens when you type a line.’

‘It is saved. Immediately, locally, on the machine.’ She was precise. ‘There is no pending state. There is no queue. There is nothing waiting to be sent when the connection returns, because nothing is going anywhere.’

‘And when you close it?’

‘When I close it the material is on disk, and if the machine loses power in the next second the line is still there.’ Nkechi shrugged. ‘Which I have tested, because I am the kind of person who tests things, and because power is not entirely reliable on an older vessel.’

‘Mine is different in cause and identical in effect,’ Raj said. ‘An agave field forty minutes outside a town with no service of any kind.’

‘Which is where you work.’

‘Which is where the entire production happens, and there is a version of this problem where I would be capturing into something that told me it would save when I was back in range.’ He shook his head. ‘And I would not trust it, and not trusting it would mean not using it, and not using it means losing the material.’

‘The trust is the point rather than the mechanism.’

‘The trust is entirely the point.’ Raj was definite. ‘Because I am typing four words while a man waits for me. If there is any part of my head asking whether that was actually saved, the whole thing collapses, because I will start checking, and checking costs more than the capture did.’

They discussed the saving behaviour, which both had observed and neither had thought about deliberately.

‘There is a small indicator that tells me the state,’ Nkechi said. ‘And it took me about three months to stop looking at it.’

‘Say why you stopped.’

‘Because I had looked at it enough times to know what it does, which is the only route to trusting anything.’ She was matter of fact. ‘I watched it for three months. It was always correct. Now I do not look, and the not looking is what makes eleven seconds possible.’

‘That is the same thing Suki said about something else entirely,’ Raj said.

‘Observe, test, then rely. She says it constantly and she is right.’ Nkechi nodded. ‘And the corollary is that a mechanism which gives you nothing to observe cannot be trusted in the same way, because you never get to complete the sequence.’

Raj asked the practical question.

‘Does it slow down as the log grows?’

‘I have a voyage log with several hundred lines in it and it opens instantly.’ She shrugged. ‘Which I would not have raised except that you asked, because I stopped wondering about it a long time ago.’


## Finding a line two years later

‘One thing I have never asked anybody,’ Raj said. ‘Can you search these?’

‘Can you not?’

‘I have never tried, which having said it out loud sounds absurd.’

‘Then try it now,’ Nkechi said. ‘Search something you know is only in a log line.’

He thought, and typed the name of a village.

Results came back.

‘Those are lines,’ he said slowly. ‘From three different shooting days, on two productions.’

‘Say what they are.’

‘One is from a recce two years ago on a project that never happened. One is from this shoot, last week. And one is a note I made about somebody who lives there, which I had entirely forgotten.’ He was scrolling. ‘And the third one is the useful one and I have no memory of writing it.’

‘Which is the general case.’

‘Which is the general case, and I have been treating my logs as a temporary staging area that gets emptied into entries.’ Raj shook his head. ‘They are not a staging area. They are a searchable record of everything I have observed in the field for two years, and I have been ignoring half of what I own.’

Nkechi described her own version.

‘I search mine perhaps once a month and always for the same kind of thing,’ she said. ‘A piece of equipment.’

‘Say why.’

‘Because an equipment problem is never a single event and I never know that at the time.’ She was definite. ‘Somebody asks whether a particular pump has been giving trouble, and my honest answer from memory is that I think so, occasionally.’

‘And from the log?’

‘From the log the answer is that it has appeared in eleven lines across four months, on watches worked by me, and the interval between them is shortening.’ Nkechi paused. ‘Which is a completely different answer and it is the one that gets somebody to look at it.’

‘That is not a note. That is evidence.’

‘That is evidence produced by eleven observations that individually meant nothing, none of which I would have written up as an entry, all of which I captured because I do not judge at the moment of capture.’ She was emphatic. ‘Which is the strongest argument I have for the whole practice and I have never put it that way before.’

Raj was quiet.

‘So the unprocessed material is not waste.’

‘The unprocessed material is a dataset,’ Nkechi said. ‘The processed material is the entries. And I spent a year deleting the dataset because it looked untidy.’


## What each of them changed

‘I have one thing I am taking,’ Raj said, ‘and it is yours.’

‘Say it.’

‘Keeping everything.’ He was slightly rueful. ‘I have been deleting processed lines for two years, on exactly the tidiness grounds you described, and I have just realised that I have destroyed the sequence for every shooting day I have ever worked.’

‘You have the entries.’

‘I have the entries, which are the things I judged worth making, decided in the evening, at the end of a long day.’ He shook his head. ‘And what is gone is the seven things around each of them, in order, with times, which is exactly the material that would tell me why I made a decision I now cannot explain.’

‘Does that happen?’

‘It happens on every production, in the edit, eight months later.’ Raj was definite. ‘I look at a scene and cannot reconstruct why we shot it the way we did, and I have a summary I wrote that evening which describes the decision and not the circumstances.’

‘Then keep everything from now.’

‘Keeping everything from now, and I am not going to pretend I can recover what is gone.’

Nkechi's was smaller and she said it was overdue.

‘I am going to use the button more,’ she said.

‘Say why that is a change.’

‘Because I put everything in the log, including things that are standalone, on the grounds that the log is already open.’ She was frank. ‘Which means I create work for myself in the processing session that need not exist. A study note about a chart correction is complete when I type it. It does not need to be a line I later convert into an entry.’

‘So you are moving some capture out of the sequence.’

‘I am moving the standalone things out of the sequence, which should reduce my processing by perhaps a third, and which I would not have thought about if you had not asked me to name the boundaries.’ She paused. ‘Which is the actual reason to have this conversation with somebody who does something else.’

‘Say more.’

‘Because a colleague of mine would have described the same workflow back to me,’ Nkechi said. ‘You described a completely different workflow and I had to state my own rules out loud in order to compare them, and stating them is where I found the one that was wrong.’


## Capture is a first-order property

The value of any system for holding knowledge is bounded by what actually gets into it, which sounds obvious and is routinely treated as a secondary concern. Organisation, retrieval, structure, and analysis are all downstream of a prior question: whether the observation survived the moment it occurred in. A tool with excellent search and nothing in it is a tool with excellent search.

What the two practitioners in that conversation describe is a specific economic situation rather than a failure of discipline. A thought arriving during committed activity has a working life measured in a minute or less, and every action between the thought and the record is a chance for it to escape. When recording costs more than the thought appears to be worth in that instant, the thought loses, and this happens below the level of deliberation. The person involved concludes that they are forgetful. Lower the cost to seconds and the same person captures constantly, with no change to their memory and no new resolve.

Fixing capture usually produces a larger improvement than any amount of restructuring. The practical test is to spend one working period capturing everything and observe how much of it would otherwise have been lost. Practitioners consistently describe the number being higher than they expected, which is the argument that no amount of reasoning about the subject produces.

### What is the one habit worth adopting immediately?

Capturing without judging. The instinct to assess whether something is worth recording is precisely what the surface exists to remove, and it operates at the moment somebody knows least about what the period will contain. Practitioners describe a conversion rate around a quarter and identify the unconverted majority as the price of not having had to decide, which is a bargain given that the decision would have been made badly. Nobody manages this on the first attempt, since the instinct to filter is strong and largely unconscious. Noticing it happening, and deliberately capturing the thing that seemed not worth capturing, is what establishes the practice within a fortnight.

### What does this look like after a year of use?

A log spanning every working period, searchable, containing several thousand short observations, of which perhaps a quarter became formal records. Practitioners describe using both layers: the entries for anything they need to find as a discrete thing, and the log for questions about frequency, pattern, and context. Most also describe having recovered something at least once that they had entirely forgotten writing, which is generally the point at which they stop questioning the practice. The transition from treating the log as temporary staging to treating it as a permanent record is the change most practitioners describe as significant, and it usually arrives through an accidental search rather than through a decision.


### Why do people in different professions describe this so similarly?

Because the situation is defined by conditions rather than by content. Somebody committed to an activity, unable to pause, receiving something from another person or from circumstances that will not recur, with seconds rather than minutes available. That describes a bridge at night, a shooting day, a laboratory session, a consulting room, a shop counter, and a hospital corridor equally well. The vocabulary differs completely and the constraint is identical, which is why practitioners recognise each other's accounts immediately. Anybody whose own work meets those conditions can reasonably expect the same practice to suit them, whatever their field, and the recognition tends to be instant rather than requiring persuasion. Practitioners describe reading an account from an unrelated profession and identifying their own situation in it within a paragraph.

### What should somebody take away from all of this?

That the record is bounded by what survived the moment, and that the moment is shorter than any composition surface assumes. Everything else follows: a capture surface with no decisions in it, an automatic timestamp because nobody in that state knows the hour, a sequence because observations accumulate, a processing session at a fixed point because raw material is not knowledge, and retention of the unconverted lines because the pattern lives there rather than in the entries. Each of those properties is small and the combination changes what a person actually records over a year, which is the only measure that matters for a system holding knowledge. None of them requires additional discipline once established.

### Is there anything this approach does not suit?

Deliberate composition, which needs a different surface entirely. Anything requiring structure, sections, attachments, or a considered arrangement should be written where those exist, and attempting it in a sequential log produces something ordered rather than organised. Practitioners describe having made this mistake once, usually because they were already in the log and did not want to move, and describe the resulting document as genuinely poor rather than merely untidy. Knowing which surface a piece of work calls for takes a moment and saves rewriting, and the two rules are short enough to hold: order belongs in a log, structure belongs in an editor. Practitioners describe the demonstration landing considerably better than any explanation, largely because the loss being described is one almost everybody has experienced and nobody has named.

### How does somebody explain this practice to a colleague?

By describing a specific loss rather than a mechanism. Most people recognise immediately the experience of having observed something during a busy period, intending to write it down afterward, and finding it gone. Naming that experience produces more interest than any account of chat-style interfaces, and following it with one concrete case, where a few seconds of typing preserved something that changed a decision, is generally sufficient. The mechanism explains itself once somebody wants the outcome. Anybody worried about the maintenance burden should try it for one week with a strict processing session, since a week is enough to establish whether the discipline is sustainable for them.

### What is the most common objection and is it fair?

That it will become another thing to maintain, which is entirely fair and is the correct thing to worry about. The honest answer is that capture requires no maintenance and processing does, and that the processing session is the part which decides whether the whole arrangement survives. Practitioners describe twenty minutes at a fixed point, attached to something that already happens, and note that the discipline is genuinely the hard part rather than the capture. The effect is rarely noticed at the time and is one of the changes practitioners most consistently mention when asked what they would least willingly give up. Both practitioners in this conversation could name a specific observation, captured in seconds, that changed something substantial about their work, which is generally the form the argument takes once somebody has been doing it for a year.

### What happens to somebody's working memory once they trust the capture?

Practitioners describe stopping the background rehearsal that previously accompanied any busy period. Somebody holding four observations in mind while continuing to work is running a continuous process, and the consumption is invisible until it ends. Several report noticing this only in retrospect, usually because a period that would previously have been stressful passed without the familiar sense of trying not to forget things. Practitioners describe the demonstration landing considerably better than any explanation, largely because the loss being described is one almost everybody has experienced and nobody has named. The immediate benefit is what sustains the practice through the first weeks, and the accumulated one is what makes anybody keep it for years afterward.

### What single change would somebody notice first?

That they stop losing the thing that occurs to them while their hands are busy. Practitioners describe this as immediate rather than gradual: within the first working period, several observations exist that would previously have evaporated, and the person can usually name them. The compounding benefits, meaning the searchable history, the patterns visible only in aggregate, and the reduced background rehearsal, arrive over months and are what keep the practice going once the novelty has passed. ### Is this practice worth adopting for somebody whose work is mostly at a desk?

Frequently yes, and the trigger is attention rather than mobility. Somebody moving between meetings, working through a difficult problem, or handling a demanding period has the same few seconds and the same committed attention as anybody in the field, and loses observations at a comparable rate. The main difference is that desk-based professionals rarely identify the loss, since nothing about their environment makes it obvious, and they attribute it to ordinary forgetfulness instead. A useful test is to capture everything notable across two consecutive working days and then read the log, since the material somebody did not expect to have recorded is generally the evidence that persuades them. Most people find several observations they would certainly have lost.
