There is a category of material that almost every professional possesses and almost none of them manages. Recorded interviews. Lectures. Read regulations. Conversations somebody had the sense to capture. Voice memos made in a car. This material frequently contains more than any written account of it does, because a recording preserves hesitation, emphasis, correction, and the specific words somebody chose, all of which are discarded the moment anybody summarises.

The reason it goes unmanaged is structural rather than a matter of neglect. Everything else in a knowledge system is text, and text is transparent to retrieval: somebody types a word and the material containing it appears. A recording is opaque. It cannot be scanned, skimmed, or searched. It can only be played, in sequence, at the speed at which it was originally spoken, which means locating a specific passage inside an hour of audio requires listening to whatever precedes it.

![How Audio Becomes Knowledge](/assets/img/feature-screenshot-placeholder.svg)

The consequence is a familiar accumulation. Somebody holds a folder of recordings, each of which they know contains something valuable, none of which they can reach without an investment of time they cannot justify for a question that would take four seconds to answer if the material were written. So the folder sits there, its contents intact and effectively unreachable, and the person gradually stops thinking of it as part of their working material.
<!--more-->


There is a second problem that compounds the first, which is that recordings arrive in the shape of the occasion rather than in the shape of the content. An interview lasts as long as the conversation lasted. A recorded chapter runs to whatever length the chapter runs to. Neither is organised by subject, which means a recording about six things is a single object that cannot be consulted about any one of them without traversing the other five.

Both problems yield to the same two-part response, and neither part works alone. The first is editorial: cutting the material into units that correspond to subjects rather than to occasions, joining fragments that belong together, and removing whatever is not content. What results is a set of objects each concerning one thing, which is the shape material needs to be in before anybody can work with it.

The second is textual and is the part people skip. A recording sitting alongside notes about it is a recording that has become findable, because the notes are text and text is searchable. This is not transcription, which is expensive and which almost nobody sustains. It is a deliberate record of the terms, names, dates, and subjects the recording contains, written specifically so that a future search reaches it.

That last phrase carries the whole discipline. Notes written to help somebody remember and notes written to help somebody find are different documents. The first captures impressions and the second captures vocabulary. Anybody who writes listening notes in the second mode converts an opaque object into a reachable one, at a cost of a few minutes, and the conversion is permanent.

What follows is a conversation between two people whose recordings could hardly be less alike, one studying for a professional examination and one holding decades of interviews with people who are mostly no longer living.


## Colombo, and a box of cassettes

Nkechi had proposed it after Felix mentioned something in passing.

‘You said you had tapes,’ she said. ‘And you said it the way people mention something they have been avoiding.’

‘Which is exactly what it was.’ Felix was frank. ‘Eleven boxes in the barn is the photographs. The tapes are a smaller problem and a worse one.’

‘Say the number.’

‘Around sixty cassettes, digitised about four years ago by somebody's nephew, sitting on a machine in a folder called Tapes.’ He shrugged. ‘Which I have opened perhaps twice.’

‘Say what is on them.’

‘Interviews. Academic ones, conducted over about twenty years, with colleagues and with people in the field I was working in.’ Felix counted. ‘Most of whom are dead.’

Nkechi was quiet for a moment.

‘Which changes the object entirely.’

‘Which changes it from research material into something else, and I have never quite known what to call it.’ He was matter of fact. ‘A recording of a conversation with a person who has died is not a document.’

‘Say what it is.’

‘I do not know. It is a voice.’ Felix paused. ‘Which is why I have opened the folder twice and closed it both times.’

‘Mine is considerably less freighted,’ Nkechi said. ‘Mine is a man reading regulations aloud.’

Felix laughed.

‘Say yours.’

‘Audiobook recordings of maritime law and safety material, for the certification I am working toward.’ She counted. ‘Which is about six hours per subject and which is unusable in that form.’

‘Say why unusable.’

‘Because I have forty minutes, not six hours, and the forty minutes happen at unpredictable times.’ Nkechi was matter of fact. ‘A six-hour recording is a thing you listen to on a long journey. My life does not have long journeys in it, which sounds absurd on a ship and is true.’


## Six hours into twenty pieces

‘Show me what you did,’ Felix said.

Nkechi shared.

‘This is one subject,’ she said. ‘Collision avoidance, which is the one that matters most and which I have been through four times.’

‘Say what you started with.’

‘One file, six hours and eleven minutes, with the chapter divisions announced by the reader and not marked in any way.’ She was matter of fact. ‘Which means finding a section means scrubbing through and listening for a man saying a number.’

‘And now?’

‘Twenty clips.’ Nkechi listed them. ‘The shortest is four minutes and the longest is about nineteen.’

‘Say how you decided the divisions.’

‘By subject rather than by chapter, which is the thing I got right by accident.’ She was precise. ‘Because the chapters are how the book is organised and the subjects are how the examination is organised, and those are not the same.’

‘Give me a case.’

‘Two rules that are treated in different chapters and that are examined together, always, because they interact.’ Nkechi counted. ‘Which as a chapter division is two separate listens forty minutes apart. As a subject division it is one clip of eleven minutes.’

‘So you cut across the original structure.’

‘I cut across it deliberately, and the clip I made is a thing that does not exist in the book.’ She shrugged. ‘Which is the point. I am not preserving the recording. I am producing study material out of it.’

Felix asked about the trimming.

‘You mentioned removing silence.’

‘Which sounds trivial and which matters more than the cutting.’ Nkechi was definite. ‘Every recorded section has perhaps eleven seconds at each end. Introduction, pause, breath, and the same at the close.’

‘Which across twenty clips.’

‘Which across twenty clips is about seven minutes of nothing per subject, and there are five subjects.’ She counted. ‘Which is over half an hour of dead air I would otherwise listen to repeatedly.’

‘Because you listen to these many times.’

‘Four times through the collision material and I will do it twice more before the examination.’ Nkechi was matter of fact. ‘So anything in a clip gets heard six times, and eleven seconds of breathing heard six times across twenty clips is not a small number.’

‘Which nobody would calculate.’

‘Which nobody calculates and which everybody feels, because a clip that starts immediately is a clip you play again and one that starts with throat-clearing is one you find slightly irritating.’ She shrugged. ‘And slightly irritating over six months is how a study routine dies.’

‘Say when you listen.’

‘During a watch when conditions allow, which is a specific and limited circumstance.’ Nkechi was precise. ‘One clip, which is between four and nineteen minutes, and then I write afterward.’

‘Not during.’

‘Never during, for reasons that should be obvious.’ She laughed. ‘I write in the twenty minutes after I come off, which is the actual study session. The listening is input.’

‘So the clip length is set by that interval.’

‘The clip length is set by what I can attend to properly while also doing something else, which is about fifteen minutes, and the ones that run to nineteen are the ones I get least from.’


## Sixty cassettes and a decision

‘Now yours,’ Nkechi said. ‘And I want to know what you have actually done rather than what you intend to.’

‘Which is a fair question and the answer is four interviews out of sixty.’ Felix was honest. ‘Which took me about three weeks and which taught me the method.’

‘Say the first problem.’

‘That a cassette has two sides and an interview does not.’ He counted. ‘So a ninety-minute conversation exists as two files, with a break in the middle at whatever point the tape ran out.’

‘Which is usually mid-sentence.’

‘Almost always mid-sentence, and occasionally with a gap where somebody turned the machine over and missed thirty seconds.’ Felix shrugged. ‘So the first operation was joining. Two files into one continuous conversation.’

‘Which restores the object.’

‘Which restores the conversation as a conversation rather than as two artefacts of a recording medium.’ He was definite. ‘And that is a considerable psychological difference. Two files feel like archive material. One file feels like a person talking.’

‘Say what you did next.’

‘The opposite, which sounds contradictory.’ Felix laughed. ‘I joined them and then I cut them.’

‘Say why both.’

‘Because the joining fixed a problem caused by the medium and the cutting solved a problem caused by the length.’ He counted. ‘Ninety minutes of one person is not consultable. Nobody consults ninety minutes.’

‘So the segments.’

‘Segments by subject, which for an academic interview falls out naturally.’ Felix listed them. ‘Early career. The project we were both involved in. Departmental matters, which is a polite phrase. And one segment I have labelled personal, which I will come to.’

‘Four segments from ninety minutes.’

‘Four segments totalling perhaps seventy minutes, because about twenty minutes is administrative and does not need keeping.’ He was matter of fact. ‘Where the tape recorder was being adjusted and where we discussed lunch.’

‘Which you cut out.’

‘Which I cut out and which I want to say something about, because I hesitated over it for a long time.’ Felix was careful. ‘I was editing a recording of a person who cannot consent to being edited.’

‘Which is a real question.’

‘Which is a genuinely real question and I resolved it in a way I am comfortable with.’ He counted. ‘I keep the joined original, complete, untouched. The segments are derived. Nothing is lost, and what I work with is usable.’

‘So the edit is additive.’

‘The edit is additive rather than destructive, which is the only version I could live with.’ Felix was definite. ‘If the segments were all that survived I would be making decisions about somebody's words that they never agreed to.’

Nkechi said hers had no such problem and she recognised the principle.

‘Mine is a commercial recording of a man reading a document,’ she said. ‘And I still keep the originals.’

‘Say why, since you have no ethical difficulty.’

‘Because I cut wrongly twice and had to go back.’ She shrugged. ‘Which is a considerably less interesting reason and produces the same practice.’


## The notes that make audio findable

‘Now the part that actually matters,’ Nkechi said. ‘Because everything we have described is editing and none of it makes anything findable.’

‘Say it.’

‘A clip is still opaque. Cutting it into twenty pieces means I have twenty opaque objects rather than one.’ She was precise. ‘Better organised and equally unsearchable.’

‘So the notes do the work.’

‘The notes do all of the work and I want to describe how I write them, because it is different from how I would write notes for myself.’

‘Say the difference.’

‘Notes to remember and notes to find are not the same document.’ Nkechi counted. ‘When I write to remember, I write what I understood. When I write to find, I write the words that are in the recording.’

‘Which is a vocabulary list.’

‘Which is partly a vocabulary list and partly prose, and the vocabulary part is deliberate.’ She showed one. ‘Rule numbers. The specific terms. The scenario types. Anything I might type into a search in four months.’

‘Rather than what you concluded.’

‘My conclusions are there too, in a separate section, because those are what I revise from.’ Nkechi was definite. ‘But my conclusions use my words and the examination uses the regulation's words, so a note containing only my conclusions is a note I cannot find with the terms I will actually be searching.’

‘Which is a specific insight.’

‘Which took me two months and one frustrating afternoon.’ She laughed. ‘I searched for a term I knew was in the material and found nothing, because I had paraphrased it every time.’

Felix said his version was names.

‘Mine is entirely names and dates,’ he said.

‘Say why.’

‘Because I will search for a person.’ He was matter of fact. ‘That is the question I will have. Did anybody say anything about this individual, and if so, who and when.’

‘Which requires the names to be in the text.’

‘Which requires me to write down every name mentioned, spelled correctly, even when the mention is trivial.’ Felix counted. ‘Which feels like clerical work while doing it and is the only thing that makes the collection answerable.’

‘Say how you record a position.’

‘Timestamp and a phrase.’ He showed one. ‘Around eleven minutes, mentions the appointment and describes the committee. Which is enough to find the passage and enough to know whether it is worth finding.’

‘So you can go straight to it.’

‘I can go straight to it, which is the difference between consulting a recording and listening to one.’ Felix was definite. ‘Nobody consults a ninety-minute file. Everybody can consult a note saying that the relevant part is at eleven minutes.’

‘Which is a small annotation.’

‘Which is perhaps twelve lines per segment and which took me about fifteen minutes each.’ He shrugged. ‘Against ninety minutes of listening. Which is the ratio that makes it sustainable.’

Nkechi asked about transcription.

‘Have you considered doing it properly?’

‘I considered it and I will not.’ Felix was definite. ‘Sixty interviews at ninety minutes is ninety hours of audio and I am seventy-two.’

‘Which is an honest answer.’

‘Which is the only honest answer and there is a better one underneath it.’ He counted. ‘A transcript is a considerable amount of work that produces something less useful than the recording, because a transcript loses the voice.’

‘Say what the voice carries.’

‘Hesitation. Where somebody stopped. What they said and then corrected.’ Felix was quieter. ‘I have one interview where a man pauses for about four seconds before answering a question about a colleague, and that pause is the whole answer.’

‘Which a transcript would render as nothing.’

‘Which a transcript renders as a full stop, and the pause is the finding.’ He shrugged. ‘So the recording is the document and the notes are the index, and I would not reverse those roles even if transcription were free.’


## The segment Felix has not attached

‘There is one thing I have not done and I want to describe why,’ Felix said.

‘Say it.’

‘I mentioned a segment I labelled personal. Which exists and which is not attached to anything.’

‘Say what is on it.’

‘About four minutes at the end of an interview where the tape was still running and we both knew the work part was finished.’ He was careful. ‘And he talks about his wife, who was ill.’

Nkechi did not respond immediately.

‘Which is not research material.’

‘Which is not research material and it is not memoir material either, and it is the most valuable four minutes in the collection.’ Felix was quiet. ‘Because it is a man being a person rather than a colleague.’

‘So what do you do with it?’

‘I have cut it out and I have kept it and I have put it nowhere.’ He shrugged. ‘Which is an unresolved position and I have been in it for about three weeks.’

‘Say what the difficulty is.’

‘That attaching it to a memoir chapter makes it material for a book.’ Felix was precise. ‘And I am not confident he would have wanted that, and I cannot ask him.’

‘Which is the whole problem.’

‘Which is the whole problem and it is not a problem about audio.’ He was definite. ‘It is a problem about what a recording of a private moment becomes when the person is dead and I am writing a book.’

Nkechi said something after a pause.

‘Say why you cut it out rather than leaving it in the interview.’

Felix stopped.

‘That is a considerably better question than I have been asking myself.’

‘Say it back.’

‘That cutting it out was already a decision.’ He was working through it. ‘I separated it because I recognised it was different, which means I had already judged that it did not belong with the rest.’

‘And having judged that.’

‘And having judged that, the honest position is that it stays out of the book and stays in the collection.’ Felix nodded slowly. ‘Which is where it is, and I have been treating an unresolved feeling as an unresolved decision.’

‘Which are different.’

‘Which are entirely different and I would not have separated them without being asked,’ he said.


## Putting pieces together

‘There is an operation we have both mentioned and not examined,’ Felix said. ‘Which is joining rather than cutting.’

‘Say your use.’

‘Two, and the second is more interesting.’ He counted. ‘The first is repairing the medium, which I described. Two sides of a cassette into one conversation.’

‘Which is restoration.’

‘Which is purely restoration and produces the thing that always should have existed.’ Felix was matter of fact. ‘The second is composition, which produces something that never existed.’

‘Say it.’

‘I interviewed one person three times across about eleven years.’ He was precise. ‘Three separate conversations, three separate occasions, three separate recordings.’

‘And you joined them.’

‘I joined the segments where he talks about the same subject, from all three, into one file.’ Felix counted. ‘Which is about twenty minutes of a man discussing one thing across a decade.’

‘Which is a different object.’

‘Which is an object that never happened and which is more revealing than any of the three conversations.’ He was definite. ‘Because you hear his position change. And the change is gradual across the three and obvious when they are consecutive.’

‘That is a research method.’

‘That is a research method I would have used forty years ago if it had been possible, and I have made three of them and I intend to make more.’ Felix was matter of fact.

Nkechi said hers was less inventive and useful.

‘Mine is compilation for a purpose,’ she said.

‘Say it.’

‘All the clips on one subject joined into a single study session.’ She counted. ‘Which is perhaps fifty minutes and which I use in a different way from the individual clips.’

‘Say the difference.’

‘Individual clips are for learning something. The compilation is for revision.’ Nkechi was precise. ‘When I know the material and want to run through it, I want one continuous fifty minutes rather than twenty separate decisions about what to play next.’

‘So the same material serves two purposes.’

‘The same material in two arrangements serves two stages, and I would not have thought of the second until I reached it.’ She shrugged. ‘I made the compilations about a month before my last examination, entirely out of impatience with choosing clips.’

‘Which is how most useful things get made.’

‘Which is how everything in my arrangement got made, and none of it was designed.’


## Audio entries behaving like everything else

‘I want to establish something,’ Nkechi said, ‘because people treat media as a separate category and it is not.’

‘Say it.’

‘My audio entries are entries. They have everything any other entry has.’

‘Say what that means concretely.’

‘Dates, which for me are examination deadlines, so my study material appears when it needs attention.’ She counted. ‘Categories for the subject area, so I can filter to one topic across everything.’

‘Which cuts across.’

‘Which cuts across the structure, so material about one subject that lives in different places is reachable as one set.’ Nkechi was definite. ‘And the clips I use most are starred, which is about six of them.’

‘Say which six.’

‘The ones covering the material I find hardest, which is not the same as the material that matters most.’ She was frank. ‘Which is an honest thing to admit about a study routine.’

Felix described his placement.

‘Mine sit inside the memoir structure,’ he said.

‘Say what that means.’

‘Each segment is attached to the entry for the period it concerns.’ He counted. ‘So an interview segment about a particular decade sits in the chapter about that decade, alongside my written account of the same events.’

‘Which is the useful arrangement.’

‘Which is the arrangement I could not have had before, and it is the whole reason I finally opened the folder.’ Felix was definite. ‘Because previously the tapes were archive and the memoir was writing, and they were separate activities in separate places.’

‘And now?’

‘Now I am writing a chapter and the recording of somebody discussing the same events is in the same entry, one section below.’ He shrugged. ‘Which has changed two chapters substantially.’

‘Say how.’

‘I remembered a meeting one way and a man who was present describes it differently.’ Felix was matter of fact. ‘Which I have written about, as a disagreement, rather than picking one.’

‘Which is better than either.’

‘Which is considerably better than either and it only exists because the two accounts are in the same place.’

Nkechi asked about the notes changing.

‘Do you revise the listening notes?’

‘Constantly, which surprised me.’ Felix counted. ‘I listen to a segment while working on a chapter, notice something I missed, and add to the notes.’

‘So the notes accumulate.’

‘The notes accumulate across several listenings, and the earlier versions are retained, which means I can see what I noticed first.’ He was thoughtful. ‘Which occasionally tells me something about what I was looking for at the time.’


## What Nkechi does that Felix cannot

‘There is an asymmetry between us that I want to name,’ Nkechi said.

‘Say it.’

‘My material was made to be listened to and yours was not.’

‘Which is true and I had not framed it that way.’

‘Say what a professional reader does.’ She counted. ‘Clear articulation. Consistent pace. Pauses in sensible places. Which means I can cut anywhere and the result is coherent.’

‘Whereas a conversation.’

‘Whereas a conversation has people talking over each other, sentences that never finish, and a point being made across four exchanges.’ Nkechi was matter of fact. ‘Which means your cuts have to be found rather than chosen.’

‘Which is exactly right and is why four interviews took three weeks.’ Felix was rueful. ‘I listen to locate the boundary, and the boundary is frequently not where the subject changes.’

‘Say the difference.’

‘Somebody moves onto a new subject and then returns to the previous one for thirty seconds.’ He counted. ‘So a clean subject division would cut that return out, and the return is often where the interesting thing is said.’

‘Because people revisit what they are uncomfortable about.’

‘Because people revisit what they have not finished thinking about, which is the same observation.’ Felix was definite. ‘So my segments overlap deliberately. Two segments both contain the same ninety seconds.’

‘Which mine never do.’

‘Which yours never need to, because a regulation does not circle back to something it said earlier out of unease.’ He laughed.

Nkechi was thinking.

‘That is a genuine method and I would not have arrived at it.’

‘Say why not.’

‘Because my material has no reason to overlap and I would have treated overlap as an error.’ She was frank. ‘Which is what I would have told somebody to fix.’

‘Which would have been wrong advice for my material.’

‘Which would have been confidently wrong advice, and I want to note that, because we have both been describing methods as though they were general.’ Nkechi was definite. ‘Mine works for produced audio and yours works for captured audio, and those are different problems.’


## Seeing what has arrived

‘There is a small thing I use during a working session and I want to mention it,’ Felix said.

‘Say it.’

‘The panel showing what has been attached recently.’

‘Which sounds unremarkable.’

‘Which is unremarkable most of the time and is genuinely useful during the specific work we have been describing.’ He was matter of fact. ‘Because a session of this kind produces perhaps eleven files across six entries.’

‘Which is a lot of moving parts.’

‘Which is a lot of moving parts and I am seventy-two and I lose track.’ Felix laughed. ‘So the running list of what has landed is how I confirm that a segment I made actually went where I intended.’

‘Rather than checking each entry.’

‘Rather than opening six entries to confirm six attachments, which is what I did for the first fortnight.’ He shrugged. ‘Which sounds trivial and it is four minutes per session and it removes a specific anxiety.’

‘Say the anxiety.’

‘That I have produced something and lost it.’ Felix was frank. ‘Which is the fear of somebody who has spent three weeks on four interviews.’

Nkechi said hers was verification rather than reassurance.

‘I use it to check I have not attached something twice,’ she said.

‘Which happens?’

‘Which happened twice when I was building the compilations, because I was working quickly and the files have similar names.’ She was matter of fact. ‘And two copies of a fifty-minute file is not a disaster and it is untidy and it is the kind of thing that accumulates.’

‘So you glance at the list.’

‘I glance at it at the end of a session, which takes about eight seconds, and it has caught it both times.’


## What audio holds that text does not

Audio is the most widely held and least managed category of professional material. Recordings contain more than any written account of them, because they preserve hesitation, emphasis, correction, and the exact words somebody chose, all of which are discarded the moment anybody summarises. And yet recordings sit unconsulted, because they are opaque to retrieval and can only be played in sequence at the speed at which they were spoken.

The two problems that produce that neglect are separable, and both practitioners here addressed them in the same order. Recordings arrive in the shape of the occasion rather than the shape of the content: an interview lasts as long as the conversation lasted, a read chapter runs to whatever length the chapter runs to. Neither is organised by subject, which means a ninety-minute file about six things cannot be consulted about any one of them.

The editorial response is to produce units corresponding to subjects rather than to occasions, which involves both cutting and joining. One practitioner divided a six-hour reading into twenty clips organised by examination subject rather than by chapter, deliberately cutting across the original structure, on the grounds that he was producing study material rather than preserving a recording. The other joined two sides of a cassette back into one conversation before cutting it into topic segments, and describes the joined version as feeling like a person talking where two files felt like archive material.

Trimming turned out to matter more than either expected. Eleven seconds of silence at each end of a clip, across twenty clips heard six times each, is a substantial quantity of dead air. More importantly it is the difference between a clip somebody plays without hesitation and one that is slightly irritating, and slightly irritating across six months is how a study routine dies.

Joining produced something neither had anticipated. One practitioner interviewed the same person on three occasions across eleven years and joined the segments where that person discusses one subject into a single file, producing an object that never happened and in which a gradual change of position becomes audible. He describes it as a research method he would have used decades earlier had it been possible. The other joined individual study clips into continuous revision sessions, discovering that the same material in two arrangements serves two different stages of learning.

None of that makes anything findable, which both stated plainly. Cutting one opaque object into twenty produces twenty opaque objects. What bridges the gap is text sitting alongside the audio, and the discipline is specific: notes written to help somebody remember and notes written to help somebody find are different documents. The first captures impressions; the second captures the vocabulary actually present in the recording.

One practitioner learned this from a frustrating afternoon searching for a term she knew was in her material and finding nothing, because she had paraphrased it consistently in her own words. Her notes now carry the regulation's terms alongside her own conclusions, in separate sections, because she revises from one and searches with the other. The other records every name mentioned, spelled correctly, even in trivial mentions, because a name is the question he will actually have.

Position markers convert consultation from listening. A note saying that the relevant passage falls at around eleven minutes, with a phrase describing it, is enough both to locate the passage and to judge whether it is worth locating. Twelve lines per segment against ninety minutes of recording is the ratio that makes the practice sustainable across a substantial collection.

Neither transcribes, and the reasoning goes beyond effort. A transcript loses the voice, and one practitioner describes an interview where a man pauses for four seconds before answering a question about a colleague, where the pause is the entire answer and a transcript would render it as a full stop. The recording is the document and the notes are the index, and he would not reverse those roles even if transcription cost nothing.

Audio entries behave like every other entry, which is the point most easily missed. They carry dates, so study material surfaces before an examination. They carry categories, so a subject can be filtered across the whole collection. They sit inside the structure where the relevant written work lives, which for one practitioner means a segment of somebody discussing a period sits one section below his own written account of the same events. That arrangement changed two chapters of his memoir, since a colleague remembered a meeting differently and the disagreement is now what he has written about.

The one ethical question in the conversation belongs to anybody editing recordings of people who cannot consent. It was resolved by making the editing additive rather than destructive: the complete joined original is retained untouched, and every segment is derived from it. Nothing is lost, what somebody works with is usable, and no decisions are being made about a person's words that survive as the only version.

A recording can only be played, in sequence, at the speed it was spoken, which means locating a specific passage inside an hour requires listening to whatever precedes it.
