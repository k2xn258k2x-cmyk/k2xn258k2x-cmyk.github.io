There is one category of professional knowledge that everybody produces and almost nobody organises, and its universality is what makes the neglect so striking. Every person who works with other people takes notes about those encounters. Nobody is taught how to keep them. The result is a body of material that is simultaneously the most common thing professionals write and the least systematically held.

The failure is not one of diligence. People take notes attentively, in good faith, capturing what was decided and what they undertook to do. What happens afterward is the problem. The notes land wherever was nearest at the time: a document, a message to oneself, a physical notebook, an application chosen because it was already open. Six months later the material exists in four places in three formats and the person is the only index to any of it.

![How Twelve People Handle Meeting Notes](/assets/img/feature-screenshot-placeholder.svg)

What makes this different from other organisational failures is the shape of the eventual need. Nobody browses meeting notes. They are consulted only in response to a specific question: what did we agree, who was present, when was that raised, and what did I say I would do. Each of those is a retrieval question about a particular occasion, and retrieval is precisely what an undifferentiated accumulation cannot support.
<!--more-->


There is a second property that compounds it. Meeting notes contain commitments, and commitments have dates. A note recording that somebody undertook to do something by a particular time is a note containing an obligation, and separating the obligation from the record means either maintaining a parallel list or discovering the commitment after the date has passed. Most people do neither reliably, which is why meetings routinely produce actions that nobody performs.

They also frequently contain material about other people. What somebody disclosed, what was said about a third party, what a colleague is struggling with. That content sits in the same document as the entirely mundane administrative detail, which means either the whole record is treated as sensitive, which is impractical, or none of it is, which is inappropriate. Very few people have a mechanism for the middle.

The final complication is that meetings are not one thing. A clinical session, a production briefing, a client call, a committee, a supervision, a handover, and a conversation with oneself are all encounters that generate notes, and they have almost nothing else in common. Their participants differ, their sensitivity differs, their outputs differ, and what somebody needs to find afterward differs. A single template applied across all of them serves none of them well.

Which is why the interesting question is not what the correct system is. There is no correct system, because meeting culture is professional culture and professional cultures differ. The interesting question is what several people arrive at independently when they have the same building blocks and entirely different obligations, and whether anything survives the differences.

What follows is a thread in a small private group, started by somebody who described her own arrangement as a disaster, which produced twelve answers over three days and one conclusion nobody had set out to reach.

## Dr. Meera Iyer, psychiatrist, Austin

You are not uniquely disorganised and I will start because mine is the most rigid and therefore the least interesting.

Every session is an entry. Not every patient, every session, which is the decision everything else follows from.

I resisted that for a year. A patient is a person and a person should be one record, and I built it that way and it failed within four months, because a record containing forty sessions is a document nobody can navigate and every session is buried in it.

Now each session is its own entry under the patient's page, titled with the date and nothing else, and the patient is the page rather than the entry.

Three sections, identical every time. Session notes. Treatment plan updates. Follow-up actions.

The identical structure is the part I would defend hardest. Because an empty treatment plan section is a statement rather than an absence. It says nothing changed, which is clinically meaningful, and I can see it in a second.

If the structure varied by session I would have to read to find out whether something was missing, and reading forty sessions to establish what did not happen is not a thing anybody does.

Everything is protected, obviously.

I want to say something about how that actually works during a session, because David asked me about it once and I gave him a poor answer.

I enter the password when I open the record at the start of the appointment, and I do not enter it again. It stays available for the working period, which means I write during the session without the mechanism intruding.

Which sounds minor and is the difference between a workable arrangement and an unworkable one. A person typing a password every time they touch a record during a fifty-minute appointment would abandon the whole thing in a week.

The screen locks whenever I stand, which we have discussed at length and which I will not repeat.

A due date on the entry for the next session, which means my due list is my appointment schedule and also tells me which records I should read beforehand.

Which is the last thing and the one that matters most. The entry with the date on it is the record I read before the person arrives, so the date and the material are the same object.

## Raj Malakar, documentary filmmaker, Berlin and Mumbai

Mine has two halves and they are separated by about four hours.

During a meeting I do not write notes properly. I capture lines.

Which is the whole of my method and it took me two years to admit that trying to write structured notes during a production meeting was making me a worse participant.

A production meeting is eleven people in a room deciding four things quickly, and a person typing into a structured document is a person not in the conversation.

So I use the message log. Short lines, timestamped, as they occur. Someone commits to something, I write one line. A decision is made, one line. Something is raised that nobody resolves, one line.

Perhaps fifteen lines in an hour, each of them four or five words, none of them a sentence anybody else could read.

Then in the evening, and this is the half that matters, I process them into an entry.

Which takes about twenty minutes and which is where the actual notes get written, by somebody who was fully present in the meeting rather than half typing.

The entry has three sections. Decisions. Action items. Creative notes.

The third one is mine rather than the meeting's, and it is where I write what I thought about what was decided, which frequently disagrees with what was decided.

Labels for the meeting type, which for me is three: crew, client, and review session. Which are different animals and which I need to filter separately.

And an inline marker for the production stage, which drives my board, so a meeting entry appears in the column for whatever phase we are in.

I want to make a suggestion to everybody rather than only describing mine.

Try capturing during and writing after. Whatever your meetings are.

Because I think most people in this group are attempting to produce a finished record in real time, which is two jobs at once, and one of them is being done badly and it is usually the attending.

## Tomas Herrera, chemistry teacher, San Antonio

Mine is the simplest here and I want to describe why simple is correct for my situation.

I have perhaps four meetings a month and none of them is sensitive.

Department meetings, robotics club planning, and parent conferences. Which is a small volume with low stakes and which does not justify any structure at all.

So my entry is a date, a title, and prose. That is it. No sections.

What I do have is one thing and it is the thing Claire has just said she is adopting.

Every action item goes in a block that is visually set apart with a coloured bar down the side.

Which means when I open a meeting entry three weeks later, the things I committed to are the things I see, and the discussion is around them rather than containing them.

Labels for the meeting type, which is three: department, robotics, and parent conference.

Due dates on anything with a deadline, which is perhaps one item in four.

I want to say something about the low-stakes point because I think it matters.

Everybody in this thread with an elaborate system has a reason for it. Meera has clinical obligations. Claire has billable records. Suki has contracts.

I have none of those, and building something elaborate would produce a structure I maintain out of duty rather than need, which is the thing that gets abandoned in a difficult term.

So my rule is that the structure should be the minimum that solves the problem I actually have, and my problem is finding one meeting and not missing a commitment. Two problems, two mechanisms, nothing else.

## Suki Tanaka, security consultant, Tokyo and remote

Mine is contractual, which means the structure is not entirely my choice.

An engagement meeting produces a record my client may eventually see, and may be referenced in a report, so it has to be defensible rather than merely useful.

Four sections. Agenda. Discussion. Technical notes. Next steps.

The third one is the one that differs from everybody else's, and it is where anything technical goes in a form that survives.

Commands, configuration fragments, and anything I need to reproduce exactly go in a block that preserves them. Which sounds trivial and is the difference between a colleague being able to reproduce something and a colleague getting an error caused by a punctuation substitution.

I have handed a meeting record to a client engineer who ran something out of it directly, which is only possible because the content was preserved rather than prettified.

Protection at the entry level for anything where client systems were discussed in detail, which is most of them.

I generate credentials during engagements and the tool for that is in the same place, which matters more than it sounds.

Because previously I generated something in one application and recorded where it was used in another, and the gap between those two is where a credential gets recorded incorrectly.

Labels for engagement type. Due dates for anything I committed to, which in my work is frequently a specific deliverable on a specific date and which is contractual rather than aspirational.

I want to respond to Tomas because he has stated something I say to clients constantly and rarely hear from anybody else.

Match the structure to the stakes.

An organisation with a compliance obligation needs a defensible record. A person with four low-stakes meetings a month needs to find things. Applying the first arrangement to the second situation produces an unused system, and applying the second to the first produces a finding in an audit.

Both failures are common and the second is more expensive and the first is more frequent.

## Pastor David Achebe, Atlanta

Mine has a structural feature nobody else here needs and I want to explain it because the underlying problem is more common than the specific case.

A church committee meeting is a mundane administrative event that regularly contains one item of an entirely different kind.

Finance committee. Building repairs, the budget, a supplier, and then somebody mentions that a family is in difficulty and asks the committee to hold them.

So one record contains eleven administrative lines and one item concerning a named family, and those two things have completely different requirements.

For a year I handled this by treating the whole record as sensitive, which meant protecting every finance meeting, which meant entering a password to look at a discussion about guttering.

Which nobody sustains, and I stopped, and then for about two months I was recording nothing about the second category at all.

Now the entry has four sections and one of them is protected on its own.

Attendees. Prayer requests, which is the protected one. Decisions. Action items.

Which means the guttering is open and the family is not, in one record, and I stopped having to choose between an unusable arrangement and an inappropriate one.

Labels for the committee, which is four: finance, worship, youth, and pastoral.

The recurring committees are in my starred list, so the entry for the next finance meeting is one action away rather than a search.

And the next meeting goes into the schedule at the end of the current one, which is a habit I built after failing to do it for two years and then wondering in six weeks when we had said we would meet.

I want to make an observation about what I record rather than how.

I write what was decided and I do not write what people said.

Which is a deliberate choice in a small community. A minute recording that a particular person objected is a document that exists, and I am not confident enough about who might read it in fifteen years.

So the record says the committee decided something after discussion. Which is accurate, is sufficient for every purpose I have ever needed it for, and does not create a permanent artefact of somebody having been in a minority on a Tuesday.

## Twelve systems, one tool

Meeting notes are the most universally produced and least systematically held category of professional knowledge. Everybody takes them. Almost nobody organises them. The material ends up distributed across whatever was nearest at the time, and the person who wrote it is the only index to any of it.

What twelve professionals with the same building blocks and entirely different obligations produced is twelve systems with almost nothing in common at the level of detail and a great deal in common underneath.

Structure varied enormously and always for a stated reason. A clinician uses identical sections so that an empty one is a clinical statement rather than an oversight. An attorney records attendance first because who was present is the question that arrives eight months later. A ship's officer keeps her sections fixed because a stranger may have to read the record in circumstances she would rather not contemplate. A minister protects one section rather than an entry so that a discussion of guttering does not require a password while a family's difficulty does. A teacher with four low-stakes meetings a month uses no sections at all, on the explicit grounds that structure exceeding the need is structure abandoned during a difficult term.

That last principle was stated twice by people who arrived at it independently, and it is worth extracting: match the structure to the stakes. An elaborate arrangement applied to a modest problem produces something maintained out of duty. A modest arrangement applied to a regulated obligation produces a finding in an audit. The first failure is more common and the second is more expensive.

Two practitioners in unrelated fields described the same two-stage method for capture, and both had arrived at it after realising that producing a finished record during a meeting is two jobs performed simultaneously with the attending done badly. Short timestamped fragments during, processing into a structured entry afterward, with the processing performed by somebody who was actually present. A filmmaker described it as the change that made him a better participant; an officer described it as the difference between a chronological log that is complete and useless and a selective record somebody consults weeks later.

The question of what to record about other people produced three positions and no disagreement. A minister does not record who said what, because a permanent artefact of somebody having been in a minority is not something he is confident about. A journalist does not, because a record of who advocated what could be compelled. An attorney must, because attendance and position are the professional record. A retired academic recorded it for forty years and has since removed names, having discovered that his characterisation of a colleague aged considerably worse than the facts did.

The retrieval finding came from counting rather than from argument. ### How should meeting records be titled?

With whatever sorts usefully and identifies unambiguously. Practitioners with many relationships lead with a client or subject code so that everything for one party groups together, followed by the date. Practitioners with few meetings use the date alone. In both cases the title is chosen for how it behaves in a sorted list rather than for how it reads in isolation. The title is doing retrieval work rather than descriptive work, which is worth deciding deliberately at the outset since renaming a body of records later is tedious. Applying the same test as anywhere else, meaning whether somebody has ever filtered by a distinction, settles most of these cases quickly.
