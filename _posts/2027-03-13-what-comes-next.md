There is a question people almost never ask when adopting a tool, and it is the question that determines whether they will still be using it in five years. Not whether it does what they need today. Whether it will still do what they need when what they need has changed.

The omission is understandable. Somebody choosing a tool is solving a present problem, and the present problem is vivid while future ones are hypothetical. What makes the omission expensive is that professional life changes reliably rather than occasionally. People take on new responsibilities, acquire colleagues, move into adjacent fields, complete projects and begin others, and pass through transitions that alter not only the volume of what they hold but its kind.

![What Comes Next](/assets/img/feature-screenshot-placeholder.svg)

A tool that assumed one shape of work handles those transitions badly, and the failure has a particular character. It does not stop functioning. It becomes progressively more awkward: a structure that has to be worked around, a vocabulary that no longer describes the material, a set of assumptions that fit somebody's situation two years ago. People adapt, and the adaptations accumulate, and eventually somebody quietly begins keeping things elsewhere.
<!--more-->


What distinguishes an arrangement that survives is not flexibility in the abstract but a small number of specific properties. That the structure can be rearranged without anything becoming unreachable. That the vocabulary can be revised without invalidating what already carries it. That new kinds of material can be added without disturbing existing ones. And that the whole thing can be duplicated when somebody's work genuinely divides into separate contexts rather than merely growing.

Those properties are invisible during the period when nothing is changing, which is most of the time, and they are the only thing that matters during the periods when something is. Somebody assessing a tool while their situation is stable is assessing it under exactly the conditions that reveal least about it.

There is a second dimension that is easier to overlook, which concerns other people. Work that begins solitary frequently does not remain so. A practice acquires a colleague, a project acquires a collaborator, a department decides to standardise. An arrangement that assumes one person, and that offers nothing when a second appears, forces a choice between abandoning it and working around it.

What follows is a conversation among twelve people who all happen to be approaching a change at once, which is coincidence rather than design and which produced an unusually forward-looking discussion. Each of them has been using the same arrangement for long enough to have stopped thinking about it. All of them are about to find out whether it holds.


## The call

It took four attempts to schedule and it happened at an hour that suited almost nobody.

Nkechi joined from a cabin on a ship in the Arabian Sea at what was, for her, twenty past four in the morning. She had made coffee and she looked entirely awake, which Meera remarked on and which Nkechi attributed to fourteen months of watchkeeping.

Amir appeared with Yasmin visible behind him for the first ninety seconds, adjusting something, and then leaving with an expression Claire described afterward as the universal expression of a daughter who has explained this before.

Felix was in Vermont with the light behind him. Priya was in a library. Raj was in a rented flat in Porto with boxes visible.

David opened it, having volunteered to keep the thing moving, and did so by saying that he had a question rather than an agenda.

‘Everybody here is about to have something change,’ he said. ‘Which I noticed in the thread and which is either a coincidence or a sign that we are all at a similar point in something.’

‘Say what you mean by change,’ Suki said.

‘Something about your work that will be different in six months.’ David counted. ‘Which I have, and I know Meera has, and I suspect several others do.’

‘Everybody does,’ Claire said. ‘Which is either remarkable or is what a year always looks like and we do not usually compare.’

‘Then let us find out which.’


## What is changing

Meera went first, having been asked to.

‘I am taking on a junior colleague,’ she said. ‘Which after fifteen years of solo practice is a considerable adjustment.’

‘Say the shape of it,’ Claire asked.

‘She will have her own patients. We will share supervision and premises and nothing else clinically.’ Meera was precise. ‘Which means two entirely separate bodies of confidential material in one office.’

‘Which is a structural question.’

‘Which is a structural question and an ethical one, and the ethical one is settled: her patients are not mine and I should not be able to open their records.’

Raj went next.

‘I am co-directing for the first time,’ he said. ‘Which everybody who has done it tells me is either wonderful or a catastrophe.’

‘Say who with,’ Tomas asked.

‘Somebody I have worked alongside for years and never made anything with.’ Raj shrugged. ‘Which means two people who each have a way of working and who have never had to reconcile them.’

‘And you both keep material.’

‘We both keep a great deal of material and hers is organised entirely differently from mine, and neither of us is going to abandon our own arrangement.’

Claire described something larger.

‘I have been asked whether the firm should adopt what I use,’ she said.

There was a pause.

‘Say the size,’ Marcus said.

‘Eleven attorneys and about thirty support staff.’ Claire was matter of fact. ‘Which is not a decision I would make alone and which I have been asked to advise on.’

‘Say how you feel about it.’

‘Cautious. Because what works for me is a thing I built over four years and nobody can be handed four years.’ She was definite. ‘Which is the problem with recommending anything you have grown into.’

Tomas was moving schools.

‘New building, new department, new students in September,’ he said. ‘And about six hundred entries of material from a place I am leaving.’

‘Which comes with you,’ David said.

‘Which comes with me legally and which is a question I have thought about for a fortnight.’ Tomas was careful. ‘My lesson material is mine. My records about specific students are not, in any meaningful sense, and they stay.’

Suki was expanding rather than moving.

‘I am adding training to the consulting,’ she said. ‘Which is a different kind of work producing a different kind of material.’

‘Say the difference.’

‘Consulting produces findings about one organisation. Training produces material intended to be used repeatedly by people who are not me.’ Suki counted. ‘Which is almost the opposite of everything I currently hold.’

David went next, since he had opened.

‘Michelle wants to connect what she keeps with what I keep,’ he said. ‘For planning, principally. Events, rotas, the shared calendar of a church year.’

‘Which sounds straightforward,’ Amir said.

‘Which is straightforward for the events and complicated for everything else, because a great deal of what she holds is pastoral and a great deal of what I hold is pastoral and those are two separate confidences.’

Priya described a transition.

‘My viva is in seven weeks,’ she said. ‘And then, assuming it goes well, I stop being a student.’

‘Which changes what,’ Felix asked.

‘Everything about how my material is organised.’ Priya was precise. ‘Because it is currently organised around a thesis, which is a document with six chapters, and in three months there will be no thesis.’

Marcus was diversifying.

‘Commercial property,’ he said. ‘Which I have been circling for two years and have finally committed to.’

‘Say what is different about it.’

‘Longer leases, larger tenants, entirely different regulation, and a completely different rhythm.’ Marcus counted. ‘Which is not more of what I do. It is a second thing.’

Lena was approaching an end.

‘The investigation goes to a publisher in about four months,’ she said. ‘Which means the next phase is not investigating.’

‘Say what it is.’

‘Fact-checking, legal review, and a considerable amount of correspondence with people who will want to know exactly where each claim comes from.’ She was matter of fact. ‘Which is a different use of the same material.’

Amir was opening a second shop.

There was a small collective noise on the call, which he waved away.

‘It is four streets away and it is smaller and my nephew will run it,’ he said. ‘Which is either a good idea or the worst decision of my life.’

‘Say what changes for your records.’

‘Everything is currently one shop.’ Amir shrugged. ‘Which was fine when there was one shop.’

Nkechi was studying.

‘Master mariner,’ she said. ‘Which is the qualification that lets me command rather than serve.’

‘Say the scale of it,’ Suki asked.

‘Considerably larger than anything I have done. Perhaps eighteen months of study alongside a full contract rotation.’ Nkechi was matter of fact. ‘Which is why I am awake at twenty past four talking about how to organise it.’

Felix went last.

‘I am nearly finished,’ he said. ‘Which after three years is a strange thing to say out loud.’

‘Say the number,’ Priya asked.

‘Eighty-one thousand words of a projected ninety.’ Felix was quiet about it. ‘Which means I am thinking about what happens afterward, and I have never not had this book to work on.’


## What each of them will build

‘Now the practical half,’ Suki said. ‘Because everybody has described a change and I want to know what anybody is actually doing about it.’

Meera answered first.

‘Two separate arrangements rather than one shared one,’ she said. ‘Her patients in her folder on her machine. Mine in mine.’

‘Which is the simplest possible answer,’ Claire said.

‘Which is the simplest and the only one I am comfortable with, because anything shared creates a route by which I could open a record that is not mine.’ Meera was definite. ‘And a route that exists is a route somebody eventually takes.’

‘What do you share, then?’

‘Nothing clinical. A vocabulary.’ She counted. ‘We will use the same section structure and the same descriptions, so that when we discuss a case in supervision we are speaking about material shaped the same way.’

‘Which is a convention rather than a connection.’

‘Which is a convention, and I have come to think that a shared convention is what people usually mean when they say they want a shared system.’

Raj described something similar arrived at differently.

‘We are keeping separate material and agreeing on markers,’ he said.

‘Say what that means practically.’

‘Two people, two folders, two ways of working.’ Raj counted. ‘And one agreed list of stage markers, so that when either of us says something is in a particular phase we mean the same thing.’

‘Which is the minimum.’

‘Which is the minimum and I suspect it is also the maximum that survives a difficult shoot.’ He was frank. ‘Anything requiring both of us to maintain a shared structure will collapse in week three.’

Claire had thought about hers considerably.

‘A structure by practice area at the top, which I already have, with each attorney beneath their own areas,’ she said.

‘Say how you would do it.’

‘Slowly and with two people first.’ Claire was precise. ‘Two attorneys, one practice area, for a quarter, before anybody else is asked to change anything.’

‘Which is testing it.’

‘Which is testing it on people who will tell me honestly, and which is what I did not do when I restructured my own material and regretted.’

Tomas was starting fresh.

‘Everything from the old school archived and closed,’ he said. ‘New structure for the new one, built with what I have learned.’

‘Which is not migrating.’

‘Which is deliberately not migrating, because my structure at the old school grew around that school's timetable, its equipment, and its examination arrangements.’ Tomas counted. ‘None of which follows me.’

‘So what does?’

‘The lesson material and the conventions. Which is about two hundred entries out of six hundred and which is the part that was ever really mine.’

Suki was adding a branch.

‘A separate area for training material,’ she said. ‘Alongside the client work, not inside it.’

‘Say why alongside.’

‘Because training material is written to be read by other people and client material is written to be read by me.’ Suki was definite. ‘Which is a different kind of writing and mixing them would produce two things done badly.’

David described a negotiation.

‘Michelle and I will keep separate material and share three things,’ he said.

‘Say the three.’

‘The event calendar. The rota. And a set of descriptions for the kinds of thing a church does.’ David counted. ‘Which covers everything either of us actually needs from the other and touches nothing pastoral.’

‘Which is the boundary.’

‘Which is a boundary we discussed for about an hour and which we both wanted in the same place, which was a relief.’

Priya was rebuilding.

‘From a thesis structure to a research structure,’ she said. ‘Which means the chapter descriptions retire and something else replaces them.’

‘Say what.’

‘Research questions rather than chapters.’ Priya was thinking aloud. ‘Because a chapter is a unit of a document and a research question is a unit of work, and I will have questions long after the document is finished.’

‘Which is a better organising principle.’

‘Which is a more durable one, and I built the first structure around a document because the document was the goal.’

Marcus was adding rather than changing.

‘A second top-level area for commercial, alongside residential,’ he said.

‘Say why not one property area.’

‘Because they are genuinely different.’ Marcus counted. ‘Different regulation, different lease structures, different questions. Putting them together would produce a filter that returns two unrelated kinds of thing.’

‘Which is Claire's problem from the other direction.’

‘Which is exactly her problem and I am solving it before it exists rather than after, having watched her solve it after.’

Lena was creating a phase.

‘An area for the book process,’ she said. ‘Separate from the investigation.’

‘Say the distinction.’

‘The investigation holds evidence. The book process holds correspondence with a publisher, verification records, and legal queries.’ Lena was precise. ‘Which are about the evidence rather than being evidence, and mixing them would make my sources harder to find in exactly the period when I most need them.’

Amir was extending.

‘Two shops means two of everything,’ he said. ‘Which Yasmin says is easy and which I am not sure about.’

‘Say what you will actually do,’ Marcus asked.

‘One area for each shop and one for the things that are both.’ Amir counted. ‘Recipes are both. Suppliers are mostly both. Daily takings are separate.’

‘Which is a sensible division.’

‘Which is a division my nephew suggested and I am taking credit for.’

Nkechi was building for a purpose.

‘An area for the examination, with subjects beneath it,’ she said. ‘Which is what I did for the last qualification and which worked.’

‘Say what is different this time.’

‘Eighteen months rather than four, and considerably more material.’ Nkechi was matter of fact. ‘Which means the structure has to hold up under a volume I have not previously handled.’

‘Do you think it will?’

‘I think the structure will and I am less certain about me, which is an honest answer at half past four in the morning.’

Felix went last again.

‘An area for whatever is next,’ he said. ‘Which currently contains nothing.’

‘Say what you think it will contain.’

‘I have two ideas and I am not going to describe either of them.’ Felix was cheerful. ‘Because I have learned that describing a project before it exists is how you talk yourself out of it.’

‘That is a considerably better answer than an outline,’ Lena said.


## Why any of this is possible

‘I want to say something about what we have just listed,’ Suki said, ‘because I have been counting and it is unusual.’

‘Say it.’

‘Twelve people have described twelve changes, and not one of them requires anything to be replaced.’ She counted. ‘Restructuring, adding, archiving, separating, and creating something new. All of which are operations within the same arrangement.’

‘Which is not remarkable,’ Amir said.

‘Which is extremely remarkable and you would only know that if you had watched organisations attempt it.’ Suki was definite. ‘I have assessed places where adding a second department meant a migration project.’

‘Say why it works here,’ Claire asked.

‘Four properties and I will be brief.’ Suki counted. ‘The structure can be rearranged and nothing becomes unreachable, because moving something changes where it lives and nothing else.’

‘Which Claire and Tomas have both done.’

‘Which we have both done and both survived,’ Claire said. ‘Mine took a weekend and a disorienting fortnight and nothing was lost.’

‘Second property,’ Suki continued. ‘The vocabulary can be revised. Descriptions can be renamed and everything carrying them carries the new name.’

‘Which is the thing I avoided for months because I thought it was drastic,’ Claire said.

‘Third. New kinds of material can be added without disturbing existing kinds.’ Suki was precise. ‘Which is why Marcus can add commercial alongside residential and I can add training alongside consulting, without either of us touching what we already have.’

‘And fourth?’

‘That the whole thing can be duplicated when work genuinely divides.’ She was matter of fact. ‘Which is what Meera is doing, and what I do for every client engagement, and which is the property people notice last.’

‘Say more about that one,’ Meera asked.

‘A separate folder is a separate everything.’ Suki counted. ‘Separate structure, separate vocabulary, separate protection, no route between them. Which for your situation is not a convenience. It is the requirement.’

‘Which is why I am comfortable taking on a colleague at all.’

Tomas raised the practical version.

‘There is a fifth thing and it is unglamorous,’ he said.

‘Say it.’

‘That starting a new one costs nothing.’ Tomas was matter of fact. ‘I am setting up at a new school in September. There is nothing to install and nothing to procure and nothing to ask anybody's permission for.’

‘Which matters in a school.’

‘Which matters enormously in a school, where anything requiring approval takes a term.’ He shrugged. ‘I will open a file and choose a folder and that will be the whole of it.’

Amir said something that made Suki laugh.

‘I do not understand any of the four properties,’ he said. ‘And I understand the fifth one perfectly, and it is the reason my nephew will have this at the second shop within an hour of asking.’

‘Which is the correct summary,’ Suki said.

Lena added something about permanence.

‘There is a property nobody has mentioned and it is the one I rely on most,’ she said.

‘Say it.’

‘That none of these changes involves asking anybody's permission or depending on anybody's continued existence.’ Lena was precise. ‘Everything we have described is somebody rearranging files in a folder they control.’

‘Which sounds obvious.’

‘Which is obvious and is not how most of this works, and it means that everything we have planned this afternoon is a plan we can actually execute.’ She was definite. ‘I have watched organisational plans die because a service changed its terms.’


## The thing Lena said that stopped the call

There was a section near the end that David had not planned and that everybody afterward said was the reason the call mattered.

It began with Priya asking Lena a question.

‘You have been quieter than usual,’ she said. ‘And you said one thing about permission that I want you to expand.’

Lena took a moment.

‘I have been quiet because I am the only person here whose change is an ending rather than a beginning,’ she said. ‘And I have been listening to eleven people describe growth.’

‘Say what you mean by ending.’

‘The investigation goes to a publisher and then it is somebody else's.’ She was precise. ‘Which after two years is a strange thing to arrange for.’

‘Felix is ending something,’ Amir said.

‘Felix is finishing a book about his own life, which he keeps.’ Lena was matter of fact. ‘I am handing over an account of eleven people who trusted me, to lawyers and editors, and the handing over is the part I have not solved.’

‘Say what the problem is,’ Suki asked.

‘That a publisher's legal team will want to see the evidence behind every claim.’ Lena counted. ‘Which is reasonable and necessary and involves showing material to people I have never met.’

‘Which includes source material.’

‘Which includes some source material and must not include all of it, and the line between those two is the most consequential decision of the whole project.’ She was definite. ‘And it is a decision about people rather than about documents.’

Meera said something quietly.

‘That is the same decision I make about a report to another clinician.’

‘Say it.’

‘What is necessary for the purpose and nothing beyond it.’ Meera was precise. ‘Which sounds simple and is the hardest judgement in my profession, because the temptation is always to include one more thing that provides context.’

‘And context is how people become identifiable,’ Lena said.

‘Context is exactly how people become identifiable, and I have watched a colleague do it accidentally in a case report.’

There was a pause on the call.

‘I want to say something that is not about software,’ Lena said. ‘Because I have been on this call for an hour listening to people plan expansions and I want to name what none of us has said.’

‘Say it.’

‘Every one of us holds material about people who did not choose to be in our records.’ She counted. ‘Patients. Sources. Congregants. Students. Tenants. Interviewees. Crew.’

‘Which is true,’ David said.

‘Which is true of eleven of the twelve and the twelfth is Amir, who holds recipes.’ Lena almost smiled. ‘And every plan described this afternoon involves that material moving somewhere or being shared with somebody or being handed over.’

‘Which is worth stating before anybody executes anything,’ Claire said.

‘Which is why I am stating it, because the arrangements are all sound and the judgements are all ours.’

Amir spoke after a moment.

‘I hold recipes and I also hold eleven things about my staff,’ he said. ‘Which I had not thought about until you listed the professions and I noticed I was in it too.’

‘Say what they are.’

‘Why somebody left. Why somebody needed a fortnight off.’ Amir shrugged. ‘Small things, and they are about people, and my nephew is about to have access to everything.’

‘Which is a decision you now know you have to make.’

‘Which is a decision I did not know I had until four minutes ago,’ he said, ‘and I am glad I found out on a call rather than afterward.’


## What they would like

‘Since we are looking forward,’ David said, ‘I want to ask what anybody hopes for.’

‘Say what you mean,’ Priya asked.

‘Something you would find useful that you have thought about.’ He was matter of fact. ‘Not a complaint. A thing you have imagined.’

Meera went first.

‘Supervised sharing,’ she said. ‘Which is specific to my situation and which I have thought about carefully.’

‘Say it.’

‘When I supervise my colleague on a case, we discuss a patient who is hers.’ Meera counted. ‘And it would be useful to look at the same record together, during that session, with her having opened it.’

‘Which is a controlled circumstance.’

‘Which is an entirely controlled circumstance with both of us present, and which is different from any arrangement where I could reach it alone.’ She was precise. ‘The distinction I care about is between something she shows me and something I can take.’

Raj had a smaller one.

‘Colour on the cards,’ he said. ‘On the board.’

‘Say what for.’

‘Because when I co-direct, the board will hold two people's material, and I would like to see whose at a glance.’ Raj shrugged. ‘Which the descriptions tell me if I read them and which colour would tell me without reading.’

‘Which is the same argument Claire made about her structure.’

‘Which is the same argument and I stole it from her about ten minutes ago.’

Priya's was about the suggestions.

‘I would like to know how well trained mine is,’ she said.

‘Say what you mean.’

‘My contextual suggestions are useful now and were not eighteen months ago.’ Priya was precise. ‘And I have no sense of where in that progression I currently sit.’

‘Which matters why?’

‘Because when I hand my material to somebody after my viva, they will inherit suggestions trained by me, and I would like to be able to tell them how much to trust them.’ She was thoughtful. ‘Which is a strange thing to want and I want it.’

Marcus wanted something about comparison.

‘A way of marking one thing as the reference,’ he said. ‘When I have four open.’

‘Say it properly.’

‘I keep one ordinary building open as a baseline while the others rotate.’ Marcus counted. ‘And I would like that one to look different, so I never accidentally close it.’

‘Have you closed it?’

‘Twice, and both times I did not notice for an hour and my comparisons that hour were worse.’

Nkechi's was about the long horizon.

‘Something for material I will need in eighteen months and not before,’ she said.

‘Say the case.’

‘My examination is a long way off and I am accumulating material for it constantly.’ Nkechi was matter of fact. ‘Which is all present all the time, and most of it is not relevant this month.’

‘Which is a filtering question.’

‘Which is a question about time rather than subject, and I currently solve it with descriptions and it is slightly clumsy.’

Felix said his was not a capability.

‘I would like somebody to write about how to use this at seventy-two,’ he said.

There was a pause.

‘Say what you mean,’ Meera asked.

‘Everything anybody has written about tools of this kind assumes somebody building a career.’ Felix was matter of fact. ‘I am not building anything. I am closing something, and the questions are different.’

‘Say the questions.’

‘What happens to it. Who reads it. Whether my daughter will find any of this navigable in fifteen years.’ He shrugged. ‘Which nobody addresses because it does not sell anything and which is the question I actually have.’

‘That is a considerably more interesting wish than mine,’ Raj said.

‘It is a wish that somebody writes something rather than that somebody builds something,’ Felix said. ‘Which may be within our own power.’


## Looking forward

David called for a final round, one sentence each, on what anybody was actually looking forward to.

Meera said she was looking forward to having somebody in the next room to talk to about a difficult case, after fifteen years of not having one.

Raj said he was looking forward to arguing with somebody about a film rather than deciding everything alone, which he expected to hate and to need.

Claire said she was looking forward to finding out whether anything she had built was transferable, which she suspected was the real question underneath the firm's request.

Tomas said he was looking forward to a new building with equipment that works, and to a first day where nobody knows what he is like.

Suki said she was looking forward to teaching, which she had avoided for years on the grounds that she was not a teacher and had recently reconsidered.

David said he was looking forward to Michelle running the youth programme entirely, which she has effectively been doing for a year without the title.

Priya said she was looking forward to being asked a question she cannot answer by two people who are not trying to be kind, and to finding out what she actually knows.

Marcus said he was looking forward to being a beginner again at something, which he had not been for a long time and had missed more than he expected.

Lena said she was looking forward to somebody else reading it, after two years of being the only person who has.

Amir said he was looking forward to his nephew making something better than he can, which he had not expected to be able to say without minding.

Nkechi said she was looking forward to eighteen months of being very tired for a good reason.

Felix said he was looking forward to finding out what he thinks about, when there is no longer a book to think about, which he had been avoiding wondering.

There was a short silence after that.

‘We should do this again,’ Priya said.

‘We should do this in person,’ Amir said. ‘And I will feed everybody, which I am saying now so that it becomes an obligation.’

‘I will send postcards from every port until then,’ Nkechi said. ‘Which I mean literally and which will take a while.’

‘And I will mention all of you in the acknowledgements,’ Felix said. ‘Which I have already drafted, and which none of you can prevent.’


## The arrangement that came with them

The question nobody asks when adopting a tool is whether it will still serve them when their circumstances have changed, and the omission is expensive because circumstances change reliably rather than occasionally. What makes it difficult to assess is that the relevant properties are invisible during stable periods, which are most periods, and decisive during the transitions that arrive unpredictably.

The twelve situations described here arrived at once by coincidence and cover most of the transitions professional life produces. Acquiring a colleague. Collaborating for the first time. Being asked whether an individual practice can become an organisational one. Changing employer. Adding a second kind of work. Sharing part of a practice with somebody while withholding the rest. Moving from a qualification to a career. Diversifying. Completing a long project. Doubling a business. Beginning a substantial course of study. And finishing something that has occupied three years.

Not one of those required anything to be replaced. All of them are operations within the same arrangement: rearranging, adding, archiving, separating, or beginning something new. A practitioner who assesses systems professionally described that as unusual, having examined organisations where adding a department constituted a migration project.

Four properties account for it, and they are worth naming because they are what somebody should look for rather than what any feature list emphasises. The structure can be rearranged without anything becoming unreachable, since moving something changes where it lives and nothing else, which two participants have done and survived. The vocabulary can be revised without invalidating what already carries it, which one participant avoided for months because renaming felt drastic and which turned out to be undramatic. New kinds of material can be added without disturbing existing kinds, which is how commercial work sits alongside residential and training alongside consulting. And the whole thing can be duplicated when work genuinely divides, which is what makes a second clinician's records genuinely separate rather than nominally so.

A fifth property is unglamorous and produced the most enthusiastic agreement. Starting a new one costs nothing: no installation, no procurement, no approval. A teacher moving schools described this as decisive in an institution where anything requiring authorisation takes a term. A baker who understood none of the first four properties understood the fifth immediately and observed that his nephew would have it running at a second shop within an hour of asking.

The one participant working under adversarial conditions added something the others had not stated. None of the changes described requires anybody's permission or depends on any organisation continuing to exist. Every plan discussed is somebody rearranging files in a folder they control, which means the plans can actually be executed, which she contrasted with organisational plans she has watched die when a service altered its terms.

The collaboration cases produced a consistent answer that nobody had agreed in advance. In every instance where two people needed to work together, the arrangement was separate material plus a shared convention. Separate folders and identical section structures for two clinicians. Separate folders and an agreed set of stage markers for two directors. Separate everything and three shared elements for a minister and his colleague. A shared convention, one of them observed, is what people usually mean when they say they want a shared system, and it survives a difficult period in a way that a jointly maintained structure does not.

The wishes section was deliberately forward-looking and produced one that was not about capability at all. A memoirist approaching seventy-three noted that everything written about tools of this kind assumes somebody building a career, and that he is closing something rather than building it. His questions concern what happens to the material, who reads it, and whether his daughter will find it navigable in fifteen years. He described that as a question nobody addresses because it sells nothing, and noted that answering it may be within the group's own power rather than requiring anything from anybody else.

What the final round revealed is that none of them was looking forward to a capability. They were looking forward to a colleague in the next room after fifteen years alone, to arguing with somebody about a film, to being a beginner again, to somebody else finally reading two years of work, to a nephew doing something better than they can, and to eighteen months of being very tired for a good reason.

Which is the appropriate place for a discussion about tools to end. The arrangement they have all been describing is not what any of them cares about. It is what allowed them to stop caring about it, and to spend the attention elsewhere, which is what every one of them is now planning to do in a different direction.


## FAQs

### Why is future adaptability the question nobody asks?

Because somebody choosing a tool is solving a present problem, and the present problem is vivid while future ones are hypothetical. Practitioners describe the omission as understandable and expensive, since professional life changes reliably rather than occasionally, and the properties that matter during a transition are invisible during the stable periods when most assessments happen. Practitioners across entirely different professions describe the same requirement, which suggests it follows from how professional work changes rather than from any particular field. It is one of the properties that is invisible during stable periods and decisive during the transitions nobody schedules. Anybody uncertain can test the question against their own arrangement in a few minutes rather than reasoning about it.

### How does a tool fail to accommodate change?

Gradually rather than suddenly. Practitioners describe a structure that has to be worked around, a vocabulary that no longer describes the material, and assumptions that fit somebody's situation two years earlier. Nothing stops functioning; people adapt, the adaptations accumulate, and eventually somebody quietly begins keeping things elsewhere. The cost of getting this wrong is not a failure but a slow accumulation of workarounds nobody notices until somebody has stopped using the arrangement. Stating the reasoning rather than the rule is what allows somebody to apply it to a change the rule was not written for. Practitioners across entirely different professions describe the same requirement, which suggests it follows from how professional work changes rather than from any particular field.
