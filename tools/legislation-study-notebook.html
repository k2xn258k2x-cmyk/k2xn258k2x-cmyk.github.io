The titles

Eleven titles, each with what it requires, the authority Congress claimed for it, and how much of it survives in the form it was written. Tap a title to open it.

How it passed

Six procedural gates, in order. Each is a place where a bill can die, each has a standard technique for getting through it, and each cost something. The gates are general: any major bill faces the same sequence.

The reusable lesson. A bill is not passed by persuading a majority, it is passed by clearing every gate in turn, and a majority that exists at gate one is worthless if the bill never reaches gate five. Most legislation dies in committee or on the calendar rather than on the floor, which is why procedural control matters more than headcount.

The votes

Four roll calls decided it. Each is shown two ways, by party and by region, because the two tell different stories and only one of them is a good predictor of how a member voted.

Vote totals here follow the standard published tallies and are given to show the pattern rather than to settle a dispute about any individual member. For a specific member's vote, check the official roll call record, which is the only authority.

Title VII in practice

What the enforcement process actually looks like, stage by stage, and a calculator for the deadlines that end more claims than any argument on the merits.

Charge clock calculator
These are the general federal periods and they are not the only rule that can apply. Continuing violations, pay discrimination under the Ledbetter amendment, different state deadlines, and equitable tolling all change the answer. This calculator shows the ordinary case so you know roughly where you stand, and anyone with a real claim needs a lawyer well before the date it produces.

The ledger

The construction history as a single sequence. Courts read the statute, Congress sometimes disagreed and amended it, and the pattern of who moved last is the most instructive thing about the whole statute.

The pattern worth writing about. On several occasions the Supreme Court narrowed the statute and Congress responded by amending the text to restore the broader reading. That sequence is unusual, and it makes this statute one of the clearest available demonstrations that statutory interpretation is a conversation between branches rather than a final word by either.

Extending this

Everything renders from five arrays. Adding a title, a gate, a roll call or a ledger entry is appending one object, and the framework generalises to any statute.
