The technology industry has declared RSS dead at least three times, with confidence and considerable coverage on each occasion. A major company retired its reader and the obituaries were written that week. Social platforms replaced chronological feeds of things people had chosen with algorithmic timelines of things they had not, and the argument was that nobody wanted to curate anything. Push notification replaced pulling content when you wanted it, on the theory that the important thing about information is that it should arrive.

Each of those declarations was accurate about the mainstream and wrong about the underlying need, which is why the format persists quietly among people who never argued about it. What survives is not nostalgia. It is a small population of professionals who noticed that the replacements solved a different problem from the one they had, and who quietly kept using something that solved theirs.

![The Reader Nobody Expected to Love](/assets/img/feature-screenshot-placeholder.svg)

The problem in question is specific and rarely named. It is not access to information, which is abundant to the point of being a burden. It is following a small number of particular sources, reliably, without anything deciding on your behalf what you see from them. That sounds modest and turns out to be almost impossible to arrange by any other means, because every other channel introduces an intermediary with its own objectives.
<!--more-->


An algorithmic timeline is optimised for engagement, which is a measurement of how long somebody stays rather than of whether they learned anything. Those two things correlate loosely and diverge exactly where it matters. A thoughtful piece from somebody with two hundred readers will lose, every time, to something designed to provoke, and the loss is invisible: nobody is told what they did not see. A person following twelve writers on such a platform is following an algorithm's opinion of twelve writers.

Electronic mail is the other common substitute and it fails differently. A subscription arriving in an inbox lands alongside invoices, requests, scheduling, and everything else demanding a response. Reading becomes an interruption competing with obligations, which means it happens badly or not at all, and the material accumulates as a source of guilt rather than a source of ideas.

What a feed reader provides is narrow and unusual: exactly what was subscribed to, nothing else, ordered by time rather than by anybody's judgment, available when the reader decides to look. There is no ranking, no insertion, no notification, and no mechanism attempting to increase the frequency of visits. Somebody who does not open it for eleven days finds eleven days of material waiting, which is the correct behaviour and is precisely what platforms are designed to prevent.

The addition worth noticing is what happens when the reader sits inside the place somebody already keeps their thinking rather than in a separate application. Reading and writing stop being separate activities occurring in different windows. An article that provokes something becomes an entry immediately, filed where it belongs, while the provocation is still live, which is a workflow rather than an intention. Anybody who has read something excellent, resolved to write about it later, and never done so understands why that distance matters.

What follows is a conversation between three people who arrived at this independently, none of whom would describe themselves as technical, and all of whom are more enthusiastic about it than they expected to be.


## Three men, none of them under any illusions

Raj had proposed the call and had been slightly apologetic about the reason.

‘I want to say at the outset that this is the least interesting subject any of us could have chosen,’ he said, ‘and that I have been looking forward to it for a fortnight.’

‘Then say why,’ said Felix, from a study with more books visible than wall.

‘Because I mentioned it to a colleague last month and he laughed at me.’ Raj was cheerful about it. ‘Actually laughed. He said something about a technology his father used, and then asked whether I still burn things onto discs.’

‘And what did you say?’

‘I said nothing useful, which is why I have been thinking about it since.’ He shrugged. ‘I know it works. I could not explain why it works better than what he uses.’

David joined a moment later, adjusting something and apologising for the delay.

‘I have the opposite problem,’ he said. ‘Nobody in my life has ever heard of it, so there is no argument. I mentioned it to our elder board and got the expression people produce when a minister uses a word they were not expecting.’

Felix laughed.

‘I have a third position, which is that I used this thirty years ago and stopped and did not notice I had stopped,’ he said. ‘Which I find more interesting than either of yours, because it means something was lost quietly rather than taken.’

‘Say what you mean.’

‘I mean that nobody decided to stop following the people they had chosen to follow.’ He turned a hand over. ‘It simply became difficult, and then the difficulty became normal, and then a generation arrived who had never had it and could not miss it. That is not a technology being replaced. That is a habit being eroded.’

‘That is considerably more serious than I intended this conversation to be,’ Raj said.

‘It is nine in the morning where I am. I get one grand statement before eleven and I have used it.’


## What each of them is actually following

‘Let us do the boring part first,’ David said. ‘What are you following and how is it arranged?’

Felix went first.

‘About thirty feeds and I would say roughly a third of them are things nobody else would consider a feed.’ He shared. ‘Three folders. Academic, literary, personal.’

‘Explain personal.’

‘Retired colleagues who write.’ He said it plainly. ‘Eleven of them. People I taught alongside for twenty or thirty years, who now, in their seventies, write things on their own sites that four hundred people read.’

‘And that is the folder you open first.’

‘That is the folder I open first every single morning and I would give up the other two before I gave up that one.’ Felix was definite. ‘Because those are the people whose thinking I used to encounter in a corridor, and I do not have a corridor any more. This is the corridor.’

David made a sound of recognition.

‘Academic is journals and blogs from people still in post, which I follow to remain informed rather than because I enjoy it. Literary is reviews, essays, and two literary journals, which is where most of what I steal comes from.’

‘Steal?’

‘A memoir is a work of prose,’ Felix said. ‘If I only read history I will write like a historian, which is what forty years of habit has already made likely. Reading essayists is how I keep the sentences from calcifying.’

David went next.

‘Fifteen feeds and I am the least ambitious person on this call.’ He shared his own. ‘Theology, which is writers and a couple of institutions. Ministry resources, which is practical material about running programmes. News, which is denominational and mostly administrative. And people, which is new.’

‘Say what new means.’

‘I had four folders organised entirely by relationship until about a fortnight ago.’ David was slightly rueful. ‘People I know personally. Institutions I am accountable to. The field generally. And one I deleted, which was people I felt I ought to be reading and never did.’

‘What changed?’

‘I explained the arrangement out loud to somebody and heard myself doing it, which is a considerably more effective audit than any amount of thinking.’ He shrugged. ‘I noticed the obligation folder, deleted it, and then found I was not sure the relationship scheme was right either.’

‘So you are mid-argument with yourself.’

‘I am mid-argument with myself and I have arrived at a mixture, which I suspect is where these things end up.’ He counted. ‘Topic for the material I read to stay informed. Relationship for the people whose writing I care about specifically. Because those are genuinely different reasons for following somebody.’

Raj shared last.

‘Twenty-five feeds and three folders, and mine are shaped by the fact that I am always in one of two states,’ he said. ‘Craft, business, festivals.’

‘Explain the states.’

‘Shooting, in which case I read nothing at all for six weeks, or between projects, in which case I read obsessively for a fortnight and then start something.’ He laughed. ‘My folders are organised for the second state, and the first state is why the whole arrangement has to tolerate being ignored.’

‘Craft is technique?’

‘Craft is technique and equipment. Business is funding announcements and commissioning news, which is genuinely how I find out that money exists. And festivals is submission deadlines and programming announcements, which is a calendar I do not maintain, maintained by other people.’

‘That last one is a good use I would not have thought of,’ Felix said.

‘It is the one that has directly earned me money,’ Raj said, ‘which I mention because everything else on this call is going to be about the life of the mind and I would like one item on the ledger.’


## The part that is not about reading

‘Now the thing I actually wanted to talk about,’ Raj said, ‘which is what happens after you read something.’

‘Say your version first,’ said David, ‘because I suspect mine is the simplest and I want to go last.’

‘Mine is that a feed item is a starting point rather than a thing to keep.’ Raj was definite. ‘About one item in thirty produces a thought, and the thought is the valuable part, and it has a life of about four minutes before it evaporates.’

‘Four minutes.’

‘Perhaps six. It is short.’ He shrugged. ‘Which is why the thing that matters is not the reader. It is that the reader is inside the place where I write.’

‘Describe what you actually do.’

‘I read something about a community of weavers in a region I have never been to.’ He was pulling something up as he spoke. ‘Three paragraphs in a newsletter from an organisation I follow. And something goes off, which is not an idea yet, it is a feeling that there is something there.’

‘And?’

‘And I make an entry immediately. Not a bookmark. An entry, in my vault, with two lines about where it came from and then whatever I can get down before the thing fades.’ He showed one. ‘This is four sentences and it is mostly nonsense. It says something about hands, and about a process that takes eleven days, and a question about who is buying.’

‘That is a project.’

‘That is a project which is now in development and which has a page and about forty entries under it,’ Raj said. ‘And it began as four sentences typed in about ninety seconds because I did not have to go anywhere to type them.’

Felix leaned forward.

‘That last clause is the whole thing and I want to make sure it does not go past unnoticed,’ he said. ‘You did not have to go anywhere.’

‘Say why that matters so much to you.’

‘Because I have spent forty years watching thoughts die in transit.’ Felix said it evenly. ‘I read something, I think, I should write about that, and then I open something else, and by the time it is open the thought has changed shape or gone. Every intervening step is an opportunity for it to escape.’

‘And now?’

‘And now the reading and the writing are the same window and there is no transit.’ He shrugged. ‘Which sounds trivial and is the difference between a hundred entries and none.’

He described his own version, which was more specific than Raj's.

‘One of my retired colleagues wrote about a departmental meeting from decades ago.’ He said it carefully. ‘Not about me. She was writing about something else entirely and mentioned the room in passing.’

‘And you were in the room.’

‘I was in the room and I had not thought about it since, and reading her description brought back an entire afternoon, including something I said that I am not proud of.’ Felix paused. ‘That entry exists because I wrote it within about ninety seconds of reading her paragraph. If I had waited until the evening I would have had a summary. What I have instead is the thing itself, including the part where I sound worse than I would have chosen.’

‘Is it in the book?’

‘It is a chapter,’ he said. ‘Which is not what I expected when I subscribed to a friend's blog.’

David went last, as he had said he would.

‘Mine is the simplest and it is a habit rather than a technique,’ he said. ‘I read for forty minutes with coffee before anybody in the building is awake. And when something sets something off, I write an entry, in my own words, about what it made me think rather than about what it said.’

‘Never the article itself?’

‘Almost never, and I want to explain the rule because I arrived at it after doing the opposite for six months.’ He counted it out. ‘At first I kept articles. Saved them, filed them, felt organised. And in six months I had perhaps ninety saved articles and had returned to none of them, because a saved article is somebody else's thinking sitting in my folder and I could not remember why I had kept any of it.’

‘And now?’

‘Now I write two lines about the source and then whatever the thing provoked, for as long as it runs, and underneath there is a section listing what it connects to.’ He was definite. ‘About a fifth of what I have written this year began that way.’

‘A fifth is enormous,’ Raj said.

‘A fifth is enormous and I do not think it is about the technology at all.’ David paused. ‘It is that I have colleagues again. Not people I talk to. People whose thinking arrives regularly and provokes a response, which is what a department does for somebody and which I have not had since seminary.’

Felix was quiet for a moment.

‘That is my corridor,’ he said.

‘It is the same thing exactly.’

‘It is the same thing exactly and we have arrived at it from a bakery, a church, and a farmhouse,’ Felix said, ‘and the mechanism is a technology two of my former colleagues would tell me is obsolete.’

The labelling came up because Raj asked, and produced a small disagreement.

‘Do you label these differently from anything else?’

‘I have one label for it,’ David said. ‘Everything that began as a reaction to something I read carries it, so I can gather them.’

‘Why does that matter?’

‘Because reading them as a set once a quarter tells me what I have been circling.’ He was matter of fact. ‘Twelve entries about the same underlying question, written across three months from three different provocations, is a sermon series and I would never have seen it as one without the label.’

‘I do the opposite,’ Raj said. ‘I label by project, and if a thought does not belong to a project yet, it has no label at all until it does.’

‘Does that not lose things?’

‘It loses them for about a fortnight and then the widget hands one back to me at random and I know what to do with it.’ He grinned. ‘Which is an argument for not being organised that I would not make about anything else.’


## Why not the things everybody else uses

‘Right,’ Raj said. ‘I want to solve my problem, which is that I could not answer my colleague. Why is this better than what he does?’

‘What does he do?’

‘He follows the same people on two platforms and gets things in a newsletter.’ Raj shrugged. ‘Which is not unreasonable. Those are the normal answers.’

Felix took the first one.

‘A platform decides what you see, and the decision is not made in your interest.’ He said it without heat. ‘That is not a complaint about anybody's morals. It is a description of what those systems optimise for, which is time spent, and time spent is not the same as anything I want.’

‘Make it concrete.’

‘I follow eleven retired academics who write carefully about small subjects for small audiences.’ He counted. ‘On a platform, those eleven are competing with everything else in an ordering somebody else controls, and they will lose, because a careful essay about a departmental reform is not designed to hold anybody's attention against material that is.’

‘So you would see them occasionally.’

‘I would see perhaps two of the eleven, chosen for me, and I would not be told about the other nine.’ He was definite. ‘Which is the part that troubles me most. An absence produces no evidence. I would simply believe I was following them.’

David took the second.

‘Mine is about the container rather than the ordering,’ he said. ‘Everything that arrives in my mail is a thing somebody wants from me. A meeting, a question, a form, a request, a difficulty.’

‘And reading material lands in the same place.’

‘Reading material lands in the same place and therefore competes with obligation, and obligation wins every time, correctly.’ He shrugged. ‘So I would open a thoughtful essay while feeling guilty about the four things underneath it, which means I would either skim it or leave it, and after a fortnight there would be thirty of them and I would feel worse.’

‘That is exactly my experience,’ Raj said.

‘Whereas this has its own room.’ David said it simply. ‘I go into it deliberately, when I have decided to read rather than to respond, and nothing in there is asking me for anything.’

Raj took the third himself.

‘Mine is about who initiates.’ He was working it out as he spoke. ‘A notification decides when I read. Which sounds neutral and is not, because the moment somebody else chooses is never the moment I would have chosen, and there is no such thing as a small interruption during an edit.’

‘And this does not interrupt.’

‘This does not interrupt, and more importantly it does not punish being ignored.’ He was emphatic. ‘I disappear for six weeks during a shoot. When I come back it is all there, in order, with nothing shouting about how much I missed.’

‘There is no count telling you that you are behind,’ Felix said.

‘There is no count telling me I am behind and I did not realise how much I hated that until it was absent.’ Raj shook his head. ‘Every other thing in my life has a number on it telling me I have failed to keep up with something. This is the only channel that has never once made me feel that way.’

David added the last argument, which he said was the one he would use.

‘And it is inside the place where I keep my thinking, which none of the alternatives are.’ He shrugged. ‘A separate application would give me everything Felix and Raj have described and would put a wall between the reading and the writing. That wall is where my ideas used to die.’

‘Then that is my answer,’ Raj said. ‘Four sentences. Nothing chooses for me, it has its own room, it never interrupts, and it is in the same place as my notes.’

‘Your colleague will not be persuaded,’ Felix said.

‘My colleague will not be persuaded and I will have said something true, which is a different objective.’


## Twenty-five feeds and no guilt

‘Practical question,’ David said. ‘How do you stop this becoming another thing you are behind on? Because I have fifteen feeds and I am at my limit, and both of you have more.’

‘I read almost none of it,’ Felix said immediately.

‘Say more.’

‘Thirty feeds, and I would estimate I read perhaps one item in eight.’ He was entirely unbothered. ‘The personal folder I read properly. Academic I scan headings and open perhaps two things a week. Literary I dip into when I want prose rather than information.’

‘And the rest?’

‘Goes past, and I have made peace with that, and making peace with it is the whole skill.’ He leaned back. ‘A feed is not a list of obligations. It is a river. You are not behind on a river.’

David laughed.

‘I read most of mine,’ he said, ‘which is a function of having fifteen rather than any virtue. And I chose fifteen deliberately, because at twenty-five I would be doing what Felix does and I would rather read a small number carefully.’

‘Is that a temperament?’

‘It is a temperament and it is also my job.’ He was thoughtful. ‘I am not looking for information. I am looking for something that provokes a response, and one carefully read essay does that more reliably than nine skimmed ones.’

Raj described a third approach.

‘I scan everything and read almost nothing until something is relevant to an active project,’ he said. ‘Which means the same feed produces nothing for four months and then produces six useful items in a fortnight.’

‘Because your attention changes rather than the feed.’

‘Because my attention changes rather than the feed, exactly. An item about dyeing techniques is invisible to me in March and essential in September, and the item has not changed at all.’

They discussed folder prioritisation, which all three had arrived at differently.

‘Mine is essential and everything else,’ Raj said. ‘Business and festivals are the ones with consequences, so those are the folders I open when I have four minutes. Craft is what I read when I have an hour.’

‘Mine is not priority, it is mood,’ said Felix. ‘Personal in the morning with coffee. Academic when I am being dutiful. Literary in the evening when I want a sentence rather than a fact.’

‘That is a better organising principle than mine,’ David said.

‘It is a retired person's organising principle and it would be useless to either of you.’

‘Mine is closest to Raj's,’ David said. ‘Theology when I have time. Resources when I have a problem. News when I have four minutes and want to feel informed.’

‘And nothing tells you off for not opening them,’ Felix said.

‘Nothing tells me off, which after four decades of ministry is the single most restful property of any tool I have ever used.’


## The thing Raj had actually called about

‘I have been saving something,’ Raj said, ‘and I want to raise it because it has been bothering me for a month.’

‘Go.’

‘What happens when the thing disappears?’

There was a short silence.

‘Say what prompted it,’ Felix said.

‘A piece I read eighteen months ago about a craft cooperative. Three thousand words, by somebody who lived there for a year, and it was the best thing I had read on the subject.’ Raj was visibly annoyed. ‘I went back to it in October and the site is gone. Not the page. The site. The organisation folded and nobody thought about the writing.’

‘And you had a link.’

‘I had a link and a line in a note saying it was excellent, which is a description of something I no longer have.’

David made a noise.

‘I have had that with theology articles more times than I would like to admit,’ he said. ‘Denominational sites get restructured. Somebody redesigns and nothing survives the migration. And in one case an author took down eleven years of writing because of a controversy that had nothing to do with what I had read.’

‘Was it recoverable?’

‘Some of it, through an archive service, badly formatted, and only because I remembered enough of it to search.’ He shook his head. ‘Two things I could not find at all, and one of them I had quoted from the pulpit and could no longer verify.’

Felix's version was quieter and worse.

‘Three of the eleven people in my personal folder have died,’ he said.

Nobody said anything.

‘Which is what happens when your peer group is in its seventies, and it is not a complaint.’ He continued evenly. ‘Two of those sites are still up because a family member is paying for something they do not understand. One went down about four months after the funeral, presumably when a payment failed.’

‘What did you lose?’

‘About nine years of a man's thinking about teaching, written after he retired, which was better than anything he published while employed.’ Felix paused. ‘Four hundred readers. No publisher. No institution. It existed because he paid a small amount of money every year, and then he stopped being able to.’

‘Did you have any of it?’

‘I had four pieces, because I had written entries about them and quoted at length, which I did out of habit rather than foresight.’ He shrugged. ‘Everything else is gone and there is no archive because nobody archives a personal site with four hundred readers.’

Raj described what he had started doing, which he said came from a conversation with Lena.

‘She said something that has stayed with me. A link is not a record. It is a request to a stranger for permission to see something again later.’

‘That is exactly right,’ David said.

‘So when something in the reader matters, I do not bookmark it. I save it into the vault as an entry, with its content and where it came from, immediately.’ Raj was definite. ‘Which takes about four seconds and which I now do without thinking.’

‘How do you decide what matters?’

‘I have stopped deciding, which is her advice as well.’ He laughed. ‘Her rule is that judgment about significance requires knowing how a story develops, which you never do at the start. So she saves everything and discards nothing, and about one in twenty turns out to be a page that no longer exists.’

‘One in twenty is high,’ Felix said.

‘One in twenty is her number for investigative material, which is unusually fragile. Mine is lower.’ He shrugged. ‘But my number is not zero, and the cost of the nineteen is a few seconds each.’

Felix was quiet for a moment, and then said something the other two did not expect.

‘I am going to do the eleven,’ he said.

‘Meaning?’

‘Everything on the personal folder. All of it. Not the pieces that provoke something. Everything any of those eleven people has written and still has up.’ He was working out the scale as he spoke. ‘Which is perhaps six hundred pieces across eleven sites, and which would take me a fortnight of evenings, and which I am going to do because two of them are unwell and I know it.’

‘That is not an archiving strategy,’ David said gently. ‘That is something else.’

‘It is something else and I am aware of it,’ Felix said. ‘It is also the only version of a library any of those people are going to get, and I am the one with the time.’

Raj said nothing for a second.

‘I will help,’ he said. ‘If it is a fortnight of evenings for you it is four evenings for three of us.’

‘You do not know these people.’

‘I do not need to know them to click a button six hundred times,’ Raj said, ‘and I would like to have done it.’

‘I am in as well,’ said David.

Felix did not say anything for long enough that Raj checked his connection.

‘Then I will send you both a list on Thursday,’ he said eventually, ‘and we will not make this a large emotional occasion, because two of them would find that unbearable.’


## Where the feeds came from in the first place

‘Practical question I should have asked forty minutes ago,’ David said. ‘How did each of you find these?’

‘Slowly and badly,’ said Raj.

‘Say the method anyway.’

‘Three sources.’ Raj counted them. ‘Sites I already visited and had been visiting by hand for years, which is the obvious one and which nobody thinks of first. People those sites link to. And two lists that somebody in a forum posted, of which perhaps a third were useful and the rest were not for me.’

‘How long did it take to get to twenty-five?’

‘About four months, and I want to say that clearly, because when I started I expected to sit down for an evening and build a collection.’ He shrugged. ‘That is not how it works. You add one, read it for a fortnight, and either keep it or do not. Mine is the survivors of about sixty attempts.’

‘Sixty.’

‘Perhaps more. I remove things constantly.’ He was matter of fact. ‘Which is the part I would emphasise to anybody starting. Adding is not the decision. Removing is the decision, and you make it every few weeks, and after six months what remains is genuinely good.’

Felix had done something different.

‘I asked people,’ he said. ‘Directly. I wrote to nine former colleagues and asked what they read.’

‘That is not a method I would have considered,’ David said.

‘It is the oldest method there is and it works considerably better than any list.’ Felix was definite. ‘Because a recommendation from somebody whose thinking you respect is a signal, and a list from a stranger is a catalogue. Six of my thirty came from three of those replies.’

‘And the other question. Did they know what you were asking?’

‘Four of them did not, and two of those have since subscribed to the others, which was not my intention and is my favourite outcome from the whole exercise.’

David described his own, which he said was the least systematic.

‘Mine came from James, mostly, who set up the first four and then stopped, deliberately, so that I would have to work out the rest.’ He laughed. ‘Which annoyed me at the time and was correct.’

‘And you found the rest how?’

‘By noticing what I was already checking manually.’ He was rueful about it. ‘Which was a list I could have written in about ninety seconds and which I did not think to write for eleven months, because I did not think of my Saturday morning ritual as a list of anything.’

Raj raised the awkward case, which all three had encountered.

‘What do you do when somebody you want to follow does not offer a feed?’

‘I have four of those and I handle them badly,’ Felix admitted. ‘I check them by hand, monthly, on a recurring reminder, which is exactly the manual habit this whole thing replaced.’

‘Do you resent it?’

‘I resent it enormously and I do it anyway, because the people are worth it.’ He shrugged. ‘Two of the four are the most interesting writers I follow and neither has any idea what a feed is, and I am not going to be the person who writes to a novelist about syndication formats.’

‘I asked one,’ Raj said.

‘And?’

‘And she asked her nephew and it appeared three weeks later, and she has never mentioned it since.’ He grinned. ‘So it is worth asking. The worst outcome is that nothing happens, which is the situation you were already in.’


## Offline, which none of them had thought about

‘One more and then I will let you go,’ David said. ‘Because something happened last week that I did not expect.’

‘Go on.’

‘I was at a retreat centre with no usable connection for two days.’ He said it plainly. ‘Which I had planned around. And on the second morning I opened the reader out of habit, expecting nothing, and there were eleven articles in it that had loaded before I left.’

‘And you could read them.’

‘I read them for an hour and wrote three entries, on a mountain, with no connection at all.’ He shrugged. ‘Which I had not anticipated because I had assumed a reader was a thing that fetched.’

Raj sat up.

‘I have had this and I did not connect it,’ he said. ‘On a shoot, in a valley, four days. Everything that had come in before I left was there.’

‘Why does that matter more than it sounds?’ Felix asked.

‘Because the times when I have hours to read are exactly the times when I am somewhere with no connection,’ Raj said. ‘Travel. Waiting. A location day where the light is wrong and nothing is happening. Those are my reading hours and they are almost always offline hours.’

‘So the alternative would be useless precisely when you need it.’

‘The alternative is useless precisely when I need it, which I had never articulated.’ He was thinking. ‘Every platform I have ever used is unusable in a valley, and a valley is where I have time.’

Felix laughed.

‘Mine is less romantic. I lose the connection at the farmhouse about once a fortnight for reasons nobody has explained to me in eleven years.’

‘And?’

‘And the reader continues to contain everything it contained an hour earlier, which means my morning is unaffected.’ He shrugged. ‘Which sounds small and is the difference between an ordinary morning and one spent being irritated with a company.’


## What they each took away

‘I want to close with the thing I am changing,’ David said, ‘because we have spent two hours agreeing with each other and that is pleasant and not useful.’

‘Go.’

‘I am going to stop keeping articles entirely.’ He was definite. ‘I said earlier that I almost never do it. Almost is doing a lot of work in that sentence. I still save perhaps three a month, and I have looked, and I have opened none of them.’

‘Why do you save them?’

‘Because saving feels like a decision and writing feels like work.’ He said it plainly. ‘And saving is a way of postponing the work while feeling as though I have done something.’

Raj laughed with recognition.

‘Mine is smaller,’ he said. ‘I am going to add a fourth folder for something entirely outside film.’

‘Why?’

‘Because Felix said something an hour ago about reading essayists so his sentences do not calcify, and I have twenty-five feeds and every single one of them is about my own industry.’ He shrugged. ‘Which means everything arriving in my head comes from people who think about the same things I do in the same way, and I have been wondering for a year why my last two ideas felt like other people's.’

‘That is a considerably more serious diagnosis than a folder,’ David said.

‘It is, and a folder is what I can do about it this week.’

Felix went last and took longer.

‘Mine is not about the reader,’ he said. ‘Mine is the telephone call.’

‘To Ostrowski.’

‘To Ostrowski, which I have been not making for four days and which I am going to make tomorrow.’ He rubbed his face. ‘And I want to say what has actually stopped me, because it is not what either of you would guess.’

‘Say it.’

‘I am afraid he will say no and then be embarrassed, and that we will have a slightly awkward friendship for whatever time either of us has left.’ He said it evenly. ‘Which is a small consideration weighed against nine years of writing that four hundred people have read.’

Nobody said anything for a second.

‘Make the call,’ Raj said.

‘I am going to make the call. I am telling you both so that I have to.’

‘That is what a group is for,’ said David.

‘That is what a corridor is for,’ Felix said. ‘Which is where this conversation started and I did not expect it to come back round.’


## The Thursday list, and what came of it

The list arrived on Thursday as promised, and it was longer than Felix had said.

Eleven names, and beside each one a note about how much was there, and beside two of them a short line explaining why that one mattered more.

Raj wrote back within an hour: this is about eight hundred pieces rather than six hundred and I am not complaining, I am reallocating my week.

David wrote: I have taken the four with the most on them, because I read slowly and I would rather do fewer properly.

They worked through it over ten days, mostly in the evenings, and reported progress in the group in the flat way people do when something has become slightly emotional and nobody wants to say so.

What they found was not what any of them expected.

## A quiet technology in a professional toolbox

Three people who would not describe themselves as technical, in professions with nothing in common, describing an enthusiasm for something the industry has repeatedly declared finished. That combination is worth taking seriously precisely because none of them is arguing from nostalgia. Two of them adopted it recently and the third had abandoned it decades ago without noticing.

The argument they eventually assembled has four parts, and each addresses a different failure of the alternatives. Nothing chooses on the reader's behalf, which matters most for exactly the sources least able to compete in a ranked system: careful writing for small audiences, which is what most professional value looks like. It occupies its own space rather than competing with obligation, so reading stops being an interruption performed guiltily between requests. It never initiates, which means it cannot interrupt and, more importantly, cannot punish being ignored for six weeks. And it sits inside the place where thinking is already recorded, which removes the distance between reading something and writing about it.

That last property is the one all three identified as decisive, and it is the least visible from outside. An item that provokes something has a working life of a few minutes. Any intervening step, opening another application, finding the right place, deciding where something goes, is an opportunity for the provocation to evaporate. When reading and writing occupy the same environment, the entry gets made while the thought is still live, in whatever rough form it arrives, which is how four sentences typed in ninety seconds becomes a project with forty entries under it.

What emerged more quietly is that two of the three described the same underlying gain in almost identical terms, and neither had framed it as a technology question at all. One called it a corridor and the other called it having colleagues again. Both were describing the recovery of something structural: a stream of thinking from particular people, arriving regularly, provoking a response. A retired academic and a minister without a department had both lost that, and both got it back through a mechanism that costs nothing and that most people would consider obsolete.

The vulnerability discussed at the end is the counterweight and is genuinely serious. Independent writing exists at the discretion of whoever pays for it, and that arrangement fails routinely: organisations fold, sites are restructured, authors withdraw, and payments lapse after a death. A link records that something existed rather than preserving it, which is why the practice of saving content into a durable local entry at the moment of encounter is worth adopting as a default rather than as a judgment. The judgment about significance requires knowing how something develops, and nobody does at the point of reading.

The offline behaviour, which none of them had considered in advance, turns out to matter for a specific and predictable reason: the hours in which people have time to read are frequently the hours in which they have no connection. Travel, waiting, a location day, a mountain, a rural connection that fails fortnightly. Material already loaded remains readable, and entries written in response are written locally, which means the reading hours are usable rather than merely available.

None of which resurrects anything. The format never died; it moved out of the mainstream and into a professional toolbox, where it is used by people who are not making a point about it and who mostly assume nobody else is interested. The three on that call each thought they were the only one until somebody mentioned it, which is roughly how quiet technologies survive.


## FAQs

### What problem does a feed reader actually solve?

Following a small number of specific sources reliably, without anything deciding what gets shown. That sounds modest and is nearly impossible to arrange otherwise, because every other channel introduces an intermediary with different objectives. Algorithmic timelines optimise for time spent rather than for value, electronic mail places reading in competition with obligation, and notifications decide when somebody reads. A feed reader delivers exactly what was subscribed to, in time order, available whenever the reader chooses to look, with nothing inserted and nothing withheld. The narrowness of that description is the point, since most tools in this area attempt something broader and consequently introduce the intermediary that causes the problem.

### Why do careful writers with small audiences lose on algorithmic platforms?

Because ranking systems optimise for engagement, and a considered essay about a narrow subject is not designed to hold attention against material that is. The loss is invisible, which is the serious part: nobody is informed about what they did not see, so somebody following a dozen writers on such a platform reasonably believes they are following twelve people when they are in fact following a system's selection from twelve people. Feed subscription removes the selection entirely. Practitioners who follow specialists in narrow fields describe this as the decisive argument, since almost everything they value falls into exactly that category. Practitioners in unrelated fields arrive at the same practice independently, which is a reasonable sign that it reflects something general rather than any particular kind of work.

### Why is a separate space better than receiving material by email?

Because an inbox is where obligations arrive. Everything in it is something somebody wants, and reading material placed alongside requests, invoices, and scheduling competes with those and loses, correctly. The practical result is skimming, guilt, and accumulation. A reader occupies its own space, entered deliberately when somebody has decided to read rather than to respond, and nothing inside it is asking for anything, which changes the quality of attention brought to it. The distinction is between a space entered to read and a space entered to respond, and conflating the two reliably damages the first. It is one of the adjustments that costs nothing to make and produces a return every subsequent week, which places it among the easier decisions here.

### What does pull-based consumption actually change?

Who decides when reading happens. A notification chooses the moment, which is never the moment the reader would have chosen, and there is no such thing as a small interruption during concentrated work. More significantly, a pull-based channel does not punish being ignored: somebody who disappears for six weeks returns to find everything present, in order, with nothing indicating they have fallen behind. Practitioners consistently identify the absence of a shaming counter as unexpectedly restful. The property matters most for anybody whose work involves long stretches of unavailability, since those periods stop generating a backlog that has to be confronted. ### How should feed folders be organised?

By whatever question somebody actually brings to the reader, which differs by person. Some organise by relationship, separating people known personally from institutions they are accountable to from the field generally. Others organise by topic, and others by the state they are in when reading, such as material for four spare minutes against material for an unhurried hour. Practitioners frequently end up with a mixture, since following somebody because you know them and following a source to stay informed are genuinely different reasons. Reviewing the folders occasionally is worthwhile, since one of them is usually being maintained out of obligation rather than genuine use. ### How many feeds is too many?

There is no fixed number, only a relationship between volume and reading style. Practitioners with around fifteen feeds tend to read most of what arrives; those with thirty read perhaps one item in eight and treat the rest as passing them by. Both work. What fails is holding a large collection while expecting to read all of it, which produces exactly the accumulation problem the reader was meant to solve. The skill is deciding which mode you are in and choosing volume accordingly. Deciding deliberately which mode applies, rather than drifting into a large collection with an expectation of reading it all, is what keeps the arrangement pleasant.

### How do you avoid feeling behind on a feed?

By treating it as a river rather than a list. A list implies completion and creates an obligation; a river does not, and nobody is behind on a river. Practitioners who read selectively describe making peace with the material that passes them by as the entire skill, and note that the absence of an unread count makes this considerably easier than it would otherwise be. Nothing in the arrangement is designed to increase visit frequency, which removes the pressure at its source. Anybody who finds themselves anxious about unread material should consider reducing the collection rather than increasing their reading, since the volume is the variable they control.

### What is the consumption-to-creation workflow?

Reading something, being provoked by it, and writing an entry about the provocation immediately rather than saving the article. The critical detail is speed: practitioners describe a working life of four to six minutes for a fresh thought, after which it changes shape or disappears. Every intervening step is an opportunity for it to escape, which is why having the reader inside the same environment as the notes matters more than any feature of the reader itself. Practitioners describe capturing in whatever rough form arrives, including fragments and questions, since a polished note written later is a summary of a thought rather than the thought itself.

### Why write about the provocation rather than saving the article?

Because a saved article is somebody else's thinking sitting in a folder, and the reason it was kept becomes unrecoverable within months. Practitioners who saved articles for a period describe accumulating dozens and returning to none of them. Two lines identifying the source followed by a personal response, with a section noting what it connects to, produces something meaningful years later. Practitioners report a substantial share of their own output beginning this way. Doing both is also reasonable, provided the personal response is the part treated as primary and the saved source is treated as supporting evidence. Considerably more than most people assume, since the operation takes seconds per item and the storage cost is negligible relative to any modern machine. Practitioners describe archiving several hundred pieces across a fortnight of evenings as a deliberate project, and archiving anything encountered as an ordinary habit. The constraint is attention rather than capacity, which is why archiving by default rather than by judgment is the workable policy. Practitioners in unrelated fields arrive at the same practice independently, which is a reasonable sign that it reflects something general rather than any particular kind of work. It is a small adjustment that shows up in whether the practice is still in use a year later, which is the only test that matters.

### Is there any value in archiving material collectively?

Substantial, and it is rarely attempted. A project taking one person a fortnight of evenings takes three people four evenings each, and practitioners who have done it describe the shared version as both faster and more pleasant. The participants also read material they would never otherwise have encountered, which occasionally produces genuine discoveries and, in at least one case, prompted somebody to approach an elderly writer about publishing work that had sat unread on a personal site. It is one of the adjustments that costs nothing to make and produces a return every subsequent week, which places it among the easier decisions here. Anybody weighing this against other habits should note that the cost is measured in seconds and the benefit accumulates without any further attention.

### What does this suggest about independent professional writing?

That its obscurity is a distribution problem rather than a quality problem. Practitioners repeatedly describe personal sites, written after retirement or outside institutional constraint, as better than the same authors' formally published work. Those sites have small readerships, no institutional support, and no archive, and depend on somebody continuing to pay a modest annual cost. The material is frequently excellent and structurally precarious, which is an uncomfortable combination worth being aware of. Anybody unsure whether this applies to them can settle it by looking at what they actually opened over the past fortnight. Practitioners describe arriving at this after some months of doing the opposite, which suggests it is worth adopting deliberately rather than waiting to discover it.

### What would you tell somebody setting this up for the first time?

Start with the sites you already check by hand, which is a list you can write in ninety seconds and probably never have. Add perhaps six of them, organise into two or three folders reflecting why you read each one, and leave it alone for a month. Remove anything you have not read. Then write an entry the first time something provokes you, immediately, in whatever rough form arrives, and notice whether that entry would otherwise have existed. The point tends to be obvious in retrospect and difficult to appreciate in advance, which is why most people learn it by having got it wrong first.


### Does this approach work for people who are not writers?

The reading benefit applies to anybody following specific sources, and the creation half applies more widely than expected. Somebody who does not write professionally still has ideas prompted by what they read, and capturing them produces a record of their own thinking that accumulates. Practitioners in trades, operations, and management describe the same loop with different output: a supplier's article prompts a note about a process, and the note becomes a change in how something is done. The distinction worth drawing is between people who produce prose and people who produce decisions, since both benefit from recording what prompted them. Anybody who has acted on something they read and later been unable to recall why has encountered the gap this closes.

### How does this compare with simply visiting sites manually?

Manual checking works and costs a specific amount of time repeatedly, which is why people who do it rarely notice how much. Practitioners describe eleven months of going round a dozen sites weekly, copying anything useful by hand, and feeling diligent about it throughout. The replacement is not faster reading; it is the removal of the checking, which turns out to be most of the effort. Nobody who has made the switch describes returning to the earlier arrangement. Anybody currently doing the manual version should time it honestly for a fortnight, since the total is almost always larger than the estimate and the comparison makes the case better than any argument. Most people are surprised.

### What is the most common reason people abandon a reading habit?

Guilt produced by accumulation. A system presenting unread counts, or a person treating a collection as a queue, generates a debt that grows during any busy period and becomes unpleasant enough to avoid. Once avoidance begins it compounds, since the accumulated volume grows further. Practitioners who maintain the habit for years describe having abandoned the idea of completeness early and deliberately, which removes the mechanism by which the habit fails. Recognising the pattern early is what prevents it, and the remedy is to reduce the collection rather than to resolve to read more. Anybody feeling a debt building should treat that feeling as information about volume rather than about discipline.

### Is there a risk of following too narrow a set of sources?

Yes, and it is the one genuine drawback of choosing sources deliberately. A collection assembled entirely from one field, one perspective, or one professional circle will reflect that back. Practitioners address it by maintaining at least one folder unrelated to their work, and by periodically adding something recommended by somebody whose views differ from theirs. The remedy is deliberate rather than automatic, which is the trade for having removed the automatic selection. Adding something deliberately outside one's own field, and keeping it even when it goes unread for weeks, is the practical guard. Practitioners who maintain such a folder describe it as the one that occasionally produces the most useful ideas.


### Why has this format survived being declared obsolete?

Because the declarations were accurate about the mainstream and wrong about the underlying need. Mass audiences moved to algorithmic timelines, which serve a different purpose adequately. The population that remained consists of people following a small number of specific sources for professional reasons, for whom selection by a third party is the problem rather than a feature. That group was never large enough to sustain a consumer market and never disappeared, which is what a quiet technology looks like from outside. Practitioners describe this as the observation that persuaded them when nothing else had, largely because it is about their own behaviour rather than about the technology itself, and behaviour is the thing anybody can check for themselves within a week.

### What is the single change most worth making after reading this?

Writing an entry the next time something read provokes a thought, immediately, in whatever rough form it arrives, rather than resolving to come back to it. That single action tests the entire argument in about ninety seconds. Practitioners describe the resulting entry as the moment they understood why proximity between reading and writing matters, and the habit generally establishes itself within a fortnight without any further deliberate effort. The experiment costs almost nothing and settles the argument privately, which is generally more convincing than any account somebody else can give of their own experience. Most people know within a fortnight whether it suits them.

### What should somebody expect after six months of this?

A collection that has stabilised through removal rather than addition, a reading habit occupying a fixed and modest part of the day, and a measurable number of entries that would not otherwise exist. Practitioners also report two less predictable outcomes: a restored sense of professional company for anybody working alone, and at least one occasion on which something they archived turned out to be the only remaining copy. Anybody who has reached this point of the article is already the kind of person for whom this works, which is worth saying plainly rather than hedging about it. The population is self-selecting and always has been.


### Is it worth telling other people what you have decided to change?

Practitioners in small groups describe it as the mechanism that makes changes actually happen, particularly for anything uncomfortable. Announcing a specific intention to two people who will remember it converts a private resolution into a commitment, and the effect is disproportionate to how casual the announcement feels. Several of the changes described in conversations of this kind, including one difficult telephone call that had been deferred for days, happened only because somebody said out loud that they were going to do it. The same applies to reading habits specifically, since nothing external enforces them and the only accountability available is whatever somebody arranges for themselves. A group of three is generally sufficient.
