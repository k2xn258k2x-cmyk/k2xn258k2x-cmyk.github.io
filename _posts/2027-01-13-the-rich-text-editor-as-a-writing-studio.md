There is an assumption running through almost every knowledge tool ever built, and it is so widely shared that nobody states it. Notes are where thinking is captured, and documents are made somewhere else. The tool provides a way to record, organise, and retrieve; the actual composition happens in a word processor, and whatever emerges is either pasted back or attached as a file. Two environments, one for thinking and one for producing, with a boundary between them that everybody crosses several times a day.

The boundary is expensive in ways nobody accounts for, because the cost is distributed across thousands of small crossings rather than concentrated anywhere visible. Somebody researching a topic accumulates material in one place and then opens another to write about it. The material is in the first environment and the writing is in the second, which means the writing cannot search the material and the material does not know the writing exists. Every reference requires a switch, and every switch requires reconstructing where one was.

![The Rich Text Editor as a Writing Studio](/assets/img/feature-screenshot-placeholder.svg)

The consequence appears later. A document produced in a word processor and attached to a note is an object in a folder. The reasoning behind it lives in the note, the evidence lives in attachments, and the document itself is opaque to everything around it. Somebody returning eighteen months later has three related things in three states, and the relationships between them existed only in a memory that has since faded.
<!--more-->


What makes the separation feel inevitable is a belief about capability. Serious composition is understood to require a serious environment: headings that structure a long argument, tables that hold data legibly, emphasis that survives a reader's attention, images placed where they belong rather than appended, and a way to mark passages for later reconsideration. Note-taking tools are assumed to offer bold and italic and little else, which makes the assumption self-confirming.

The assumption becomes worth examining once the editor inside a knowledge tool is genuinely capable. Structural headings at several levels. Tables built to a chosen size and adjusted afterward without rebuilding. Blocks that set text apart with a visible marker and a heading of their own. Technical content presented as technical content rather than as prose that happens to contain symbols. Ordered and unordered lists. Images placed inline. Colour applied to text and behind it. Case changed across a selection. Links. And, for people who prefer typing structure to clicking it, plain markup that renders when read.

That collection is not a subset of what a word processor does; it is most of what anybody actually uses. The features a dedicated processor adds beyond it are largely concerned with printing, with collaboration mechanics, and with layout precision for material destined for a press. For a professional producing prose, procedures, reports, or teaching material, those additions are rarely engaged and their absence is rarely noticed.

What changes when composition moves inside is not the writing but everything around it. The document becomes searchable alongside the research that produced it. Revisions are retained without anybody arranging for it. Categories and structure apply to the writing exactly as they apply to everything else. Protection, where it matters, covers the draft as well as the source material. And the gap between the moment somebody produces something and the moment they can find it again reduces to nothing, because the two were never in different places.

What follows is a conversation between two people who arrived at the same conclusion from opposite kinds of writing, one producing flowing prose and one producing structured procedure, and who both noticed the change only in retrospect.


## Two people who stopped exporting

Tomas had proposed it after something Felix said in the group.

‘You mentioned in passing that you had not opened a word processor in eleven months,’ he said, ‘and I realised I could not remember the last time I opened one either, and neither of us has ever mentioned it as a thing that happened.’

‘Because it did not happen,’ Felix said. ‘Nothing happened. I simply stopped, over some months, without deciding to.’

‘Say when you noticed.’

‘When my daughter asked me to send her something and I could not remember how I used to produce documents.’ He laughed. ‘Which is an alarming sentence from a man who wrote for forty years.’

‘Mine was a colleague asking which template I used for lab procedures.’

‘And you had none.’

‘I had none, because I had not made one in over a year, and I had to think for a moment about what she was asking.’ Tomas shrugged. ‘Which is when I realised the switch had happened without any decision.’

‘Then let us compare,’ Felix said, ‘because I suspect our writing has nothing in common and our reasons are identical.’

‘Say your output.’

‘Continuous prose. Chapters of about eight thousand words, in sections, with photographs.’ He counted. ‘Almost no structure beyond headings, and no tables at all, and I have never used a code block in my life.’

‘Mine is the inverse,’ Tomas said. ‘Procedures, tables, safety notices, materials lists, and about a third of what I write is instructions for a machine rather than for a person.’

‘Which is as far apart as two writers can be.’

‘Which is exactly why this is worth an hour.’


## Eight thousand words at a time

‘Show me the shape of a chapter,’ Tomas said.

Felix shared.

‘One entry per chapter, which is the first decision and the one everything else follows from.’ He was deliberate. ‘Inside it, sections. The current draft, the previous approach I abandoned, and the research notes.’

‘Three sections.’

‘Three sections, occasionally four, and the fourth is generally correspondence I am drawing on.’ He scrolled. ‘And each of those has its own editor, so I am not scrolling through a research note to reach my prose.’

‘Say the structure inside the draft.’

‘Second-level headings for the movements of the chapter and third-level for the passages inside them.’ Felix was matter of fact. ‘Which sounds mechanical for a memoir and is how any long piece of prose is actually built, whatever it pretends to be on the page.’

‘Do the headings survive to the finished thing?’

‘Most of them do not and they are not there for the reader.’ He was definite. ‘They are there so that I can see the shape of eight thousand words without reading it, which is the only way I can tell whether a chapter is balanced.’

Tomas asked about the images.

‘You embed photographs.’

‘Inside the prose, at the point they belong, which I have described in this group before and will describe again because it is the single largest change to how I write.’ Felix pulled one up. ‘That is a departmental photograph and beneath it is the paragraph about what happened in that room.’

‘And previously?’

‘Previously the photograph was in a folder and I described the room in prose, badly, from memory, so a reader could follow.’ He shrugged. ‘Half a paragraph of description that the photograph does in a glance, and worse writing for it.’

‘So the image changes the prose.’

‘The image removes an obligation from the prose, which frees it to do the thing prose is for, which is to say what the room meant rather than what it looked like.’

He mentioned two things Tomas said he had not expected.

‘I use two font families and I want to explain why, because it sounds like an affectation.’

‘Go on.’

‘The memoir voice is set in a serif face and my own process notes are set in a sans-serif one.’ Felix was slightly embarrassed. ‘Which is not about appearance. It is that I can tell at a glance whether a passage is the book or is me talking to myself about the book.’

‘Which within a single entry matters.’

‘Which within a single section matters enormously, because I write instructions to myself in the middle of a draft constantly, and I need to be able to see them without reading them.’

‘And the second thing?’

‘Colour, used sparingly, and I want to stress sparingly.’ He counted. ‘Two colours only. Red for a passage I am not certain about, and blue for anything quoted from correspondence.’

‘Why blue for quotations?’

‘Because a memoir that quotes letters has an obligation to be accurate about which words are mine, and I have twice caught myself paraphrasing a letter and presenting it as a quotation.’ Felix was serious. ‘The colour is a check on my own honesty, applied while writing rather than during a proofread that might not happen.’

‘That is a considerably better use of colour than mine.’

‘It came from having got it wrong, which is where most of my practices came from.’


## Tables, warnings, and code for teenagers

‘Now yours,’ Felix said, ‘because I am genuinely curious what a science teacher does with a text editor.’

‘Four things and they are all things you have never used.’ Tomas shared. ‘Tables first, because they are most of my work.’

‘Say the use.’

‘Comparison tables for properties of compounds, and blank data tables for students to fill in during a laboratory.’ He built one as they spoke. ‘Choose the size from the picker, which is faster than any dialogue, and then adjust.’

‘Adjust how?’

‘Right click into the table and add or remove a row or a column.’ Tomas demonstrated. ‘Which is the part that changed my behaviour, because previously an under-sized table meant rebuilding it and I would therefore make every table too large and leave empty rows.’

‘Which looks careless.’

‘Which looks careless to thirty teenagers who are considerably more observant about that than anybody admits, and which is now simply not a problem.’

‘And the second thing?’

‘Safety notices, which are the most important thing I produce and which used to be a paragraph in bold that nobody read.’ He was emphatic. ‘Now they are set apart in a block with a coloured bar down the side and a heading of their own.’

He showed one.

‘A heading saying what the hazard is, and beneath it the specific detail and what to do about it.’ Tomas counted. ‘Which means it is visually distinct from everything around it and cannot be skimmed past, and that is not a preference. That is the requirement.’

‘Do you use the same treatment for anything else?’

‘One other thing, in a different colour, which I label as a key concept.’ He shrugged. ‘Which is the thing that will be on an assessment and which students have learned to look for, and the training took about a term.’

‘So the visual convention teaches them to read differently.’

‘The visual convention is doing a considerable amount of teaching that I am not personally doing, which is the highest praise I can give any formatting.’

‘And the third?’

‘Code, for the robotics students, and this is the one that would mean nothing to you.’ Tomas showed a block. ‘Instructions for a microcontroller, in a block that identifies which language it is, presented as code rather than as prose.’

‘Why does it matter that it looks different?’

‘Because in ordinary text an instruction for a machine is a mess of symbols and spacing that the editor will helpfully ruin.’ He was definite. ‘Quotation marks become curly. Indentation collapses. A student copies it and it does not work and they conclude they are bad at this.’

‘Whereas in a block.’

‘Whereas in a block it is preserved exactly, it is labelled with what it is, and a student copying it gets what I wrote.’ Tomas shrugged. ‘Which sounds small and it removed an entire category of frustration from my robotics club.’

‘And the fourth?’

‘Lists, which are the least interesting and the most used.’ He counted. ‘Numbered for a procedure, because the order is the point and a student skipping step four will produce something alarming. Bulleted for materials, because the order does not matter and numbering it would imply otherwise.’

‘That distinction is doing work.’

‘That distinction is doing work and I did not make it deliberately at first,’ Tomas said. ‘I noticed a student following a bulleted materials list as though it were a sequence and understood that I had been sending a signal I did not intend.’


## Typing structure instead of clicking it

‘There is something you do that I have never touched,’ Felix said.

‘Say which.’

‘You occasionally type the formatting rather than applying it.’

‘Markup, yes, and I use it for perhaps a third of what I write.’ Tomas demonstrated. ‘Certain characters at the start of a line mean a heading. Others mean a list. A pair of marks around a word means emphasis.’

‘And it becomes the formatting.’

‘It renders as the formatting when the entry is read, which means I can type a structured document without my hands leaving the keyboard.’ He shrugged. ‘Which for me is faster, because I type quickly and I resent reaching for a toolbar mid-sentence.’

‘Why have I never encountered this?’

‘Because you write flowing prose and there is almost nothing to mark up.’ Tomas was matter of fact. ‘Eight thousand words with a dozen headings does not benefit. My documents are eighty percent structure and twenty percent sentences.’

‘Which is the whole difference between us in one line.’

‘Which is the difference, and I would not recommend it to you and I would not write a lesson procedure without it.’

Felix asked something practical.

‘Can you mix them?’

‘Within one entry I use one or the other, generally, and across entries I use both freely.’ He counted. ‘Anything I am typing quickly and structurally goes in as markup. Anything I am composing carefully, with images and colour, I do visually.’

‘So the choice follows the kind of writing.’

‘The choice follows whether I am producing structure or producing sentences, which turns out to be a real distinction and not one I would have predicted.’

He mentioned the practical consequence.

‘There is a thing worth knowing about how they are held,’ Tomas said. ‘Which somebody explained to me and which I have found useful.’

‘Go on.’

‘Something composed visually is held as formatted content. Something typed as markup is held as plain text that renders when read.’ He was careful. ‘Which are two different things on disk and which behave identically to me.’

‘Does the difference matter?’

‘It matters exactly once, which is when you look at the underlying file, and then it is obvious rather than confusing.’ Tomas shrugged. ‘One reads as marked-up content and the other reads as ordinary text with a few symbols, and both are perfectly legible.’

‘Which is the thing that reassured me most about the whole arrangement,’ Felix said. ‘My chapters are readable without any of this.’


## Case, and a review layer made of colour

‘I want to show you two small things that changed my proofreading,’ Tomas said.

‘Show me.’

‘Case transformation. Select something and change it.’ He demonstrated on a compound name. ‘Which I use for emphasis in materials lists, because a substance name in capitals is unmistakable at a bench.’

‘Which is a safety thing rather than a style thing.’

‘Everything I do with formatting is a safety thing or a comprehension thing, which is what happens when your readers are seventeen.’ Tomas laughed. ‘But the use I would recommend to you is different.’

‘Say it.’

‘Sentence case, on pasted material.’ He counted. ‘When I paste something from another source it arrives with whatever capitalisation that source used, frequently a heading in full capitals, and normalising it is one action rather than retyping.’

Felix made a noise.

‘I have retyped headings.’

‘Everybody has retyped headings and it took me two years to stop.’

‘I use title case,’ Felix said. ‘For chapter headings, at the end rather than at the start.’

‘Say why at the end.’

‘Because I write a heading as a working label, in whatever form arrives, and it changes four times before the chapter settles.’ He shrugged. ‘Capitalising it early is decoration on something provisional. When the chapter is finished, one action makes every heading consistent.’

‘That is a better workflow than mine and I am adopting it.’

Tomas moved to the second.

‘Highlighting, which I use as a system rather than for emphasis.’

‘Say the system.’

‘Two colours and they mean specific things.’ He counted. ‘Yellow behind anything safety-critical, green behind anything that will appear on an assessment.’

‘Which the students know.’

‘Which the students know because I told them once in September and they have applied it since.’ Tomas was pleased. ‘And what it produces is that a student revising can scan a six-page procedure for green and find what they need in about eleven seconds.’

‘Whereas reading it.’

‘Whereas reading a procedure for revision is what students do not do, and I would rather build a document they will actually use.’

Felix described his own, which he said was entirely private.

‘Mine is a review layer and nobody sees it,’ he said.

‘Say the colours.’

‘Pink for anything I want to reconsider. Orange for anything my reader has flagged.’ He was matter of fact. ‘A friend reads chapters and sends me notes, and I mark the passages she mentions rather than keeping a separate list.’

‘So the notes live in the text.’

‘The notes live at the exact words they concern, which is the entire benefit, because a list of comments requires me to find each passage and marking finds them for me.’ Felix shrugged. ‘And when I have dealt with one I remove the colour, so the orange disappearing is the progress indicator.’

‘That is a working method rather than formatting.’

‘That is what all of this turns out to be, and I would not have said that a year ago,’ he said. ‘I thought of formatting as how a document looks. It is mostly how a document is used.’


## Pointing at things

‘Links,’ Tomas said. ‘Which we both use and probably differently.’

‘Mine are almost all external and almost all archival,’ Felix said. ‘A university page, an obituary, an article I am drawing on.’

‘Why link rather than describe?’

‘Because a memoir that makes a factual claim should say where the claim came from, and I do not want footnotes in a draft.’ He was definite. ‘A link at the point of the claim is a footnote that does not interrupt anything.’

‘Do you worry about them lasting?’

‘Constantly, and it is the reason I also save the content itself into an entry when the source matters.’ Felix shrugged. ‘One of the sites I relied on went down last year and it was the one I would have called safest, which was instructive.’

‘So the link is a pointer and the saved copy is the record.’

‘The link is a courtesy to a future reader and the saved copy is what I actually rely on, and I would recommend that division to anybody writing anything with sources in it.’

Tomas said his were mostly for other people.

‘Mine point students at things,’ he said. ‘A simulation, a demonstration, a video that explains something better than I do.’

‘Which you would previously have put where?’

‘In a separate list at the end, which nobody read, or read out loud in class, which nobody wrote down.’ He was rueful. ‘Placed at the point in the procedure where a student is confused, it gets used, and the difference in usage is not marginal.’

‘Position again.’

‘Position again, which is the theme of everything we have discussed.’ Tomas paused. ‘Whether it is an image, a warning, a highlight, or a link, the value comes from it being where it is needed rather than collected somewhere.’

He mentioned a second use.

‘I also link between my own entries,’ he said. ‘Which I do less than I should.’

‘Say the case.’

‘A lesson that depends on something taught six weeks earlier.’ Tomas counted. ‘Rather than re-explaining, I point at the earlier entry, which means the explanation exists once and is maintained once.’

‘Which is a reason to link rather than to duplicate.’

‘Which is the only reason to link internally, and I have about forty of them and would have several hundred if I were more diligent.’

Felix said something he had been thinking.

‘I have almost none of those and I should have many,’ he said.

‘Say why you do not.’

‘Because search finds things and I have relied on it entirely.’ He was frank. ‘Which works for me because I know my own material. It would not work for anybody reading after me, and this is a book that is partly for my daughter.’

‘So the links are for her.’

‘The links are for a reader who does not know what to search for, which is every reader other than me, and I have been writing as though I were the only one.’


## The thing Felix uses that Tomas has never opened

‘There is a piece of this I rely on constantly and you have not mentioned once,’ Felix said.

‘Say which.’

‘The record of previous versions.’

Tomas paused.

‘I have never opened it,’ he admitted.

‘Which tells me something about the difference between our writing and I want to work out what.’ Felix was thinking. ‘I open it perhaps three times a week.’

‘Say what for.’

‘Because I rewrite constantly and I frequently rewrite something into a worse state.’ He was frank. ‘A paragraph I have improved four times and ruined on the fifth is a thing that happens to every writer and used to cost me an afternoon of trying to remember what it said.’

‘And now?’

‘Now I look at what the chapter said last week, read the paragraph, and either restore it or discover that I was right to change it.’ Felix shrugged. ‘Which takes ninety seconds and which removes a specific kind of despair.’

‘Do you restore often?’

‘Perhaps one time in four, and the other three are more valuable.’ He was definite. ‘Because seeing that the earlier version was worse settles a doubt that would otherwise have sat there for a week.’

Tomas was working out why he did not need it.

‘My documents do not evolve,’ he said. ‘They get written, used once, and then improved slightly the following year.’

‘Which is a completely different relationship to a text.’

‘Which is annual rather than continuous, and by the time I revise something a year has passed and the old version is not a thing I want back.’ He shrugged. ‘It is a thing I have deliberately moved on from.’

‘Then you have no use for it and that is fine.’

‘Except in one case, and you have just made me think of it.’ Tomas sat forward. ‘A safety procedure.’

‘Say why.’

‘Because if something went wrong in a laboratory, the question would be what the procedure said on that day.’ He was suddenly serious. ‘Not what it says now. What the students were given.’

‘And you would have to reconstruct it.’

‘I would have to remember, which is not an answer, and there is apparently a record of exactly that sitting there which I have never once looked at.’ Tomas laughed without much humour. ‘That is a considerably more important discovery than anything I have shown you today.’

‘Do you want to stop and go and look?’

‘I want to finish this call and then go and look, and I suspect I am going to find every version of every procedure I have written for a year and a half.’


## When did the word processor stop

‘I want to work out when this actually happened,’ Felix said, ‘because neither of us decided it.’

‘Say your sequence.’

‘For about a year I drafted in a word processor and pasted the finished chapter in for storage.’ He was precise. ‘Which is the arrangement everybody has, and I would have defended it if asked.’

‘What broke it?’

‘I needed a passage from a chapter I had abandoned eight months earlier and I could not find it.’ Felix shrugged. ‘The abandoned draft was in a folder somewhere, under a name I had chosen at the time, and the search that would have found it in four seconds could not reach it because it was not in here.’

‘So the storage was not storage.’

‘The storage was a graveyard, and the moment I noticed that, the arrangement made no sense.’ He counted. ‘I was doing the work in a place with no search, no revision record, and no connection to my research, and then moving the result to a place with all three.’

‘Which is backward.’

‘Which is exactly backward and it had never occurred to me, because it is what everybody does.’

‘Say what you gained.’

‘Four things and I would rank them.’ Felix counted them off. ‘Revisions retained without my arranging anything, which for somebody who rewrites constantly is the largest. Search reaching my drafts as well as my research. Photographs available where I am writing rather than in a folder. And no step at the end.’

‘The no step is underrated.’

‘The no step is enormous and it is invisible.’ He was emphatic. ‘Every arrangement with two environments has a moment where something must be moved, and that moment is where things are forgotten. Removing it removes a category of loss.’

Tomas said his was more mundane and more sudden.

‘Mine was a Sunday evening in the second week of a term,’ he said.

‘Say what happened.’

‘I had written a lesson in a document, and I wanted to attach the safety sheet, and the safety sheet was in here.’ He shrugged. ‘So I was going to download it from here to attach it there, and I stopped with my hand on the mouse.’

‘Because it was absurd.’

‘Because I was about to take a file out of the place where it lives in order to put it next to a document that could have been written in the same place.’ Tomas laughed. ‘And I wrote the lesson here instead, that evening, and I have not opened a document editor since except to read something somebody sent me.’

‘Do you still produce handouts?’

‘Constantly, and I write them here and copy the formatted content out when I need to distribute it.’ He was matter of fact. ‘Which takes four seconds and which means the authoritative version is the one I can search, label, and revise.’

‘Rather than the distributed one.’

‘Rather than a copy in a shared folder that somebody edits and that then disagrees with mine.’ Tomas was definite. ‘Which happened twice and which is the reason I am careful about which version is the real one.’

Felix asked the question he said mattered.

‘Is there anything you genuinely miss?’

‘Two things and neither has cost me anything yet.’ Tomas thought. ‘Somebody else editing the same thing at the same time, which I never needed and which I would arrange differently if I did. And precise layout for something going to a printer, which for a classroom handout is not a consideration.’

‘Mine is a shorter list.’

‘Say it.’

‘Nothing.’ Felix said it flatly. ‘I write continuous prose with headings and pictures. Everything a word processor adds beyond that is concerned with printing a book, and when this book is printed it will be printed by somebody whose job that is, from a manuscript I hand over.’

‘Which is the honest position for most writers.’

‘Which is the honest position for most people producing most documents, and the industry has spent thirty years persuading everybody otherwise.’


## What Tomas noticed about his students

‘There is a consequence I did not anticipate and I want to describe it,’ Tomas said.

‘Go on.’

‘My documents got better, measurably, and not because I got better at writing them.’

‘Say what you mean by measurably.’

‘Fewer questions during a laboratory.’ He counted. ‘Which is the only metric I have and which is a good one, because a question during a practical is a student who could not follow the procedure.’

‘And the questions dropped.’

‘Noticeably, over about a term, and I spent some time working out why.’ Tomas was thoughtful. ‘It is not that the words are better. It is that a procedure with a numbered sequence, a set-apart warning at the step where the hazard occurs, and a table with the columns already labelled is a different object from four paragraphs of prose.’

‘Which you would previously have written.’

‘Which I previously wrote, because a document editor makes prose easy and everything else moderately awkward.’ He shrugged. ‘Formatting that is easy gets used, and what gets used shapes what you produce, and what you produce shapes whether anybody can follow it.’

‘So the tool changed the document.’

‘The tool changed what I found it easy to produce, and I produced that, and it happened to be better.’ Tomas was slightly rueful. ‘Which is not a story about my skill. It is a story about friction.’

Felix said his version was the same mechanism with a different outcome.

‘Mine is that I write more images and fewer words,’ he said.

‘Which sounds like less writing.’

‘It is considerably less writing and considerably better writing, because the words I removed were the ones doing a job the photograph does better.’ He was definite. ‘A chapter of mine is perhaps six hundred words shorter than it would have been and says more.’

‘Because the descriptive obligation is gone.’

‘Because I am no longer describing rooms, faces, and buildings for a reader who cannot see them, and I did not realise how much of my prose was doing that until it stopped.’

‘That is a substantial claim about a formatting feature.’

‘It is a claim about where the picture sits rather than about pictures,’ Felix said. ‘I had photographs before. They were in a folder. Nobody looks in a folder while reading a paragraph.’


## What each of them got wrong first

‘Before we finish I want to hear the mistakes,’ Tomas said. ‘Because everything so far has been things working and somebody starting will get things wrong.’

‘Mine was too much formatting,’ Felix said immediately.

‘Say the shape of it.’

‘Six colours, four kinds of emphasis, and highlighting I could not remember the meaning of.’ He was rueful. ‘Within about two months a chapter looked like a road map and I could not read my own text.’

‘What did you do?’

‘Removed almost all of it in one afternoon, and then reintroduced two colours with defined meanings and stopped.’ Felix counted. ‘And the rule I ended with is that a formatting convention is worth having only if I can state what it means in a sentence.’

‘Which excludes most of what you had.’

‘Which excluded eleven of the thirteen things I was doing, and the two survivors are the ones I described earlier.’

Tomas said his was the opposite.

‘Mine was not enough, for about a year, and it was a belief rather than an oversight.’

‘Say the belief.’

‘That formatting was decoration and that a serious document was plain.’ He shrugged. ‘Which is a thing I absorbed somewhere and never examined, and it produced procedures that were four paragraphs of prose containing a safety warning in the third sentence.’

‘Which nobody saw.’

‘Which nobody saw, and one of them nearly mattered, and that is the reason I now put a hazard in a block with a bar down the side.’ Tomas was serious. ‘I did not change my practice because somebody showed me a feature. I changed it because a student did something I had warned about in a sentence they had read past.’

‘That is the more instructive mistake.’

‘It is the one I would tell somebody about, and the general version is that formatting is not decoration.’ He counted. ‘It is instruction about how to read. A plain document instructs somebody to read it evenly, and almost no document should be read evenly.’

Felix was quiet for a moment.

‘That is better than my rule,’ he said.

‘Say yours again and I will tell you whether it is.’

‘A convention is worth having if I can state what it means in a sentence.’ He nodded slowly. ‘Which is a test for whether a convention is any good, and yours is the reason to have one at all. They are not in competition.’


## Writing where you think

The pattern in that conversation is worth extracting because it recurs in almost every profession that produces documents, and because both participants describe a change they did not decide on and only identified afterward.

The arrangement everybody starts with has two environments: a knowledge tool for capture and retrieval, and a word processor for composition. That division feels natural and is backward. The work happens in the environment without search, without a revision record, and without any connection to the research supporting it, and the result is then moved to the environment that has all three. One participant identified the moment it stopped making sense: he needed a passage from an abandoned draft, could not find it, and realised the storage he had been so diligent about was a graveyard.

The features that make composition viable inside a knowledge tool are not exotic. Structural headings at several levels, which one writer uses to see the shape of eight thousand words without reading them. Tables built to a chosen size and adjusted afterward, which removes the habit of making every table too large in case. Blocks set apart with a coloured bar and a heading of their own, used for safety notices at the step where a hazard occurs. Technical content preserved exactly rather than mangled by helpful punctuation substitution. Ordered lists where sequence matters and unordered where it does not, which is a signal readers act on whether or not the writer intended it.

Two smaller capabilities turned out to carry disproportionate weight. Colour, used as a system rather than for decoration: one writer marks uncertain passages and quoted correspondence in two specific colours, the second being a check on his own accuracy applied while writing rather than during a proofread that might not happen. And highlighting used as a review layer, with a reader's flagged passages marked at the exact words concerned rather than kept as a separate list, and the colour removed as each is dealt with so that its disappearance becomes the progress indicator.

The markup-versus-visual question resolved cleanly along a line neither had anticipated. Somebody producing sentences with occasional structure composes visually. Somebody producing structure with occasional sentences types it, because their hands never leave the keyboard and the toolbar is an interruption. Both described the other's preference as wrong for their own work and obviously right for the other's.

Position emerged as the theme underneath everything. An image inside the paragraph it concerns, a warning at the step where the hazard occurs, a link where the reader is confused, and a highlight on the words themselves. In each case the capability existed before and was collected somewhere else, and in each case moving it to the point of relevance is what produced the benefit. One participant made the strongest version of the claim: his chapters are several hundred words shorter and say more, because he is no longer describing rooms for a reader who cannot see them.

The most interesting consequence was one neither expected. Both describe their documents improving without their writing improving. The teacher reports fewer questions during practical work, attributes it to procedures that are structured rather than prose, and is candid that this is a story about friction rather than skill: formatting that is easy gets used, what gets used shapes what somebody produces, and what somebody produces determines whether anybody can follow it.

What remains, at the end, is that the gap between creating something and finding it again has closed to nothing. Every document produced inside the knowledge base inherits the search, the structure, the revision record, and the protection that surround it, without anybody arranging any of that. Neither participant chose this. Both simply stopped opening the other environment, months apart, and noticed only when somebody asked them a question that assumed they still did.


## FAQs

### Why do most people write in a separate application from where they keep their notes?

Because of an assumption so widely shared that nobody states it: notes are for capture and documents are made elsewhere. The division feels natural and is rarely examined. Practitioners who cross it many times a day describe the cost as invisible precisely because it is distributed, since each individual switch is trivial and only the aggregate matters. The realisation usually arrives when somebody cannot find something they wrote. Anybody who crosses that boundary daily should count the crossings for a week, since the number is usually considerably higher than the estimate. Practitioners in unrelated kinds of writing arrive at the same conclusion independently, which suggests it follows from how documents are used rather than from any particular field.

### What is actually wrong with the two-environment arrangement?

The work happens in the environment with the fewest advantages. Composition occurs somewhere with no search reaching it, no revision record, and no connection to the supporting material, and the result is then moved to the place that has all three. One practitioner describes needing a passage from an abandoned draft, being unable to find it, and recognising that the storage he had been diligent about was functioning as a graveyard rather than as an archive. The diagnosis is uncomfortable and simple: the work is happening in the environment with the fewest advantages and the result is being filed in the one with the most.

### What does an editor need to handle serious composition?

Less than people assume and more than most note tools provide. Structural headings at several levels, tables that can be created and adjusted, blocks that set text apart with a visible marker, technical content preserved exactly, ordered and unordered lists, images placed inline, colour applied to and behind text, case changed across a selection, and links. Practitioners describe that set as covering essentially everything they actually used in a word processor. Listing what one actually uses in a typical document is a short exercise and usually settles the question in a minute. It is one of the choices that costs nothing at the outset and is tedious to apply retrospectively across a body of work.

### What does a word processor add that is genuinely missing?

Simultaneous editing by several people and precise layout for material going to a printing press. Practitioners producing prose, procedures, reports, and teaching material describe neither as relevant to their work, and one notes that a manuscript destined for publication is typeset by somebody whose job that is, from whatever the author hands over. The features exist and are largely engaged by a minority of documents. Being specific about which of those two applies to one's own work is what turns a habit into a decision. Anybody uncertain can test it on a single document rather than reasoning about it in the abstract. Practitioners who have worked this way for a year describe the arrangement as having become invisible, which is the usual sign that a change was the right one.

### How are headings used in long-form prose?

As a structural view rather than as something the reader will see. One practitioner writing eight-thousand-word chapters uses second-level headings for the movements of a chapter and third-level for passages within them, and describes most of them as not surviving into the finished piece. Their purpose is to make the shape of a long document visible without reading it, which is the only way he can judge whether a chapter is balanced. The technique transfers to any long document, including ones whose readers will never see a heading at all. The benefit is only visible in retrospect, which is why the change tends to happen gradually rather than by decision.

### Why do inline images change how somebody writes?

Because they remove an obligation from the prose. Without the image present, a writer describes a room, a face, or an object so the reader can follow, which consumes a substantial proportion of every paragraph and is generally done worse than the photograph would do it. With the image present, the prose is freed to say what the thing meant. One practitioner reports his chapters being several hundred words shorter and better as a direct result. The effect is only visible in retrospect, since nobody notices the descriptive obligation while they are fulfilling it. Practitioners in unrelated kinds of writing arrive at the same conclusion independently, which suggests it follows from how documents are used rather than from any particular field.

### Is there a use for multiple font families within one document?

One practitioner uses two deliberately and describes it as functional rather than aesthetic. Prose intended for the finished work is set in one face and process notes to himself are set in another, which means he can tell at a glance whether a passage is the book or is commentary on the book. Since he writes instructions to himself in the middle of drafts constantly, being able to see them without reading them is genuinely useful. The application is narrow and worth knowing about for anybody who writes commentary to themselves inside a working draft. It is one of the choices that costs nothing at the outset and is tedious to apply retrospectively across a body of work.

### How should text colour be used?

Sparingly and systematically, according to practitioners who have settled into a practice. One uses exactly two colours: red for passages he is uncertain about and blue for material quoted from correspondence. The second is described as a check on his own accuracy, since he twice caught himself paraphrasing a letter and presenting it as a quotation, and the colour applies the check while writing rather than during a proofread that may not happen. The general rule practitioners arrive at is that a colour is worth having only if its meaning can be stated in a sentence. ### What are tables actually used for?

Comparison and data capture, in roughly equal measure for practitioners in technical teaching. Comparison tables set properties side by side; blank data tables give students a structure to fill in during practical work. The size picker allows a table to be created quickly, and the ability to add or remove rows and columns afterward is what practitioners identify as behaviour-changing, since previously an undersized table meant rebuilding and everybody therefore built them too large. Being able to adjust afterward removes a small habit that produces visibly careless documents, which readers notice more than writers expect. The benefit is only visible in retrospect, which is why the change tends to happen gradually rather than by decision.

### What are callout blocks for?

Setting something apart so it cannot be skimmed past. Practitioners in teaching use them for safety notices, with a coloured bar, a heading naming the hazard, and the specific detail beneath. A second colour marks key concepts that will appear in assessment. One describes students learning the convention within a term and using it to navigate, which means the formatting is doing teaching work that the teacher is not personally doing. Practitioners describe the convention taking about a term to establish with a regular audience and working reliably thereafter. Practitioners in unrelated kinds of writing arrive at the same conclusion independently, which suggests it follows from how documents are used rather than from any particular field.

### Why do code blocks matter outside programming?

Because ordinary text editing damages anything that must be reproduced exactly. Quotation marks become curly, indentation collapses, and a student copying an instruction gets something that does not work and concludes they are incapable. A block preserves the content precisely and labels what it is. Practitioners teaching robotics describe this removing an entire category of frustration that they had previously attributed to student error. Anybody distributing anything that must be copied exactly should check what happens to it in ordinary text, since the damage is silent. It is one of the choices that costs nothing at the outset and is tedious to apply retrospectively across a body of work.

### Does the distinction between ordered and unordered lists matter?

More than writers intend, since readers act on it. One practitioner describes noticing a student following a bulleted materials list as though it were a sequence, and recognising that he had been sending a signal he did not mean. Numbered lists say the order matters, which is correct for a procedure where skipping a step is dangerous. Bulleted lists say it does not, which is correct for a checklist. Choosing deliberately takes no additional effort and prevents a signal nobody intended to send. Anybody uncertain can test it on a single document rather than reasoning about it in the abstract. The general principle running through all of it is that formatting is instruction about how a document should be read, and every specific decision follows from asking what instruction is intended.

### When is typing markup better than using a toolbar?

When somebody is producing structure rather than sentences. The choice follows the kind of writing rather than preference. Trying both on the same document for a week settles the question faster than reasoning about it. The benefit is only visible in retrospect, which is why the change tends to happen gradually rather than by decision. ### Can both approaches be used?

Yes, and practitioners describe choosing per entry rather than mixing within one. ### What is case transformation actually useful for?

Two things, according to practitioners who use it regularly. Normalising material pasted from other sources, which arrives with whatever capitalisation the source used and would otherwise be retyped. And applying consistent capitalisation to headings at the end of a piece rather than at the start, since a working heading changes several times and capitalising it early is decoration on something provisional. One practitioner describes having retyped headings for two years before discovering this. The heading application in particular saves a surprising amount of retyping for anybody whose working titles evolve. It is one of the choices that costs nothing at the outset and is tedious to apply retrospectively across a body of work.

### How can highlighting be used as more than emphasis?

As a system with defined meanings. One practitioner uses yellow for safety-critical content and green for anything appearing in assessment, tells students the convention once, and reports them scanning a six-page procedure for green and finding what they need in seconds. Another uses it as a private review layer, marking passages a reader has flagged and removing the colour as each is dealt with, so its disappearance becomes the progress indicator. Both uses depend on the meanings being fixed and stated, since a colour whose meaning drifts becomes noise within weeks. ### Why mark a reader's comments in the text rather than keeping a list?

Because the marks are at the exact words concerned. A separate list of comments requires the writer to locate each passage, which is the tedious part and where the process breaks down. Marking places the comment where it applies, which means the text itself carries the review. Practitioners describe this as considerably more likely to result in the comments actually being addressed. Anybody who receives feedback on written work regularly should try this once, since the improvement in follow-through is immediate. The benefit is only visible in retrospect, which is why the change tends to happen gradually rather than by decision. ### How should links to external sources be handled?

As a courtesy rather than as the record, according to practitioners who write with sources. A link at the point of a factual claim functions as a footnote that interrupts nothing, and is useful to any future reader. Because sources disappear, practitioners also save the content itself into an entry where the source genuinely matters. One describes a site going down that he would have called the safest of the ones he relied on. The division between a pointer and a record is worth adopting before a source disappears rather than afterward. ### What are links used for in teaching material?

Pointing students at resources at the moment they need them. Practitioners describe previously collecting such links at the end of a document, which nobody read, or mentioning them aloud, which nobody recorded. Placed at the step where a student is likely to be confused, they get used, and the difference in usage is described as substantial rather than marginal. ### Should entries link to each other?

Where the alternative is duplication. One practitioner links a lesson to the earlier lesson it depends upon rather than re-explaining, so the explanation exists once and is maintained once. Another admits to having almost none, having relied entirely on search, and recognises the gap: search works for somebody who knows their own material and fails for any other reader, which for a document intended to be read by family is a real omission. The test is whether the alternative is duplication, since linking for its own sake produces a web nobody maintains. Anybody uncertain can test it on a single document rather than reasoning about it in the abstract.

### What is the underlying theme across all of these features?

### What happens to distribution when writing moves inside?

Asking who the reader is, before deciding how much structure to apply, resolves most formatting questions before they arise.

### Does any of this apply to short documents?

The same principles apply and the stakes are lower. A short procedure still benefits from a numbered sequence and a warning set apart. A short piece of prose still benefits from an image placed where it belongs. What changes is that the surrounding advantages, meaning search, revision history, and categorisation, matter proportionally more for long-lived documents than for something written once and discarded. The convention only works if its meaning is fixed, since a colour or a block whose significance drifts becomes noise within a few weeks and then gets ignored entirely. Anybody expecting a dramatic difference in the writing itself will be disappointed, which practitioners describe as the correct expectation to set before starting.

### What about documents that are mostly data?

Tables carry them, and practitioners describe the ability to adjust a table afterward as what makes this practical. A document consisting largely of comparison or capture tables is straightforward to produce, and the surrounding text serves to explain rather than to contain. For anything genuinely computational, practitioners keep the calculation in a spreadsheet and the interpretation in the document, which is the same division they would make anywhere. Practitioners describe applying the same habits regardless of length, on the grounds that a practice with exceptions is a practice that erodes. The exercise takes two minutes and either confirms that the record is irrelevant to somebody's work or reveals something they did not know they had.

### Is there a difference between writing for oneself and writing for others?

A considerable one, and it determines how much structure is worth applying. Practitioners writing for their own future reference use minimal formatting and rely on search. Practitioners writing for students, clients, or colleagues invest heavily in structure, because a reader who does not know the material cannot navigate prose. One participant realised mid-conversation that he had been writing a book for his daughter as though he were the only reader. Keeping the calculation and the interpretation in their proper places is a division worth stating explicitly, since documents drift toward containing both otherwise. Practitioners describe this consistently across very different kinds of writing, which is a reasonable indication that it follows from the arrangement rather than from any individual practice.

### What is the most transferable single practice here?

Establishing a small number of formatting conventions with meanings that can be stated in a sentence, and applying them consistently. Practitioners who accumulated many conventions describe their documents becoming unreadable within months. Practitioners with two or three describe them working indefinitely, being learned by regular readers, and doing navigational work that the writer would otherwise have to do explicitly. Asking who the reader is, before deciding how much structure to apply, resolves most formatting questions before they arise. Setting the expectation correctly is worth doing, since somebody anticipating a transformed writing experience will conclude the change failed when in fact it succeeded exactly as it should.

### What would somebody notice first after making the switch?

That nothing about the writing itself feels different, and that everything around it does. Practitioners describe the composition experience as unremarkable, which is the correct outcome, and describe the difference as arriving later: when they search for something they wrote, when they want an earlier version, or when they need a piece of research while mid-sentence. The change is in the surroundings rather than in the act. Anybody expecting a dramatic difference in the writing itself will be disappointed, which practitioners describe as the correct expectation to set before starting. The effect compounds, since a body of process notes becomes searchable material that informs later work rather than a set of thoughts that were never recorded.

### Is there a final piece of advice?

Check the revision record once, even if you believe you have no use for it. One practitioner in this discussion had never opened it, was certain it was irrelevant to his kind of writing, and realised mid-conversation that it constituted a dated record of every safety procedure he had issued, which is exactly what would be needed if anything ever went wrong. He described that as the most important thing he learned that afternoon. The exercise takes two minutes and either confirms that the record is irrelevant to somebody's work or reveals something they did not know they had. Anybody with a folder of abandoned drafts under names chosen at the time will recognise the problem this solves, and the solution requires no effort beyond writing in the right place.

### Does this change how much somebody writes?

Practitioners report writing more, and attribute it to the absence of a threshold. Opening a separate application to begin something is a small act with a psychological weight that a blank entry does not carry, which means marginal ideas get written down rather than deferred. One practitioner describes producing considerably more process notes than previously, on the grounds that a note about a chapter now costs nothing to start. Practitioners describe this continuity as the single property they would find hardest to give up, ahead of any individual formatting capability. Practitioners describe the accumulated effect over a year as considerably larger than any single element of it, which is characteristic of changes driven by removed friction rather than by added capability.

### What happens to material that never becomes a finished document?

It remains where it is, searchable and categorised, rather than sitting as an abandoned file with a name chosen at the time. Practitioners describe this as one of the larger practical gains, since a substantial proportion of anybody's writing does not reach completion and is nonetheless worth retaining. The abandoned material is frequently the source of a passage that belongs somewhere else entirely. The dissolution of the boundary is what people describe last and value most, which suggests it is the actual substance of the change rather than any of the features that enable it. None of it requires any adjustment to how somebody writes, only to where, which is why the transition tends to happen without anybody deciding on it.

### How does this affect the boundary between notes and documents?

It dissolves it, which practitioners describe as the honest summary. A note that grows into a chapter was never a different kind of object; it simply accumulated. Practitioners describe entries that began as a paragraph of thinking and became substantial documents without any moment of conversion, and describe that continuity as the property they would most struggle to give up if they returned to a two-environment arrangement. Practitioners in unrelated fields describe the same progression from note to document without any moment of conversion, which is worth expecting rather than being surprised by. Anybody weighing this should consider what proportion of their own writing currently lives somewhere their search cannot reach, since that figure is generally the deciding one.


### What is the honest summary for somebody deciding?

That the editor is capable enough for most professional documents, and that the reason to move is what surrounds it rather than the editor itself. Anybody whose work genuinely requires simultaneous multi-author editing or precise print layout has a real reason to keep a dedicated application. Everybody else should ask what proportion of their own writing currently sits somewhere their search cannot reach, and decide from that number. Both practitioners in this discussion answered that question implicitly rather than deliberately, by noticing over some months that they had stopped opening anything else. Neither describes the move as a decision, and both describe it as obvious in retrospect, which is the usual shape of a change driven by friction rather than by argument.


### What does the arrangement look like after a year?

Unremarkable, which practitioners describe as the objective. The editor becomes invisible, the conventions become automatic, and the surrounding advantages become assumptions rather than discoveries. What remains noticeable is the absence of things: no application switching, no exporting, no separate folder of documents, and no moment at the end where something must be moved from where it was made to where it will be kept. Practitioners asked to describe the experience struggle to say anything about the writing itself, which is the sign that the environment has receded properly. What they describe instead are occasions where something was easy that would previously have been awkward: a passage recovered, a photograph found, a draft located by searching a phrase they half remembered writing.
