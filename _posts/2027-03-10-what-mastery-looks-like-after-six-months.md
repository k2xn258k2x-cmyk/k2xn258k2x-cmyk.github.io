There is a common assumption about what expertise with a tool consists of, and it is that an expert knows more of what the tool can do. Somebody who has used something for two years is presumed to have accumulated a larger inventory of capabilities than somebody who has used it for two weeks, and to deploy that inventory more often. The assumption is intuitive and, in almost every case that matters, wrong.

What actually happens over sustained use is that a person's attention migrates away from the tool entirely. In the first weeks they are conscious of it constantly: deciding where things go, wondering whether an action worked, considering which capability suits a situation. Those decisions consume a portion of whatever they are trying to think about. After a sufficient period the decisions stop being made, because they have been settled, and the attention they occupied becomes available for the work.

![What Mastery Looks Like After Six Months](/assets/img/feature-screenshot-placeholder.svg)

That transition is worth naming precisely because it is invisible while it happens and unmistakable in retrospect. Nobody experiences the moment when they stop thinking about a mechanism. What they experience, some months later, is being unable to describe their own routine without being asked, or noticing that a colleague's question about how they do something is difficult to answer because they have not consciously done it in a year.
<!--more-->


There is a second consequence that is more interesting and considerably less discussed, which is that sustained use changes how somebody thinks about their own work rather than only how they record it. A person who has spent a year describing their material consistently begins categorising things before they write them down. A person who has compared several items side by side for months starts framing questions comparatively. The habits established for organisational reasons migrate into the reasoning itself.

That migration is not always noticed by the person it happens to, which makes it a difficult thing to ask about directly. Somebody who plans a project by thinking about structure first does not experience themselves as having acquired a habit from a tool. They experience themselves as having become better at planning projects, which may be an accurate description of the same event.

There is also a category of change that has nothing to do with efficiency at all. Removing a background anxiety, whether about forgetting something, losing something, or being observed, does not make somebody faster. It makes them differently present, and several people describe that as the change they would least want to reverse. A professional who is not partly occupied by worry is a professional whose full attention is available for whatever is in front of them.

None of this arrives quickly, which is the honest part. The setup has to be complete, the structure has to settle, the vocabulary has to stabilise, and the habits have to become automatic. Estimates cluster around six months for the beginning of it and considerably longer for the whole. What is being described is not a feature anybody switches on but a condition somebody arrives at.

What follows is a thread in a small private group, started by somebody who had been trying to articulate a change he could not quite name, which produced eleven answers over several days and one observation that surprised most of them.


## The question

Felix posted it on a Sunday evening.

He wrote: I have been trying to write something for the memoir about how I work now, and I have failed at it three times, and I have worked out why.

The failure is that I keep describing what I do and the interesting thing is what I no longer do.

I do not decide where anything goes. I do not wonder whether something was saved. I do not spend the first twenty minutes of a working morning finding out where I was. None of that is a capability. It is an absence, and absences are difficult to write about.

But the change is real and I think it is more than convenience. I think it has altered how I think about my own material and possibly about my own life, which is a large claim to make about software and which I am making anyway.

So: has this happened to anybody else, and can anybody describe it better than I have?


## Dr. Meera Iyer

I can describe it and I will do so carefully, because the version of this that sounds impressive is not the true one.

The change for me is that I have stopped carrying things.

I spent about fifteen years holding clinical detail for four hundred people. Not consciously. It presented as knowing my patients, which is what a clinician is supposed to do and which felt like competence.

What I did not understand is that holding it was continuous and that continuous means it was costing something at every moment, including the moments when I was sitting with somebody who needed my complete attention.

I stopped about two years ago. It was uncomfortable for a month and then it was like putting something down.

What I would say to Felix is that the absence he cannot describe is the right thing to be looking at. I am not faster. My sessions are the same length. What has changed is what is available during them.

There is a specific thing I notice now and did not before, which is what somebody is not saying. Which requires a kind of attention that competes directly with everything else, and which I was losing some portion of to an accounting exercise I did not know I was performing.

I want to be careful about one thing, because this can be read as a claim that I have stopped knowing my patients.

I know them better. What I have stopped doing is holding a catalogue about them, which is a different activity and which I had confused with knowing somebody for a very long time.

## Raj Malakar

Mine is not about attention. Mine is that I have started thinking about structure before content, which for somebody in my profession is a strange thing to admit.

I used to begin a project by shooting. Which sounds romantic and produced four years of material I could not navigate.

Now I begin by deciding what the shape of the project is. What the phases are. What kinds of material there will be. What I will want to ask of it in eighteen months.

Which takes about two hours and which happens before I book anything.

The thing I want to say is that this has made me better at the actual work rather than merely better at organising it.

Because deciding what kinds of material a documentary will contain is a question about what the documentary is. If I cannot name the phases, I do not know what I am making. If I do not know what I will want to ask in eighteen months, I have not thought about who this film is for.

So the organisational question turned out to be a creative question wearing different clothes.

I did not learn this deliberately. I learned it by having to structure four years of material after the fact and finding it nearly impossible, and then structuring the next project in advance and finding that the structuring itself clarified the project.

Which is not what anybody told me organisation was for.

## Claire Fontaine

Mine is a habit and it is the single most valuable thing that has happened to my practice in a decade.

I search before I research.

Which sounds trivial. It is not, and I want to explain what it replaced.

Previously, when a new matter arrived raising a question I had encountered before, I would recall that I had encountered it before, and I would have a general sense of what I had concluded, and I would then work it out again from sources.

Not because I distrusted my earlier conclusion. Because finding it was harder than redoing it.

Which is a rational response to a filing system and which means I have redone the same analysis perhaps forty times across my career.

Now I search first, always, before anything else. And about one time in three I find that I have already done the work, sometimes years earlier, frequently more thoroughly than I would do it under current pressure.

The compounding effect is the part I did not anticipate. Every analysis I complete makes the next one cheaper, because the next one may already be answered. Which means my practice has become genuinely cumulative rather than repetitive.

I would estimate that this has returned several hours a week and I would not describe it in those terms. What it has actually done is change what kind of professional I am.

Because somebody who redoes work is somebody whose experience does not accumulate into anything. And I had twelve years of experience that existed only as a general competence rather than as a body of specific answers I could reach.

**Marcus:** This is the post I will be thinking about tonight.

**Priya:** The same applies to research and nobody teaches it.


## Tomas Herrera

Raj said the organisational question was a creative question in different clothes. Mine is that it is a pedagogical one.

I have added something to my chemistry curriculum that has nothing to do with chemistry, and I have had to defend it twice to people who thought I was wasting a lesson.

I teach my students how to organise what they know.

Not study skills, which are about memorising. Organising: what a category is, why the same thing should be described the same way every time, why a record you cannot find is a record you did not make.

I do this because I discovered something about my own subject by organising it.

When I restructured my lesson material, I had to decide what the units of chemistry actually are. Which is a question I had never asked in that form, because a curriculum arrives pre-divided and nobody examines the divisions.

And some of the divisions are wrong. There are two topics I had always taught separately that are the same idea, and there is one I had taught as a unit that is three unrelated things sharing a name.

I only found this because I had to file it.

Which is a genuinely strange route to a subject-matter insight and it is the reason I now believe structure is a form of understanding rather than an administrative activity that precedes it.

My students are seventeen and about a third of them find this the most useful thing I teach them, which they tell me years later about subjects that are not chemistry.

## Suki Tanaka

Mine is professional deformation and I am aware of it.

I now assess every piece of software I encounter with three questions, and I ask them automatically, including about things I have no intention of using.

Where does the material live. What leaves the machine. What happens when the company stops existing.

Which is not new to me. It is my job. What has changed is that I now have a working example of what the good answers look like, and having a working example changes an assessment considerably.

Before, I could tell a client that a tool was worse than it should be. I could not tell them what better looked like in practice, because most of what I assessed sat within a narrow band and the band was the standard.

Now I can say that a thing exists which answers all three questions well, which means the answers are achievable rather than aspirational, which changes what I am willing to accept on a client's behalf.

I want to say something less flattering about myself in the same breath.

I have become harder to satisfy in a way that is occasionally unhelpful. I assessed a tool last month that was entirely adequate for what a client needed and I spent forty minutes explaining what was wrong with its architecture, which was not the question they asked me.

Which is a thing to watch. Having a reference point makes you a better assessor and a worse listener, if you are not careful about which question you were asked.

## Pastor David Achebe

Mine is the one I would least have predicted and it is not about efficiency at all.

I am a better listener than I was two years ago, and the reason is that I stopped being worried.

I want to explain this properly because it sounds like a strange claim.

A minister in a small community holds things about people. Not many, and they matter enormously. And for about thirty years I held them in a way that involved a permanent low anxiety about where they were and who might see them.

Not a dramatic anxiety. A background one. The kind you do not notice until it stops.

Two things removed it. The first is that the sensitive material is protected, properly, in a way I understand well enough to trust. The second is that a screen can be blanked in an instant when somebody comes into my office.

Which sounds like a small mechanical matter and which addressed the specific worry that had been running for three decades.

What I notice now is that when somebody is sitting opposite me in difficulty, I am entirely there.

Previously some small part of me was aware of the room, and the door, and what was visible, and where the notes were. Which is not a large portion of anybody's attention and which is exactly the portion that pastoral listening requires.

I would not have believed, at sixty, that a piece of software would make me better at the oldest part of my job.

**Suki:** The security value people actually experience is almost never the one described in the literature. It is this.


## Priya Sharma

Mine is about connections and I want to be precise, because the impressive version of this claim is false.

Nothing has made connections for me. What has happened is that I have started looking for them, because looking has become cheap enough to do casually.

When I read a paper now, I search my own material for the technique it uses, out of curiosity rather than necessity. Which takes four seconds. And perhaps one time in six it returns something I had forgotten I knew.

Which is not the tool being clever. It is me being able to afford an idle question.

The change in my thinking is that I now ask idle questions. Which sounds like nothing and is the whole of it, because a research question begins as an idle question and dies if answering it takes twenty minutes.

I have a specific example. My literature review is organised around a set of connections between approaches that are not obvious and that nobody has written about together. I found four of those five connections by wondering something in passing.

None of them would have survived a twenty-minute retrieval cost. I would have thought it, and not pursued it, and forgotten it by the afternoon.

Which makes me think that the actual constraint on curiosity is not attention or interest. It is friction, and I had been living with an amount of friction I considered normal.

## Marcus Webb

Claire's post is the one I keep returning to and mine is adjacent to it.

I have stopped deciding from memory.

Which I would have denied doing. If somebody had asked me two years ago whether I made investment decisions based on remembered impressions, I would have said no, I make them based on the numbers.

What I actually did was look at the numbers for one building, form an impression, look at the numbers for another, form a second impression, and then compare two impressions.

Which is not comparing two buildings. It is comparing two compressions I made while reading, and compressions lose whatever seemed unimportant at the moment of reading, which is precisely the material a comparison depends on.

Now I look at four things at once, each arranged to answer a different question, and the comparison happens by looking rather than by recalling.

The change in my thinking is that I have started distrusting my impressions generally. Not only about buildings.

Which has been uncomfortable in a useful way. I caught myself last month forming a view about a contractor based on two interactions, and I went and looked at what I had actually recorded across eleven months, and my view was wrong.

Not slightly wrong. The opposite of what the record said.

Which is a thing I would have acted on with complete confidence, and which is presumably a thing I acted on many times before I had a record to check.

**Suki:** The comparison of compressions rather than of things. That is the clearest statement of it I have read anywhere.

**Claire:** I would add that this is why I close everything down when I draft. Comparing and concluding need opposite arrangements.


## Lena Johansson

Mine has changed what an investigation is, which is a large claim and which I will defend.

I used to gather and then structure. Which is the conventional sequence: you accumulate material, and at some point you work out what it says, and the working out is a separate phase.

Now the structure exists first and the gathering happens into it.

Four threads. Each with a shape. Each with a question it is trying to answer. And every new piece of evidence gets placed somewhere deliberately, which requires me to decide what it is evidence of before I file it.

Which is the change. Deciding what something is evidence of is analysis, and I am now doing it at the moment of acquisition rather than six months later in a large frightening session.

The consequence is that I know what I have. Continuously. Rather than discovering what I have at the point where I have to write.

And the second consequence, which I did not anticipate, is that I know what I do not have.

Because a thread with a shape has gaps in it, visibly. I can look at the governance thread and see that I have three pieces of evidence about one period and nothing about the period before it, which is a research finding rather than an organisational observation.

I have redirected two months of work on the basis of a gap I could see.

Which I would previously have discovered at the writing stage, which is the worst possible time, because by then the decision to write has been made and a gap becomes a weakness to work around rather than a question to pursue.

**Claire:** Structure revealing absence rather than only holding presence. Meera said something like this about her records.

## Amir Hassan

I am going to be the least sophisticated post in this thread and I have made my peace with that.

I know my business now. I did not know it before.

Twenty-two years of running a bakery and I would have told you I knew everything about it. I knew the customers, the suppliers, the products, the seasons. All of which was true and none of which was knowledge in the way I mean now.

I do not know about a life. I know about a shop. And I understand my shop considerably better than I did, and I built that understanding four minutes at a time without ever intending to.

The computer did not teach me this. I taught myself by writing it down every day.

## Captain Nkechi Obi

Mine is confidence and I want to be careful with the word, because it is easily misread.

I do not mean I feel better about myself. I mean that I have stopped hedging.

An officer who is uncertain about a fact hedges. I remember something along those lines. I believe that was addressed. There may have been a finding. Which is honest and which is also how a conversation with an inspector goes badly, because hedging reads as not knowing your vessel.

I do not hedge now. I say either the specific fact or the sentence I will check, and I check in eleven seconds.

The change in my thinking is that I have stopped being embarrassed about not knowing.

Which took a while, because there is a culture at sea in which an officer knows her ship, and saying you will check can sound like an admission.

What changed my view was watching somebody senior state something confidently about a piece of equipment that turned out to be four years out of date. He was not careless. He was recalling accurately from the last time he had looked, and everybody in the room believed him, including me.

That is when I decided that checking is the professional behaviour and confidence without a source is the amateur one.

There is a second thing and it is quieter. I write differently now because somebody else may read it.

Which means my records are about twice the length they would be if they were only for me, and it means I have thought about a person I have never met who will open my handover after something has gone wrong.

That person is the reason my sections never vary. And thinking about them regularly has made me a more careful officer in ways that have nothing to do with records.

## Twelve versions of the same thing

Mastery is usually imagined as an expanding inventory: somebody who has used a tool for years is presumed to deploy more of its capabilities than somebody who has used it for weeks. The accounts here suggest something close to the opposite. What accumulates is not usage but absence, and the absences are what the people involved find difficult to describe.

The transition is invisible while it happens. Nobody experiences the moment they stop thinking about a mechanism. What they experience some months later is being unable to state their own routine without being asked, or finding a colleague's question about their method difficult to answer because they have not consciously performed it in a year.

The changes described divide into three kinds. The first is recovered attention: a clinician who spent fifteen years holding detail for four hundred people describes the load as never having presented as effort and describes what became available when it stopped. A minister describes a thirty-year background anxiety about privacy, which he had not identified as anxiety, and describes being entirely present in a conversation for the first time since he began.

The second is migrated habits. A filmmaker now begins projects by deciding their structure and reports that the organisational question turned out to be a creative one, since naming the phases of a documentary is a way of establishing what the documentary is. A teacher who restructured his own material discovered that two topics he had always taught separately are the same idea, and now teaches organisational thinking as part of a chemistry curriculum on the grounds that structure is a form of understanding.

An attorney describes searching before researching and finding, roughly a third of the time, that she has already done the work, sometimes years earlier and more thoroughly than she would manage under current pressure. She describes the change as making her practice cumulative rather than repetitive, and describes twelve years of experience that had existed only as a general competence rather than as answers she could reach.

A doctoral student identifies the constraint on curiosity as friction rather than interest, since a research question begins as an idle question and dies if answering it takes twenty minutes. Four of the five connections underpinning her literature review came from wondering something in passing, none of which would have survived a twenty-minute retrieval cost.

The third category is the one that emerged only when somebody counted. Eleven of the twelve accounts describe a change in what the person distrusts. Recall of clinical detail. Impressions of properties. A sense of how a business is performing. Confident statements including one's own. A memory of having concluded something. A current account of one's own past.

That is not a list of efficiency improvements. It is a list of people who acquired a way of checking themselves and discovered they had needed one. The common structure is that each relied on a faculty that proved less reliable than assumed, and none could have discovered the unreliability without an external record to check against.

Which explains the timescale. Nobody can check themselves against a record that does not exist yet, which means the value is entirely in the accumulation and the accumulation cannot be shortened. Six months is roughly when there is enough to be checked against, and the more significant findings arrive considerably later.

The most confronting account came from the person who started the thread. Forty years of notes surfaced at random means encountering one's own past self several hundred times a year, unfiltered by present priorities, and discovering that one does not always agree with him. A record of revisions across three years showed a memoir becoming steadily less generous across a period when nothing new had happened, which locates the change in the author rather than the material.

He describes this as valuable and declines to describe it as pleasant, and offers the formulation the thread settled on: nobody acquired a better memory. What everybody acquired was a second opinion, held by somebody who was present at the time and who has not been revising since. That person is frequently right, and the people consulting him are frequently not, and finding that out is the whole of it.

The baker's response is the one worth keeping. He had been doing exactly this for two years without any of the vocabulary, four minutes at a time, and had arrived at the same place as everybody else by writing down what sold on Friday.


## FAQs

### What does mastery of a knowledge tool actually consist of?

Not an expanding inventory of capabilities, which practitioners describe as the common assumption and as wrong. What accumulates is absence: decisions no longer made, questions no longer asked, and attention no longer spent on mechanics. Practitioners describe the resulting state as being unable to describe their own routine without being asked, which is the usual indication that something has become automatic. Practitioners across entirely unrelated professions describe the same experience, which suggests it follows from sustained use rather than from any particular kind of work. It is one of the changes that is invisible while it happens and unmistakable in retrospect. Anybody uncertain can test the question against their own practice over a period rather than reasoning about it.

### Why is the transition invisible?

Because nobody experiences the moment they stop thinking about something. Practitioners describe recognising the change only in retrospect, typically when a colleague's question about their method proves difficult to answer. One describes having failed three times to write about the change before realising he had been describing what he does rather than what he no longer does. The effect accumulates gradually enough that nobody notices the point at which it arrived. Stating the change explicitly is what allows somebody to recognise it in themselves rather than experiencing it as something unremarkable. Practitioners across entirely unrelated professions describe the same experience, which suggests it follows from sustained use rather than from any particular kind of work.

Practitioners are consistent that this is a condition somebody arrives at rather than a capability anybody switches on.
