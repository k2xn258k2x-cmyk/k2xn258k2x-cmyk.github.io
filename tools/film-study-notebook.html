Watch closely

Twelve elements of film language, each with what it is, what it does to a viewer, and the tell that gives away a deliberate choice. Read one before a rewatch and you will spend that viewing noticing things you have looked at a hundred times without seeing.

The single habit that changes everything

Watch a scene twice. The first time, follow the story the way the film wants you to. The second time, refuse to follow it: pick one element from the list above and track only that. Where is the camera and why. What is the cut rate doing. What is lit and what has been allowed to fall into shadow. Almost every reading worth writing comes from a second pass in which you deliberately stopped watching the plot.

Critical frameworks

Seven ways of asking what a film is doing. None of them is the correct one, and the useful move is knowing which question a given film rewards. Each entry gives the core claim, the method, the strongest objection, and the kind of film it works best on.

Choosing one, and admitting it

The weakest film writing borrows a sentence from each framework and commits to none, which produces an essay that cannot be disagreed with because it has not claimed anything. Pick the frame that the film actually rewards, name it in your opening, and accept the objection that comes with it. An argument someone can push back on is worth more than a survey nobody can dispute.

Beat map

Lay the structure out and the film stops being a stream and becomes a shape. Pick a template, fill in what happens at each beat with its timecode, and the proportions tell you where the film thinks its own weight sits.

Reading the proportions

Once the timecodes are in, look at the gaps rather than the beats. A first act that runs to the halfway mark is telling you the film is about the situation rather than the resolution. A climax that arrives with thirty minutes left means the real subject is the aftermath. Structure templates are descriptive rather than prescriptive, and the interesting films are usually the ones that fit badly. When a film refuses the shape, ask what it gained by refusing.

Shot log

The record that separates an impression from an argument. Log the shot, its scale, what the camera does, and what you noticed. Twenty entries across a sequence will show you a pattern you would never have held in your head.

Compare

Two columns, one question. Use it for a source and its adaptation, an original and a remake, two films by the same director, or two treatments of the same genre problem. The comparison rows force you to answer the same question of both, which is where the argument comes from.
