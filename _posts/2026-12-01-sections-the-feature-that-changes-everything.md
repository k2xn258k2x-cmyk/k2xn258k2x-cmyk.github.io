Almost every tool for keeping notes models a note the same way: a title and a body. That model is not wrong, it is simply an assumption about what people are recording, and the assumption is that a note is a piece of writing. For a thought, an observation, or a paragraph of prose it is entirely adequate, and it is why the model has survived unchanged across decades of software that otherwise shares nothing.

It stops being adequate the moment somebody is recording something rather than writing something. A patient encounter is not a piece of writing. It is a set of distinct kinds of information about one occasion: what was observed, what was decided, what was administered, and a set of administrative facts that concern the same person and belong to a completely different register. A property has a lease, an inspection history, a maintenance record, a correspondence trail, and a financial position, all of which concern one unit and none of which belong in the same paragraph as any other.

![Sections: The Feature That Changes Everything](/assets/img/feature-screenshot-placeholder.svg)

Faced with that, people do one of two things and both fail slowly. The first is to write everything into one long body, using headings to indicate the divisions, which works until the body reaches a certain length and then becomes a document nobody scans and nobody maintains. The second is to create separate notes for each kind of content and link them together, which preserves the divisions and destroys the fact that they concern one thing.
<!--more-->


The second failure is the more expensive and the less obvious. A collection of linked notes about one property, one patient, or one lesson is technically complete and practically unusable, because the relationship between them exists only in whoever built it. Somebody arriving later, or the same person eighteen months later, finds five items that must be assembled mentally before anything can be understood, and the assembly is exactly the work the record was supposed to have done. The information survived; the coherence did not.

What resolves this is treating sub-structure as a real thing rather than as a formatting convention. If each division within an entry is itself a container, with its own title, its own editable body, and its own attached files, then the record can hold several distinct kinds of content while remaining one record. The lease sits with the lease documents. The inspection sits with the photographs. Nothing has to be reassembled, because nothing was ever separated.

The second property that matters is that such divisions can be collapsed. An entry containing six substantial sections occupies one screen when closed, showing six labelled bars, and expands only where somebody is currently working. That turns length into a non-issue: a record can accumulate for years without becoming harder to navigate, which is the failure that eventually kills the single-long-body approach. It also does something less anticipated, which is that a collapsed record reveals nothing about its contents to anybody glancing at a screen.

The result is a middle position that most tools do not offer. The usual choice is between simplicity, meaning flat notes that are easy and structurally weak, and structure, meaning linked databases that are powerful and require somebody to become an administrator of their own information. Sub-entries provide the structure inside the simple model, so a person creates one record per thing and the record itself carries the complexity.

What follows is a conversation between three people who reached the same conclusion independently, in professions with nothing in common, and who arranged the call specifically because they had each said the same thing in a group discussion and wanted to know why.


## Three people who all named the same thing

‘I want to establish that this call exists because of a coincidence,’ Meera said.

‘It exists because of a coincidence and I want to know whether it is one,’ said Marcus.

Tomas had joined from a classroom with a period free and the door propped open.

‘Say what happened for anybody who was not following,’ he said.

‘Somebody asked in the group what one feature people would keep if they could only have one,’ Meera said. ‘Which is a slightly silly question and produced a genuinely interesting result.’

‘Three of us said the same thing within about an hour.’

‘Three of us said sections, using almost the same words, and nobody else did.’ She was interested rather than amused. ‘And we work in healthcare, property, and secondary education, which have no overlap whatsoever.’

‘I would go further,’ Marcus said. ‘I would say it is not my favourite feature. I would say it is the reason the tool works for me at all, and that without it I would be using something else.’

‘That is exactly my position and I was not going to state it that strongly,’ Tomas said.

‘Then state it strongly, because the point of this call is to find out whether we are describing the same thing or three different things that happen to have one name.’

Meera took the moderating role she always ended up with.

‘Then let us each describe what our sections actually are,’ she said, ‘before anybody explains why they matter.’


## What each of them puts in them

‘I will go first because mine is the most rigid,’ Meera said.

She did not share her screen, which the other two noticed and neither commented on.

‘Every patient entry has four sections and they are identical across every entry I have ever made,’ she said. ‘Session details, which is date, duration, format, and anything about the circumstances. Clinical observations, which is what I saw. Treatment notes, which is what I decided and why. And administrative, which is billing, correspondence, referrals, and anything that is about the arrangement rather than the person.’

‘Four, always?’

‘Four, always, including when one of them is empty.’ She was definite. ‘An empty administrative section on a session entry says there was no administrative dimension to that appointment, which is a fact. If it were absent I would not know whether it was absent or forgotten.’

‘That is the argument I make about my own,’ Marcus said, ‘and I have never heard anybody else make it.’

‘Say yours.’

‘Five per unit, and a unit is a door rather than a building.’ He shared his. ‘Lease details. Inspection history. Maintenance log. Tenant communications. Financial.’

‘And a building with fourteen units?’

‘Is a page with fourteen entries under it, each with the same five sections.’ He shrugged. ‘Which sounds repetitive and is the entire point, because when I open any door in any building anywhere in the portfolio, the same five bars are in the same order.’

‘How long did that take to settle?’

‘About a year, and I will tell you what it replaced later because it is the most useful thing I have to say.’

Tomas went last.

‘Mine varies by kind of entry, which makes me the least consistent person on this call,’ he said.

‘Say why it varies.’

‘Because a laboratory session and a lecture are genuinely different things and forcing them into one shape would produce empty sections that are not informative, they are just empty.’ He pulled his up. ‘A lab entry has objectives, materials, procedure, assessment, and reflection. A lecture entry has objectives, content, activities, and reflection. Four against five, and materials and procedure are the difference, because a lecture has no equipment and no sequence that anybody can be hurt by.’

‘So the shape follows the kind.’

‘The shape follows the kind, and within a kind it is absolutely rigid.’ He was emphatic. ‘Every lab entry I have has those five, in that order, without exception, across about two hundred of them.’

Meera made a noise.

‘Then we have all said the same thing,’ she said. ‘Standard sections per type of thing, applied without exception, including when a section is empty.’

‘Which is a template,’ Marcus said.

‘Which is a template that nobody told any of us to make,’ Tomas said, ‘and which all three of us arrived at within about a year, in three professions, without discussing it.’

‘Then that is the first finding and it was worth the call already,’ said Meera.

‘What does it produce?’ Tomas asked. ‘Beyond tidiness, which is not a professional argument.’

‘Completeness,’ Marcus said immediately. ‘That is my answer and I would defend it against anybody.’

‘Say more.’

‘A blank section is a question.’ He counted it out. ‘If every unit has an inspection section and one of them is empty, I have not inspected that unit. Not I have not written it up. I have not done it. The empty section found the gap and no list anywhere would have.’

‘Mine is the same and the stakes are different,’ Meera said. ‘An empty treatment section on a session means I recorded what I saw and not what I decided, which is a genuine clinical documentation failure and one I would be asked about.’

‘And mine is safety,’ said Tomas. ‘An empty materials section on a lab entry means somebody could run that lab without knowing what is in the room.’


## The part that is easy to miss

‘Now the thing I would put above everything else,’ Marcus said, ‘and which took me longest to start doing properly.’

‘Attachments,’ said Meera.

‘Attachments, and specifically that they belong to the section rather than to the entry.’

‘Say why that matters, because I want to hear your version,’ Tomas said. ‘Mine is about safety and yours is about disputes and I suspect they are the same argument.’

Marcus opened a unit.

‘This one has about thirty files on it,’ he said. ‘Four years of a tenancy. Lease documents, a renewal, inspection reports, forty photographs, eleven invoices, a certificate, and a correspondence trail with an insurer.’

‘And in a flat list?’

‘In a flat list it is thirty names chosen by other people.’ He was flat about it. ‘A camera named a photograph. A contractor's office manager named an invoice. A letting agent named a document after their own reference number. Nobody in that list was thinking about me.’

‘So the names tell you nothing.’

‘The names tell me almost nothing and I have spent an hour of my life scrolling a list like that while somebody waited on a telephone.’ He expanded a section. ‘Whereas here, the lease documents are in lease details. The photographs are in inspection history. The invoices are in maintenance. The insurer correspondence is in communications.’

‘And you no longer need to remember what anything is called.’

‘I need to remember what kind of thing I want, which I always know, because I know why I am looking.’ He shrugged. ‘Nobody has ever opened a record wanting an unspecified document. They want the lease, or they want the photographs from the March inspection, and both of those are a section rather than a filename.’

Meera described hers, which was more constrained.

‘Mine is smaller in volume and the placement matters more,’ she said. ‘Clinical documents go to observations. Anything I have written for the patient goes to treatment. Insurance forms, referral letters, and correspondence with a general practitioner go to administrative.’

‘Why does that division matter so much for you?’

‘Because those are three different categories of sensitivity and three different audiences.’ She was precise. ‘An insurance form is a document a third party has already seen. A clinical instrument I have used in a session is not. Keeping them in separate places within one record means I never have to think about which is which when I am producing something for somebody.’

‘So the structure encodes a policy.’

‘The structure encodes a policy that would otherwise live in my head and would fail on the day I was tired,’ Meera said.

Tomas took the last one and was more animated than either.

‘Mine is safety and I want to explain why placement rather than presence is the whole thing,’ he said.

‘Go.’

‘My materials section on any lab entry carries the safety documentation for everything in that lab.’ He opened one. ‘Not on the entry. On materials, which is the section a person reads when they are collecting equipment, which is the moment they need it.’

‘And if it were on the entry?’

‘If it were on the entry it would be in a list at the bottom with the rubric and the worksheet and the seating plan, and a substitute teacher in a hurry would scroll past it.’ He was serious. ‘I have watched somebody do exactly that with a paper folder. The document was present. It was present in the wrong place, which for practical purposes is absent.’

‘That is the strongest version of the argument on this call,’ Marcus said.

‘It is the strongest version because the consequence is a sixteen year old and a corrosive rather than an invoice,’ Tomas said. ‘But it generalises. A document is only as useful as the moment it is encountered, and placement determines the moment.’

They discussed the indicators, which all three used constantly and none had thought about.

‘The small marks on the collapsed bars,’ Meera said. ‘Showing that a section carries something.’

‘That is the thing I actually look at first,’ Marcus said. ‘I do not open sections to find out whether there is anything in them. The bar tells me, and if the maintenance bar on a unit shows nothing after four years then that unit has never had a repair, which is either excellent or a records failure and I want to know which.’

‘Mine tells me something similar and faster,’ Tomas said. ‘If a lab entry's materials bar is bare, that entry is not ready to be taught from, and I can see that across a whole unit in about four seconds without opening anything.’


## Closing things as a working technique

‘I want to raise something that sounds trivial,’ Meera said, ‘and which I would defend as the reason I can do my job with a screen in the room.’

‘The collapsing,’ said Tomas.

‘The collapsing, and specifically that it is a tool rather than a convenience.’

She described her working pattern.

‘A patient entry accumulates. Somebody I have seen for two years has a great deal in that record.’ She paused. ‘During a session I have exactly one section expanded, which is the current one, and everything else is closed.’

‘Attention.’

‘Attention is half of it and the smaller half.’ She was careful. ‘The larger half is that a person sitting three feet from me can see my screen, and what they see is a set of closed bars with dates on them. Not their history. Not what I wrote last month. Not the observation I made in the spring that they would find upsetting.’

‘That is not a feature anybody designed for that,’ Marcus said.

‘It is not, and it is the single most useful property it has for me.’ Meera was definite. ‘I lock the whole thing when I leave the desk, obviously. But during a session I am at the desk and the material is open, and a collapsed record is a record that is present and unreadable.’

Tomas came in.

‘Mine is entirely about attention and it is the same mechanism,’ he said. ‘Before a lab I expand procedure and nothing else. Not because the rest is sensitive. Because the rest is a distraction from the eleven steps I am about to walk thirty people through.’

‘And afterward?’

‘Afterward I expand reflection and close procedure, which is a physical gesture that marks the change from delivering to assessing.’ He laughed slightly. ‘Which I recognise is a ritual and which I think is a useful one.’

‘Rituals are underrated,’ Meera said.

Marcus described his, which was about scale rather than focus.

‘Mine is quarterly and it is the only way the review is possible at all,’ he said. ‘Thirty-one properties. During a financial review I open the financial section on each and nothing else.’

‘How long does that take?’

‘About ninety minutes for the portfolio, and before sections it took a day and a half.’ He was matter of fact. ‘Because previously the financial information was in a long body somewhere below the maintenance history, and finding it meant reading past everything I was not looking at, on a hundred and forty doors.’

‘So the collapse is a filter.’

‘The collapse is a filter I apply with my eyes rather than with a query,’ Marcus said, ‘and it is available instantly on a record I am already looking at, which no filter is.’


## What sections do to being found

‘There is a technical dimension I want to raise,’ Meera said, ‘because it changed something about how I name my sections and I would like to know whether either of you has noticed it.’

‘Search,’ said Marcus.

‘Search. Section content participates, at its own weight, and section titles are part of that.’

‘Explain the weights, because I know they exist and I have never learned them,’ Tomas said.

‘Somebody in the group established them by experiment and published the list.’ Meera read from something. ‘Titles are highest at eight. Labels next at six. Text found inside images within an entry at five. The body at four. Section text at three. Attachments belonging to the entry at two. Attachments belonging to a section at one.’

‘So sections rank below the body.’

‘Section text ranks just below the body, which is exactly right, because the body is what the entry is about and a section is one aspect of that.’ She paused. ‘And section attachments are the lowest of everything, which is also right and which took me a moment to accept.’

‘Why is it right?’

‘Because placement is a claim about scope.’ Marcus said it before she could. ‘Something attached to the entry concerns the whole entry. Something attached to a section concerns one part of it. The second is more specific and therefore less representative of what the record is about.’

‘That is precisely the argument and I am slightly annoyed that you made it,’ Meera said.

They ran a test, at Tomas's suggestion, which he said afterward was the most useful four minutes of the call.

‘Search a word that only exists in a section title,’ he said.

Marcus did, using a term from one of his section names combined with something distinguishing, and the entry came back.

‘So the titles are searchable.’

‘The titles are searchable, which means they are metadata rather than decoration,’ Meera said. ‘And that is what changed my practice, because I had been treating them as headings.’

‘What did you change?’

‘I standardised them absolutely, which I had already mostly done, and then I stopped abbreviating.’ She was specific. ‘I had a section called Admin on some entries and Administrative on others, from different periods. Which is two names for one thing, and a query for either returns a subset.’

‘And you fixed them.’

‘I fixed about two hundred of them over three evenings, which is the only bulk correction I have ever done and which I did because it was mechanical.’ She shrugged. ‘Every entry of a type having identical section names means the section name itself becomes a reliable filter, and an unreliable one is worse than none.’

Tomas raised the case that interested him.

‘What about something inside a section attachment?’

Marcus tested it: a phrase from inside a document attached to one of his maintenance sections. The entry appeared, lower down.

‘It is there and it ranks below everything else,’ he said.

‘Which is what you want,’ Tomas said. ‘I do not want an entry surfacing at the top because a phrase appears in a safety document that is attached to two hundred lab entries. I want it present and I want it quiet.’

‘That is exactly the behaviour,’ Meera said. ‘Present and quiet. Which is a description I am going to reuse.’


## The argument about when to split

‘Now the interesting disagreement,’ Marcus said, ‘which is where I would like to spend the rest of this call.’

‘When something should be a section and when it should be its own entry,’ Tomas said.

‘And I want to say at the outset that all three of us got this wrong in different directions,’ said Meera, ‘which I know because we discussed it in the group and nobody drew the conclusion.’

Marcus went first because his was the largest correction.

‘I built the wrong thing for a year,’ he said. ‘Separate entries for each document type per unit. A lease entry. An inspection entry. A maintenance entry. Financial entries.’

‘Which is tidy.’

‘Which is extremely tidy and which produced, under each unit, a flat list of items in no meaningful order, none of which made sense alone.’ He was blunt. ‘And it failed during a dispute, which is when everything fails. Somebody's attorney asked what we did about a heating complaint and when, and the answer was distributed across four entries, and assembling it took forty minutes and read as a reconstruction.’

‘Rather than a record.’

‘Rather than a record, which is a distinction that matters enormously in that particular room,’ Marcus said. ‘So I collapsed it. One entry per unit, five sections, and the whole tenancy reads in order.’

Meera's evolution had run the other way.

‘I started with everything in one body,’ she said. ‘Which for the first six months was correct, because my entries were short and the divisions were obvious from the prose.’

‘And then?’

‘And then a long-term patient's record reached about four thousand words and became a document I was scrolling rather than reading.’ She was frank. ‘I could not find last month's observation without reading past three months of everything else, and I was reading things I did not need to read, which is not neutral in clinical work.’

‘So you split.’

‘I split, and it took an afternoon per long record, and there were eleven of them.’ She paused. ‘And I want to say that the split was not painful, because the divisions were already there. I had been writing headings. All I did was promote them.’

Tomas described a hybrid, which he had arrived at deliberately.

‘Each unit I teach is one entry with sections,’ he said. ‘But a major assessment gets its own entry, and I want to explain why, because it is a rule rather than a preference.’

‘Go on.’

‘Because an assessment needs things an entry can have and a section cannot.’ He counted. ‘It needs its own due date, because it happens on a specific day and I want it in a time view. It needs its own labels, because it belongs to a topic that cuts across three units. And once, it needed to be protected, because it contained a version of an exam.’

There was a pause.

‘That is the rule,’ Meera said.

‘Say it,’ said Marcus.

‘If the information is about the same subject and would be looked at together, it is a section.’ She was working it out as she spoke. ‘If it needs its own date, its own labels, or its own protection, it has to be an entry, because those are properties of entries.’

‘That is a considerably cleaner statement than anything I had,’ Tomas said.

‘It is a statement of what the mechanism can and cannot carry, which is better than a preference about tidiness,’ Meera said. ‘And it settles almost every case immediately.’

Marcus tested it against his own material.

‘A lease has no date of its own that I need in a time view,’ he said. ‘Except an expiry.’

‘Then does it become an entry?’

‘No, because the expiry belongs to the unit rather than to the document.’ He worked through it. ‘The unit entry carries the expiry. The lease document sits in the lease section. Which is right, and I would not have got there without your rule.’

‘Try one that fails it,’ Tomas said.

‘An insurance certificate,’ Marcus said immediately. ‘Which has its own expiry, distinct from anything else about the unit, and which I have been keeping in a section and chasing manually every year.’

‘Then that is an entry.’

‘That is an entry and I have eleven of them to make this week,’ he said, ‘which is a genuinely useful outcome from a call about accordions.’


## The one that died and the one that grew

‘I want to ask about failures,’ Tomas said, ‘because everything so far has been three people describing arrangements that work, and I do not believe any of us got there first time.’

‘I have two,’ said Marcus.

‘Take the more embarrassing one.’

‘I had a section called notes.’ He said it flatly. ‘On every unit. Which is exactly as useless as it sounds and which I kept for about eight months.’

‘What went in it?’

‘Everything that did not belong in the other four, which meant it accumulated a genuine mess and became the section I stopped opening.’ He shrugged. ‘Which is the diagnostic. A section you stop opening is a section that is not about anything.’

‘What did you do?’

‘I read all thirty-one of them in an afternoon and found that about half the content belonged in communications, about a third in maintenance, and the rest was genuinely about the building rather than the unit.’ He counted. ‘Which produced a change to my structure. Building-level entries now exist, which they did not before, holding the things that are true of the whole property rather than of a door.’

‘So an unusable section revealed a missing level.’

‘An unusable section revealed a missing level, which I would not have found any other way, because nothing about a page tree tells you that you are recording things at the wrong altitude.’

Meera had the opposite failure.

‘Mine is a section that grew until it was two things,’ she said. ‘My treatment section originally held both what I decided and what I actually did between sessions, which is correspondence, coordination with other clinicians, and anything I chased on somebody's behalf.’

‘And they diverged.’

‘They were never the same and I had not noticed, because at the beginning there was almost none of the second kind.’ She was frank. ‘Two years in, some records had four hundred words of clinical reasoning and eight hundred words of me telephoning a hospital, in one place, in one voice.’

‘How did you find it?’

‘I found it because I could no longer answer a simple question quickly.’ She paused. ‘Somebody asked me what I had decided at a particular session and I had to read past three paragraphs about a referral to find two sentences of clinical reasoning.’

‘And you split it.’

‘I split it and it became my fourth section, which is the administrative one I described earlier, and I want to be honest that the split took two weeks of evenings across about ninety records.’ She was even about it. ‘It was worth it and I would rather have decided at the beginning.’

Tomas offered his, which he described as the least dramatic.

‘Mine is that I had objectives and outcomes as separate sections for about a term,’ he said. ‘Which sounds like a meaningful distinction and is not one, in practice, because I was writing the same content in both and could not remember which was which.’

‘So you merged them.’

‘I merged them into objectives and moved on, and it took ten minutes.’ He shrugged. ‘Which is the useful contrast with both of yours. A section that should not have been split is trivially fixable. A section that should have been split and was not is expensive, because the content is already interleaved.’

‘That is a rule,’ Marcus said.

‘That is a rule and I would state it as: when in doubt, separate.’ Tomas was definite. ‘Merging two sections costs ten minutes. Untangling one costs two weeks of evenings and Meera has just described it.’


## How each of them makes a new one

‘Practical question,’ Tomas said. ‘When you create a new patient, or a new unit, do you build the sections by hand every time?’

‘Absolutely not,’ said Meera.

‘Say what you do.’

‘I have an entry that is a template. It sits in its own place, it has the four sections, it is empty, and it has a title that begins with a word that sorts it to the top.’ She described it. ‘When a new patient begins, I copy it, rename it, and I have the structure before I have written anything.’

‘How long does that take?’

‘Perhaps twenty seconds, against about four minutes of building sections by hand, which does not sound like much and which is the difference between doing it and not doing it at the end of a long day.’

Marcus had several.

‘Two templates, because residential and commercial are genuinely different,’ he said. ‘Residential has the five I described. Commercial has seven, because there are service charges and a schedule of condition and an insurance position that residential does not have.’

‘And you never mix them.’

‘I never mix them, and the moment I acquired the first commercial unit I built the template rather than adapting a residential one, which was ten minutes of work and has saved a considerable amount of confusion.’

Tomas had the most.

‘Four,’ he said. ‘Lab, lecture, assessment, and one for the robotics team, which has nothing to do with anything else I do.’

‘Four templates is a system,’ Meera said.

‘Four templates is four entries sitting in a page called templates that I copy from, which is less impressive than it sounds.’ He was cheerful. ‘And the value is not the time saved. It is that every lab entry I have made in three years has the same five sections in the same order, including the ones I made at eleven at night in a hurry.’

‘Which is the completeness argument again.’

‘Which is the completeness argument, and it only works because the structure arrives before the content.’ He was definite. ‘If I built sections as I needed them, the entries I made when I was tired would have three sections and the ones I made on a free period would have six, and the whole thing would be uneven in exactly the places where it matters most.’

Meera added the observation that closed the section.

‘There is a second benefit which none of us has mentioned,’ she said. ‘Which is what happens when somebody else has to use this.’

‘Say more.’

‘If a colleague covers my practice, or if my records are reviewed, the reviewer encounters the same four sections on every entry.’ She said it plainly. ‘They learn the structure once and it holds for four hundred records. That is the difference between a professional record and a personal notebook, and it costs nothing beyond having decided the shape at the beginning.’

‘Mine is a substitute teacher,’ Tomas said.

‘Mine is an attorney,’ said Marcus, ‘and in that room consistency is not a convenience, it is a form of credibility.’


## Records that outlive the person keeping them

‘There is one more thing and it is the reason I care about this more than I would about most features,’ Meera said.

‘Go on.’

‘My records will be read by somebody who is not me.’ She said it plainly. ‘Possibly by a colleague covering an absence. Possibly by a reviewer. Possibly, if something happened to me, by whoever takes over a practice with four hundred people in it.’

‘And a consistent structure survives that.’

‘A consistent structure is the only thing that survives that.’ She was definite. ‘Because what does not survive is my knowledge of my own arrangement. Everything I understand about which record contains what, and why I put something where I put it, is in my head and goes with me.’

‘Whereas four identical sections on every entry.’

‘Whereas four identical sections on every entry can be learned in about ninety seconds by somebody who has never seen any of it,’ Meera said. ‘Which is not a productivity argument. It is a duty-of-care argument, and it is the reason I would not use a system that could not do this.’

Marcus came in with a version he said was less serious and structurally identical.

‘Mine is a sale,’ he said. ‘When I sell a building, the records go with it, and the buyer's people read them.’

‘All of them?’

‘Enough of them to matter, and they are reading with an interest in finding something wrong.’ He was matter of fact. ‘Which means what they encounter is not just information, it is an impression. A record where every unit has the same five sections in the same order reads as a business that has been run properly. The same information distributed across a pile of differently-shaped notes reads as somebody who did not know what they were doing.’

‘Is that fair?’

‘It is not entirely fair and it is completely real,’ Marcus said. ‘And it has changed a price twice, in my favour, and I can identify the moment in both conversations.’

Tomas's was smaller and produced the longest silence.

‘Mine is a substitute,’ he said. ‘Which sounds trivial next to either of yours.’

‘Say it anyway.’

‘I was out for eleven days last spring, unexpectedly, and somebody I had never met taught four of my classes.’ He was quieter than he had been. ‘And I could not brief her, because I was not in a state to brief anybody.’

‘What happened?’

‘She opened the unit entries and every one of them had the same five sections, and procedure told her what to do and materials told her what was in the room and what could hurt somebody.’ He paused. ‘And she taught four days of chemistry to thirty teenagers each without speaking to me once, and nothing went wrong.’

Nobody said anything for a second.

‘She wrote in the reflection sections,’ Tomas added. ‘All four of them. Which nobody asked her to do and which I did not expect, and one of her observations was better than anything I had written about that lab in three years.’

‘Because she was seeing it for the first time,’ Meera said.

‘Because she was seeing it for the first time and had no idea which parts were supposed to be difficult,’ he agreed. ‘And I have kept all four and I use them.’


## Structure without administration

Three practitioners in healthcare, property, and secondary education, asked which single capability they would keep, named the same one within an hour of each other and independently. That convergence is the most informative thing in the conversation, because the three have no shared vocabulary, no shared constraints, and no reason to arrive at the same answer unless the answer concerns the shape of professional records rather than any particular field.

What they described is a model in which an entry is not a document but a container of several distinct kinds of content about one subject, each with its own title, its own editable body, and its own attached files. A patient session holds observations, decisions, and administrative facts in separate places. A property unit holds a lease, an inspection history, a maintenance record, correspondence, and a financial position. A laboratory session holds objectives, materials, procedure, assessment, and reflection. In every case those parts belong together and must not be blurred together, which is precisely the requirement that a flat title-and-body model cannot meet.

The strongest practical argument they made was not about organisation at all. It was that a standardised structure applied to every record of a type makes absences visible, and absences are otherwise invisible. An empty inspection section is not a missing note; it is an inspection that never happened. An empty treatment section is a documentation failure that would be asked about. An empty materials section is a laboratory nobody can safely be sent into. No list, task manager, or reminder produces that information, because all of them describe what exists, and the useful signal here is a gap in a shape.

Per-section attachment placement is the property most easily underestimated and the one all three identified as decisive. Thirty files attached to a record are thirty names chosen by cameras, contractors, and letting agents, none of whom were thinking about the person who will eventually look. The same thirty distributed across the sections they concern require no memory of any filename, because retrieval becomes a question of what kind of thing is wanted, which somebody always knows. Applying it consistently is what produces the compounding benefit, and consistency here costs nothing beyond having decided the shape once at the beginning.

### Do sections make sense for personal rather than professional use?

Wherever a subject has several distinct aspects, which is common outside work as well. A household project, a piece of property, a course of study, a vehicle, or an ongoing dispute all have documents, correspondence, costs, and a history that belong together and should not be blurred. The threshold is whether somebody would ever want to look at one aspect without the others, which is the same test that applies professionally. The benefit is cumulative rather than immediate, which is why it is easy to underrate during the first weeks and obvious after a year. Practitioners describe arriving at this after a correction rather than by design, which is worth knowing for anybody deciding how much to plan in advance and how much to leave until the material has said something.

### How long does it take to see the benefit?

The organisational benefit is immediate and the compounding benefits arrive at different points. Consistency helps from the first record. Completeness signals appear once enough records exist for gaps to be visible, usually within a few weeks. Comparison across instances of a recurring activity requires a year. Succession value is invisible until somebody else needs the records, at which point it is either present or entirely absent. Practitioners in unrelated professions arrive at the same conclusion independently, which suggests it reflects the shape of professional records rather than any particular field. The principle generalises well beyond any one profession, since the underlying situation is simply a subject with several distinct aspects that belong together and must not be blurred.

### What would you tell somebody setting this up for the first time?

Decide the sections for each kind of record you keep, before creating many records, using the divisions your work already has rather than inventing any. Build an empty template per type and copy it. Apply the structure without exception, including where a section will be empty. Attach files to the section they concern rather than to the record as a whole. And when uncertain whether two things are one section or two, make them two. It is one of the decisions that costs nothing at the outset and is tedious to correct later, which places it among the few worth deliberating over. Applying it consistently is what produces the compounding benefit, and consistency here costs nothing beyond having decided the shape once at the beginning.


### Why do people from unrelated professions converge on this?

Because the requirement is structural rather than domain specific. A clinician, a property investor, and a teacher all keep records about a persistent subject, all of which comprise several distinct kinds of content written in different registers, all of which accumulate over years, and all of which may eventually be read by somebody else. Those four conditions describe most professional record-keeping. The vocabulary differs completely and the shape does not, which is why the same answer appears repeatedly. Anybody whose own work meets those four conditions can reasonably expect the same arrangement to suit them, regardless of how little their field resembles healthcare, property, or teaching. The convergence is itself the strongest available evidence for that.

### What does this replace in a typical working life?

Usually a folder of loose documents alongside a set of notes that reference them, connected only by somebody's memory. Practitioners describe the previous arrangement as functional and slow: the information existed, the relationship between the pieces lived in their head, and any question required assembling several things before it could be answered. What changes is not the amount of material but whether it arrives already assembled, which is the difference between a record and a reconstruction. Practitioners describe the previous arrangement as one they had stopped noticing, since the assembly work had become invisible through repetition. The saving only becomes apparent once somebody has spent a few weeks without doing it.

### Is there any situation where a flat entry is better?

Frequently, for anything that genuinely is a single piece of writing. A thought, an observation, a reaction to something read, a short reference note, or a piece of prose has no internal divisions and gains nothing from being given some. Practitioners who use sections heavily for records also keep large numbers of flat entries for exactly this material, and the distinction is simply whether the item is one thing or several things about one subject. Most people end up with a mixture, using structured records for anything with a persistent subject and flat entries for everything else, and the two coexist without any friction at all.

### What is the single sentence to take from all of this?

That an entry should be able to hold several distinct kinds of content about one subject without either blurring them together or splitting them apart. Every consequence follows from that: consistent shapes that reveal absences, files that sit with the material they concern, records that stay navigable as they accumulate, and a structure somebody else can learn in two minutes when the person who built it is unavailable. Practitioners describe this as the property that turns a note-taking tool into something they can run a practice, a portfolio, or a teaching load on, which is a considerably larger claim than it initially sounds. Practitioners who have used both arrangements describe the difference as the amount of thinking required before a record can be understood, which is the cost that accumulates invisibly across years of ordinary use.

### How does this compare with using linked notes for the same purpose?

Linked notes preserve the content and lose the coherence. Five items connected by references are technically complete and require somebody to assemble the relationship mentally before anything can be understood, which is exactly the work the record was supposed to have done. The links also depend on discipline that erodes: a note created in a hurry frequently never gets linked. Sub-structure inside one record has neither problem, since the parts were never separated and nothing has to be maintained. That absence of upkeep is what allows the arrangement to survive periods when somebody has no attention available for their own systems, which is the only durability test that matters.

### Does this approach require any ongoing maintenance?

Almost none, which is what distinguishes it from database-style arrangements. Once a template exists, creating a record is a copy, and the structure holds by itself thereafter. There is no linking to maintain, no relationships to keep current, and nothing that becomes stale. Practitioners describe reviewing their section definitions perhaps once a year and otherwise not thinking about the arrangement at all, which is the correct amount of attention for infrastructure. Starting from what the work already looks like, rather than from an idea of how it ought to be organised, is the difference between a structure somebody keeps and one they abandon within a season.

### What should somebody do first if they want to adopt this?

Take the kind of record they create most often, write down the distinct sorts of content it actually contains, and build an empty template with those as sections. That usually takes fifteen minutes and can be done entirely from memory, since the divisions already exist in how the work is done. Then use it for the next few records before deciding whether anything is missing, rather than designing an elaborate shape in advance. Practitioners who have used both arrangements describe the difference as the amount of thinking required before a record can be understood, which is the cost that accumulates invisibly across years of ordinary use.


### Does adopting this change how somebody thinks about their work?

Several practitioners report that it does, in a way they did not anticipate. Deciding what sections a record needs requires naming the distinct kinds of content the work actually produces, which is a question most people have never been asked and cannot answer immediately. The exercise frequently reveals that two things treated as one are separate, or that something being recorded nowhere ought to be. The structure ends up describing the work rather than merely holding it. One practitioner described discovering a missing level in her hierarchy this way, and another found that two kinds of writing he had always produced together had never belonged in the same place. Neither observation came from thinking about their work directly; both came from being forced to name its parts.
