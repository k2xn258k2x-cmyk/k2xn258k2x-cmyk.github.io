Every system for holding information passes through a threshold that nobody announces and almost nobody notices at the time. On one side of it, the system assists memory: a person recalls that something exists, roughly where it might be, and uses the tool to reach it faster than they otherwise could. On the other side, the system replaces memory: the person does not recall the detail at all, has stopped attempting to, and searches instead.

The crossing is significant because it changes what a professional does with their attention. Below the threshold, somebody carries a working model of everything they might need, refreshed continuously, at a cost they have generally stopped noticing because it has been there for years. Above it, that model is no longer maintained, and whatever capacity it occupied becomes available for something else.

![When Your Knowledge Base Becomes Your Memory](/assets/img/feature-screenshot-placeholder.svg)

The cost of maintaining an internal model is easy to underestimate precisely because it is continuous. Nobody experiences it as effort in the way that solving a problem is effort. It presents instead as a low background occupancy: a portion of somebody's attention permanently committed to holding things that might be needed, which is unavailable for anything requiring full attention while it is doing so.
<!--more-->


There is also a reliability question that people avoid asking about themselves. Human recall is reconstructive rather than retrieval-based, which means that remembering something is partly rebuilding it, and the rebuilding is influenced by expectation, by recency, and by what somebody has thought about since. The result feels identical whether it is accurate or not, which is the property that makes it dangerous in any profession where being confidently wrong has consequences.

That combination, a continuous cost and an unmeasurable error rate, is what makes the crossing worth making deliberately for anybody whose work involves a substantial volume of detail. The person who has stopped attempting to hold it is not being careless. They have concluded that a written record is more accurate than their recollection and that their attention is better spent on the things a record cannot do.

Which raises the requirement that makes the whole arrangement possible or impossible. Nobody offloads to something they do not trust. The system must be available whenever it is needed, including in circumstances where nothing else is available. It must be accurate, which means structured consistently enough that its contents can be relied upon rather than interpreted. And it must be secure enough that offloading sensitive material to it is a responsible act rather than a reckless one.

What remains after the offloading is the part that determines whether the arrangement is worth anything. Nothing about a record replaces judgement, presence, or the accumulated intuition that lets somebody notice that a situation is wrong before they can say why. The externalisation is worth doing precisely because those things require full attention, and full attention is what somebody recovers when they stop holding a catalogue.

What follows is a conversation between two people whose professions could hardly be less alike and whose relationship to their own memory turns out to be nearly identical.


## Mumbai, and a question about trust

Nkechi had proposed it, which she said was because of something Meera had written in the group months earlier.

‘You said a thing about not remembering your patients,’ she said. ‘Which I read three times because it sounded wrong and I recognised it.’

‘Say what you recognised.’

‘That I have stopped trying to remember the ship.’ Nkechi was matter of fact. ‘Which sounds like a confession and is a decision.’

‘It is exactly a decision and I want to be careful about how I describe it.’ Meera was precise. ‘Because said carelessly it sounds like I have stopped caring about people, and it is closer to the opposite.’

‘Say it carefully then.’

‘I do not hold clinical detail in my head any more.’ She counted. ‘Medications. Dates. What was said in a session eleven months ago. Which family member is estranged and which is merely difficult.’

‘Which is a great deal.’

‘Which is a considerable amount of material across four hundred people, and I carried it for about fifteen years, and I stopped about two years ago.’

‘Say what stopping felt like.’

‘Uncomfortable for a month and then like putting something down.’ Meera was thoughtful. ‘Which is the metaphor everybody reaches for and it is accurate. I had not noticed I was carrying it until I was not.’

Nkechi nodded slowly.

‘Mine was faster and less considered.’

‘Say yours.’

‘A port inspector asked me about a deficiency from a previous voyage and I could not remember it.’ She was frank. ‘Standing on a deck, being asked a direct question, and my recollection was a general sense that something had happened.’

‘Which is not an answer.’

‘Which is not an answer and I found it in about eleven seconds and read it to him.’ Nkechi shrugged. ‘And afterward I thought about the fact that I had been intending to remember it and had not, and that the intention had been costing me something for six weeks.’


## Four hundred people and no catalogue

‘Describe what you actually do now,’ Nkechi said.

‘Before every appointment, I read.’ Meera was matter of fact. ‘Which takes four or five minutes and which is the whole of it.’

‘Say what you read.’

‘The previous session in full, and the observations from the two before it.’ She counted. ‘Which is enough to reconstruct where we are, and it is more accurate than anything I would have recalled.’

‘Say why more accurate.’

‘Because what I remember unaided about a person is a general impression and one or two salient details.’ Meera was precise. ‘And salient is not the same as important. Salient means memorable.’

‘Which is a different quality entirely.’

‘Which is a completely different quality, and the memorable things about a person are frequently the least clinically significant.’ She shrugged. ‘Somebody says something striking in a session and I would remember that for years. The medication change three weeks later is what actually matters and it is unremarkable.’

‘So memory selects wrongly.’

‘Memory selects for vividness and my work requires selecting for significance, and those two are almost unrelated.’ Meera was definite. ‘Which is the argument in one sentence and it took me a decade to state it.’

Nkechi asked about the mechanics.

‘Say what opening a record involves.’

‘A password, once, which then stays available for the working period.’ She was matter of fact. ‘Which sounds like a small detail and determines whether the arrangement is possible at all.’

‘Because otherwise.’

‘Because otherwise I would be entering it every time I touched anything during a fifty-minute appointment, which nobody would sustain for a week.’ Meera shrugged. ‘The friction has to be at the start and then absent.’

‘And the structure?’

‘Three sections, identical in every record.’ She counted. ‘Session notes. Treatment plan. Follow-up.’

‘Say why identical matters for this.’

‘Because I am not reading. I am checking specific things in specific places.’ Meera was precise. ‘Which takes four minutes rather than fifteen, and which only works because I know where everything is before I open it.’

‘So the consistency is what makes it fast.’

‘The consistency is what makes it a lookup rather than a reading exercise, and a lookup is what fits into the interval between two people.’

She described the incident she said had settled it.

‘A patient mentioned a medication she had stopped taking, in passing, and asked whether we had discussed it.’

‘Which you did not remember.’

‘Which I had a vague sense of and would not have asserted.’ Meera was frank. ‘And I searched the name while she was speaking and found it in a session three appointments earlier, with what she had said about why she stopped.’

‘Which changed the conversation.’

‘Which changed the conversation entirely, because I was able to say what she had told me rather than asking her to tell me again.’ She was definite. ‘And being asked to repeat something you have already said to somebody is a small erosion, and I had been doing it regularly for fifteen years.’


## The ship's memory

‘Now yours,’ Meera said. ‘Because I think the parallel is exact and I want to hear it in your vocabulary.’

‘Mine is a ship rather than four hundred people and it behaves identically.’ Nkechi counted. ‘Inspection findings. Drill records. Cargo arrangements. Maintenance history. Regulatory requirements that change.’

‘Which is a body of detail.’

‘Which is a body of detail that a competent officer is supposed to carry, and I do not, and I am better at my job for it.’ She was matter of fact.

‘Say what you would have done before.’

‘Held a general model of the vessel's condition and consulted the official records when I needed something specific.’ Nkechi shrugged. ‘Which is what everybody does and which has a failure I only noticed once I stopped.’

‘Say the failure.’

‘The general model is out of date and feels current.’ She was precise. ‘My sense of a piece of equipment is formed by the last time I thought about it, which might be four months ago, and it presents itself as knowledge rather than as a memory of four months ago.’

‘Which is exactly what Meera described.’

‘Which is precisely what she described in a completely different profession and it is the thing that made me want this conversation.’ Nkechi was definite. ‘My recollection and her recollection have the same defect and neither of us could see it from inside.’

‘Say what you do instead.’

‘I search, which for a specific question takes seconds.’ She counted. ‘And for the recurring things I do not search at all, because they have dates on them and they appear.’

‘Say the dates.’

‘Drill intervals, survey windows, certification renewals.’ Nkechi was matter of fact. ‘None of which I track. All of which appear when they need to.’

‘Which is a category of thing you have stopped thinking about entirely.’

‘Which is a category I used to think about continuously at a low level, and the ceasing is the thing I noticed most.’ She was thoughtful. ‘Some part of me always knew roughly when things were due. That part is free now.’

She described the inspection.

‘The deficiency the inspector asked about.’

‘Say what happened.’

‘He asked about a finding from a previous voyage, by category rather than by date, which is how inspectors ask.’ Nkechi counted. ‘And I searched the category and got three entries and read the relevant one.’

‘In front of him.’

‘In front of him, on a laptop, on a deck, with no connection to anything.’ She was definite. ‘Which is the part that matters. He asked at a moment when nothing else was available.’

‘And the record was complete.’

‘The record had what was found, what was done about it, and when it was verified, because the entry has fixed sections and one of them is exactly that.’ Nkechi shrugged. ‘Which is not memory. It is a record that is better than memory and available in the same circumstances memory is.’

‘Which is the whole requirement.’

‘Which is the entire requirement, and anything requiring a connection would have been useless on that deck.’


## What the offloading buys

‘I want to say something about why this works and I am going to be slightly technical,’ Meera said.

‘Say it.’

‘Working memory is small.’ She was precise. ‘Whatever the exact figure, and people argue about it, the practical position is that a person can hold a handful of things in active attention and no more.’

‘Which everybody knows.’

‘Which everybody knows and almost nobody applies to their own working practice.’ Meera was definite. ‘Because the things occupying it are not experienced as occupying anything. They present as knowing.’

‘Say the clinical version.’

‘I am sitting with somebody who is describing something difficult.’ She counted. ‘What I need in that moment is complete attention on what they are saying, what they are not saying, and how they are saying it.’

‘Which is a full occupation.’

‘Which is more than a full occupation, and any portion committed to holding their medication history is a portion not available for the person in front of me.’

‘So it is a trade.’

‘It is a trade and it was invisible to me for fifteen years because I was making it continuously and had nothing to compare against.’ Meera shrugged. ‘I did not know I was tired at the end of a day for that reason. I assumed it was the emotional weight.’

‘And it was not?’

‘It was partly the emotional weight and partly the accounting, and removing the accounting changed how a day feels in a way I did not predict.’

Nkechi said her version had no theory behind it.

‘Mine is bridge work and I would put it in physical terms,’ she said.

‘Say it.’

‘On a watch I need everything on the situation.’ She counted. ‘Traffic, weather, the state of the vessel, what the engine room is doing, and what is about to happen rather than what is happening.’

‘Which is anticipation.’

‘Which is mostly anticipation and it is the entire job.’ Nkechi was definite. ‘A watchkeeper reacting to things is a watchkeeper who has already failed.’

‘And holding maintenance history.’

‘And holding maintenance history alongside that is a division of attention I cannot afford at the moment I most need not to divide it.’ She shrugged. ‘Which sounds dramatic and describes an ordinary Tuesday.’

‘So the record holds the history.’

‘The record holds the history and I hold the situation, which is the only sensible division of labour between a person and a document.’ Nkechi was matter of fact. ‘And I was doing it wrong for about six years without noticing.’

Meera made a point about the framing.

‘I want to address something people say about this.’

‘Say it.’

‘That it is laziness, or that it degrades memory, or that a professional should hold their material.’ She was even about it. ‘Which I have heard from colleagues and which I understand.’

‘And your answer?’

‘That the question is what somebody uses their attention for rather than whether they use it.’ Meera was definite. ‘I have not reduced my cognitive load. I have redirected it toward the things a document cannot do.’

‘Which is a different claim.’

‘Which is an entirely different claim and it is testable in my own practice.’ She counted. ‘I notice more in sessions than I did. I make connections between what somebody is saying now and how they are saying it, which is the thing I am actually trained for.’


## Searching as a way of thinking

‘There is a use I did not anticipate and I want to describe it,’ Meera said.

‘Go on.’

‘Search stopped being retrieval and started being analysis.’

‘Say what you mean.’

‘I used to search when I knew what I wanted.’ She was precise. ‘Now I search when I have a question about my own practice.’

‘Give me a case.’

‘I had a sense that a particular theme was recurring across several people this year.’ Meera counted. ‘Which is exactly the kind of impression that is either a genuine pattern or an artefact of what I happen to have thought about recently.’

‘And you checked.’

‘I searched the theme and looked at how many records contained it and when.’ She was matter of fact. ‘And it was real, and it was concentrated in a particular period, and it corresponded to something that had happened locally.’

‘Which you would not have established.’

‘Which I could not have established by recall, because recall is what produced the impression in the first place and cannot verify it.’ Meera was definite. ‘An impression checked against itself is not a check.’

‘So the search is an outside view.’

‘The search is the only outside view available on my own practice, and it is the thing I would least want to give up.’

Nkechi described hers.

‘Mine is condition rather than theme.’

‘Say it.’

‘I search a category of deficiency across several voyages.’ She counted. ‘Which produces a set of findings over time rather than a single occasion.’

‘And what do you see?’

‘Whether something is recurring, which is a completely different fact from something having occurred.’ Nkechi was precise. ‘One finding is a repair. The same finding three times is a system that is not working.’

‘Which nobody sees.’

‘Which nobody sees because the three occasions are separated by months and involve different people, and each one is dealt with correctly in isolation.’ She shrugged. ‘The pattern exists only in the aggregate and the aggregate exists only if somebody assembles it.’

‘Has it produced anything?’

‘Twice, and one of them changed a maintenance recommendation that went ashore.’ Nkechi was matter of fact. ‘Which is the difference between reporting three repairs and reporting a problem.’

‘Say what makes it possible.’

‘That the search reaches inside the documents rather than only the entries.’ She was definite. ‘Because the specifics of a finding are in an attached report, not in what I wrote about it.’

‘Which you have not read.’

‘Which I have read once, at the time, and not since, and which I search regularly.’ Nkechi laughed. ‘I have perhaps ninety of those and I have reread perhaps four.’

‘Which is the correct relationship.’

‘Which is the only possible relationship with ninety technical documents, and it works because reading and searching are different activities and I only need one of them.’


## The version Nkechi cannot use

‘There is a difference between us that I want to name,’ Nkechi said, ‘because I have been agreeing with everything and one part does not apply to me.’

‘Say it.’

‘Your material is about people and mine is about a vessel, and that changes who the record is for.’

‘Say what you mean.’

‘Yours is for you.’ Nkechi was precise. ‘You read it before a session, you write it afterward, and nobody else opens it.’

‘Which is true and is a legal position as much as a preference.’

‘Whereas mine may be read by somebody who has never met me.’ She counted. ‘An officer relieving me at short notice. An investigator after an incident. A superintendent ashore.’

‘Which changes what a good record is.’

‘Which changes it completely, because a record only I can interpret is a record that fails at the moment it matters most.’ Nkechi was definite. ‘And the moment it matters most is generally a moment when I am not there.’

‘So your structure is for a stranger.’

‘My structure is for a stranger and yours is for you, and we have both arrived at fixed sections for entirely different reasons.’ She shrugged. ‘Yours are fixed so you can check quickly. Mine are fixed so somebody who has never seen one knows where to look.’

Meera considered that.

‘Which means you cannot use shorthand.’

‘Which means I cannot use anything I would have to explain, and I write more than you do as a result.’ Nkechi was matter of fact. ‘My entries are perhaps twice the length of what I would write for myself.’

‘Which is a cost.’

‘Which is a real cost and it is the correct one to pay, and it took me a year to accept it.’ She was frank. ‘Because for the first year I was writing for myself and it was faster and it would have been useless to anybody else.’

‘Say what changed.’

‘I read an entry from eleven months earlier and could not immediately reconstruct what I had meant by one line.’ Nkechi shrugged. ‘Which is a stranger reading it, essentially. The stranger was me.’

‘Which is the argument everybody eventually encounters.’

‘Which is the argument I would make to anybody, because the person who cannot read your record in a year is you, and they are considerably more likely to encounter it than any investigator.’


## Why they trust it

‘Now the question underneath all of this,’ Nkechi said. ‘Which is why either of us trusts a piece of software with something we used to hold ourselves.’

‘Say what you would need before doing it.’

‘Three things and I would not compromise on any of them.’ She counted. ‘It has to work when I need it. It has to be right. And it has to be mine.’

‘Say the first.’

‘I am at sea for months with no connection to anything.’ Nkechi was matter of fact. ‘Which means anything requiring one is not a memory extension. It is a memory extension available in port.’

‘Which is not the same thing.’

‘Which is not remotely the same thing, because the moments I need it are on a deck with an inspector, or on a bridge at three in the morning, and those are exactly the moments when nothing is available.’ She shrugged. ‘A thing that works most of the time cannot replace something that works all of the time.’

‘So availability is prior.’

‘Availability is prior to everything and it is why I could not have done this with anything else I have used.’

‘Say the second.’

‘That the record is right, which comes from structure rather than from software.’ Nkechi was precise. ‘My entries have fixed sections because I put them there. The information is where I expect it because I have been consistent.’

‘So accuracy is your contribution.’

‘Accuracy is mine and reliability is the tool's, and both have to hold.’ She was definite. ‘A perfectly reliable system holding badly structured records is not a memory. It is a pile that never breaks.’

Meera said her third condition was the one she cared about most.

‘Mine is that it is mine.’

‘Say what that requires.’

‘That the material is on my machine, in a folder I control, protected by something only I can open.’ Meera counted. ‘And that nothing about it goes anywhere.’

‘Say why that is a condition of trust rather than a preference.’

‘Because I am externalising four hundred people's confidences.’ She was serious. ‘Which is not my material to place somewhere I do not control.’

‘So the offloading has an ethical component.’

‘The offloading has an ethical component that is prior to the practical one.’ Meera was definite. ‘I could not have made this decision if the material were somewhere a company held, regardless of how convenient it was, because the decision was not mine alone to make.’

‘Which is a stronger condition than reliability.’

‘Which is a different kind of condition entirely, and both had to be satisfied, and the second one is the reason I did not do this five years earlier.’

Nkechi added something.

‘There is a fourth thing and it is quieter.’

‘Say it.’

‘That it has never failed.’ She was matter of fact. ‘Which is not a design property. It is a record.’

‘Say what you mean.’

‘Four contracts. Something like fourteen months at sea across them. It has opened every time.’ Nkechi shrugged. ‘Which is the thing that actually produced the trust rather than any argument about architecture.’

‘Which is how trust works.’

‘Which is exactly how trust works and I would not have been persuaded by anything else.’ She was definite. ‘Somebody could have explained the design to me for an hour and I would still have kept a second record for six months, which I did.’

‘You kept one.’

‘I kept a parallel paper log for the first contract, which is about four months, and I stopped when I noticed I had not consulted it.’ Nkechi laughed. ‘Which is the only honest way anybody arrives at this.’


## What neither of them offloads

‘I want to be careful about the boundary,’ Meera said, ‘because everything we have said could be read as suggesting a person becomes unnecessary.’

‘Say what stays.’

‘The relationship, which is not a record and cannot be.’ She was precise. ‘I know what it feels like to be in a room with a particular person. Whether they are more guarded than usual. Whether something has shifted.’

‘Which is not written anywhere.’

‘Which is not written anywhere and could not be usefully.’ Meera was definite. ‘I could write that somebody seemed withdrawn, and I do, and reading it back tells me almost nothing compared to being in the room.’

‘So the record holds facts.’

‘The record holds facts and I hold the person, and the reason I can hold the person properly is that I am not also holding the facts.’ She shrugged. ‘Which is the whole argument in one sentence and it took me two years to arrive at.’

Nkechi said hers was physical.

‘Mine is the ship,’ she said. ‘Which sounds mystical and is not.’

‘Say it.’

‘I know what this vessel sounds like when it is right.’ She was matter of fact. ‘Which is not a piece of information. It is something my body knows after fourteen months on it.’

‘Which does not go in a record.’

‘Which does not go anywhere, and it is the thing that makes me notice a problem before there is any evidence of one.’ Nkechi counted. ‘A vibration that is not quite right. A note in an engine that has changed. A motion that is fractionally different in the same sea.’

‘Which nobody can write down.’

‘Which I have tried to write down for junior officers and produced nothing useful.’ She shrugged. ‘Because what I would be writing is that something feels wrong, and the useful part is the feeling rather than the sentence.’

‘So the boundary is between what can be recorded and what can be sensed.’

‘Which is roughly where it falls and I would put it slightly differently.’ Nkechi was thinking. ‘Between what has already happened and what is happening.’

‘Say that properly.’

‘Everything I have externalised is a record of something that occurred.’ She counted. ‘Inspections, drills, findings, arrangements. All past.’

‘And what stays is present.’

‘What stays is my attention on what is happening now, which is the only thing that can respond to what has not happened yet.’ Nkechi was definite. ‘Which is not a poetic distinction. It is a description of what a watchkeeper is for.’

Meera sat with that.

‘That is a better formulation than mine and it applies exactly.’

‘Say yours in those terms.’

‘The record holds what has happened with a person. My attention holds what is happening with them.’ She nodded slowly. ‘And the second is where the clinical work occurs, and it requires everything I have.’


## The thing Meera is uneasy about

‘There is something I want to raise that I have not resolved,’ Meera said.

‘Say it.’

‘I do not know what has happened to my unaided recall.’

Nkechi did not answer immediately.

‘Say what you mean.’

‘Whether it has declined because I stopped using it, or whether it is unchanged and I simply no longer test it.’ Meera was frank. ‘And I cannot distinguish those two from inside.’

‘Does it matter?’

‘It matters if I am ever without the record, which for me is nearly never and is not never.’ She counted. ‘A telephone call at ten at night about a patient. A conversation in a corridor at a conference.’

‘Where you have nothing.’

‘Where I have nothing and I am being asked something, and what I have is whatever I actually retained.’ Meera shrugged. ‘Which is less than it was and which I have chosen.’

‘Say how you handle it.’

‘I say that I would rather check.’ She was definite. ‘Which is a slightly uncomfortable sentence and is more honest than the confident answer I would have given fifteen years ago.’

‘Was the confident answer right?’

‘Sometimes.’ Meera was even about it. ‘Which is the point. I was confident at a consistent rate and correct at a variable one, and nobody including me could tell which occasion was which.’

Nkechi thought about it.

‘Mine has the same shape and I have made peace with it differently.’

‘Say how.’

‘By deciding that saying I will check is a professional answer rather than an admission.’ She was matter of fact. ‘Which took some adjusting because on a ship there is a culture of knowing.’

‘Say what that means.’

‘An officer who says they will check something can sound like an officer who does not know their vessel.’ Nkechi shrugged. ‘Which is a real perception and it is wrong, and I have stopped worrying about it.’

‘What changed?’

‘Watching somebody senior state something confidently about a piece of equipment that turned out to be four years out of date.’ She was definite. ‘He was not careless. He was recalling accurately from the last time he had looked, which was a long time ago.’

‘Which is exactly the failure.’

‘Which is precisely the failure and everybody in the room believed him, including me, and it took a document to correct it.’ Nkechi paused. ‘Which is when I decided that checking is not a weakness.’


## What Meera does not write down

‘There is a category I want to describe because I do not think we have covered it,’ Meera said.

‘Say it.’

‘Things I have decided not to record.’

Nkechi sat up slightly.

‘Which is different from things that cannot be recorded.’

‘Which is entirely different and it is a decision rather than a limitation.’ Meera was precise. ‘There are things people tell me that I do not put in a record at all.’

‘Say the kind.’

‘Something disclosed in a moment that is not clinically relevant and that concerns somebody else.’ She counted. ‘A patient tells me something about their sister. It is not about their treatment. It is context they offered because they were talking.’

‘And you leave it out.’

‘I leave it out, deliberately, because a record of it serves no clinical purpose and it is a permanent artefact about a person who is not my patient and has not consented to anything.’ Meera was definite. ‘Which is a judgement I make several times a week.’

‘Do you ever regret it?’

‘Occasionally, in the sense that something turns out to have been relevant and I have to ask again.’ She shrugged. ‘Which costs a minute and is the right trade.’

Nkechi was thinking.

‘I have the same category and I had not identified it as one.’

‘Say yours.’

‘Something a crew member tells me about another crew member.’ She was matter of fact. ‘Which happens constantly on a ship, because a ship is a small place and people talk.’

‘And it does not go in.’

‘It does not go in unless it concerns safety, and the line is clear enough that I have never agonised over it.’ Nkechi counted. ‘Somebody being unhappy with a rota is not a record. Somebody being too tired to stand a watch is.’

‘So the test is whether it affects the thing the record is for.’

‘Which is the same test in both our professions with a different subject.’ She was definite. ‘And I want to say plainly that this is the part where externalising has a limit that is nothing to do with capability.’

‘Say it properly.’

‘A record can hold anything. Whether it should is a separate question and it is entirely ours.’ Nkechi shrugged. ‘Which is the one part of this that no tool can help with and no tool should.’


## Two people who stopped remembering

Every system for holding information passes through a threshold nobody announces. Below it, the system helps somebody find what they already know exists. Above it, they have stopped attempting to hold the material and search instead. The crossing changes what a professional does with their attention, and both practitioners here made it deliberately after years of not knowing they had a choice.

The case for making it rests on two properties of human recall that are difficult to notice from inside. The first is that maintaining an internal model is a continuous occupancy rather than an occasional effort, which means it does not present as work and is therefore never accounted for. One practitioner describes fifteen years of holding clinical detail for four hundred people and describes having attributed her end-of-day tiredness entirely to emotional weight, which turned out to be partly the accounting.

The second is that recall is reconstructive, which produces a specific failure that both identified independently in entirely different professions. A person's sense of something is formed by the last occasion they thought about it and presents as current knowledge rather than as a memory of that occasion. One describes a senior colleague stating something confidently about a piece of equipment that was four years out of date, having recalled accurately from the last time he looked, with everybody in the room believing him.

The related observation is about what memory selects for. One practitioner puts it precisely: unaided recollection preserves what was memorable, and memorable is nearly unrelated to significant. A striking remark in a session persists for years while a medication change three weeks later, which is what actually matters, does not. Her profession requires selecting for significance and her memory selects for vividness, which is an argument she describes taking a decade to state.

What the offloading buys is attention, and both describe the recovery in terms specific to their work. A clinician sitting with somebody describing something difficult needs complete attention on what is being said, what is not being said, and how, which is more than a full occupation. A watchkeeper needs everything on anticipation rather than reaction, because an officer responding to events has already failed. In both cases any capacity committed to holding a catalogue is capacity unavailable where it is actually required.

Search turned out to serve a second function neither had anticipated, which is analysis rather than retrieval. A clinician noticing an apparent theme across her practice can verify it against the record, which is the only outside view available on her own impressions, since an impression checked against itself is not a check. An officer searching a category of finding across several voyages sees whether something is recurring, which is a different fact from something having occurred, and one such discovery changed a maintenance recommendation.

The conditions that make any of this possible were stated firmly and are worth separating. Availability comes first and is absolute: a memory extension that requires a connection is a memory extension available in port, which is precisely not when it is needed on a deck or a bridge at three in the morning. Accuracy is the user's contribution rather than the tool's, and comes from consistent structure, since a perfectly reliable system holding badly organised records is a pile that never breaks. And for anybody externalising other people's confidences, the material must be somewhere they control, which one practitioner describes as an ethical condition prior to the practical one and as the reason she did not do this years earlier.

Underneath the reasoning sits something less rational and more decisive, which is a record of not failing. One practitioner describes fourteen months at sea across four contracts with the thing opening every time, and describes that rather than any argument about design as what produced her trust. She kept a parallel paper log for four months and stopped when she noticed she had not consulted it, which both describe as the only honest route to relying on anything.

The boundary matters as much as the offloading. Neither externalises the part that requires being present. One holds the relationship, meaning what it is like to be in a room with a particular person and whether something has shifted, which she can write down and which reads back as almost nothing compared to being there. The other holds the vessel, meaning what it sounds like when it is right, which she has tried to write for junior officers and describes as producing nothing useful because the sentence is not the useful part.

The formulation they arrived at is worth keeping. Everything externalised is a record of what has already happened. What remains is attention on what is happening, which is the only thing capable of responding to what has not happened yet. The record holds the past and the person holds the present, and the reason either practitioner can hold the present properly is that they have stopped also holding the past.


## FAQs

### What is the threshold this discussion describes?

The point at which somebody stops trying to remember material and starts searching for it instead. Below it, a system helps a person find something they already know exists.
