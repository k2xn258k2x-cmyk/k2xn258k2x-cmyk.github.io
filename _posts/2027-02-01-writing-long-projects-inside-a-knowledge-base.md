A book-length project is not a long document. It is a different kind of object, and the difference becomes apparent somewhere around the twenty-thousandth word, when the writer discovers that they can no longer hold the whole of it in mind. Up to that point a piece of writing is something a person knows. After it, the writing becomes something a person navigates, and the tools that served the first condition begin failing at the second.

The failure is specific rather than general. Composition remains straightforward: sentences are sentences at any length. What degrades is everything surrounding composition. Finding the passage where something was already said. Knowing what a section contained before it was rewritten. Recovering the reasoning behind a decision made four months earlier. Locating the source that supported an assertion. None of those is writing, and all of them consume the time nominally allocated to writing.

![Writing Long Projects Inside a Knowledge Base](/assets/img/feature-screenshot-placeholder.svg)

The conventional arrangement makes this worse by separating the draft from everything supporting it. Research accumulates in one place, documents in another, and the manuscript in a third. Each is individually reasonable and the combination means that a question arising mid-sentence requires leaving the sentence, entering a different environment, and returning with whatever was found and with the sentence considerably colder than it was.
<!--more-->


The frequency is what makes this expensive rather than the individual cost. A person writing seriously for two hours will want something from outside the draft perhaps a dozen times: a date, a name, a phrase somebody used, the precise wording of a document. Each retrieval is a minute or two of mechanical work and a considerably larger interruption to whatever state the writing required. Multiply by several hundred sessions and the cost is a substantial fraction of the project.

Composing inside the material removes the boundary rather than reducing the friction. The research, the supporting documents, the earlier drafts, and the manuscript occupy one searchable space, which means a question arising mid-sentence is answered without leaving the sentence. That changes something about the writing itself, since a writer who can verify a detail in four seconds will verify it, and a writer facing a two-minute excursion will frequently write around it instead.

Two further properties matter at book length and are absent from most writing environments. The first is a retained record of what a passage said before it was changed, which makes bold revision possible: a writer who can recover an earlier version will attempt something drastic, and one who cannot will edit cautiously. The second is retrieval across the whole project, since a long work always contains something the author knows they wrote and cannot locate, and that hunt is one of the more demoralising activities in extended writing.

What follows is a conversation between two people writing book-length projects of entirely different kinds, one assembled from a life and one from an investigation, who have solved the same structural problems in ways that turn out to be almost opposite.


## Two books that are not the same shape

Lena had proposed it after a remark Felix made in the group.

‘You said something about being at forty thousand words and having lost the shape of it,’ she said. ‘Which I recognised, and I have never spoken to anybody who is doing this the same way I am.’

‘Say what you are doing.’

‘A book from an investigation that has been running for two years.’ She was matter of fact. ‘Which will be perhaps sixty thousand words and which currently exists as about thirty thousand across several places.’

‘And the material behind it?’

‘Considerably more than the book. Several hundred documents, eleven source compartments, and about four hundred archived pages.’ Lena counted. ‘Which is the whole difficulty. The book is a small object sitting on top of a large one.’

‘Mine is the reverse,’ Felix said. ‘My material is forty years of my own notes and about two thousand photographs, and the book is the point of all of it.’

‘Say your length.’

‘Perhaps ninety thousand when it is done, and I am at forty-four.’ He was rueful. ‘Which took three years and which is a rate that would horrify a professional.’

‘It would not horrify me. Say your structure.’

‘Chronological, which sounds obvious and which was a decision.’ Felix shared. ‘A page for the whole book, then a page for each decade, then chapters underneath.’

‘Why was it a decision?’

‘Because a memoir does not have to be chronological and mine nearly was not.’ He was thoughtful. ‘I spent two months trying to organise by theme, which is more interesting, and I could not hold it. Every chapter belonged to three themes.’

‘Whereas a decade contains a chapter unambiguously.’

‘Whereas a year is a fact and a theme is an argument, and I could not build a structure out of arguments while I was still working out what the arguments were.’

Lena made a noise.

‘Mine is the opposite and for the opposite reason,’ she said.

‘Say it.’

‘My book is organised by thread rather than by time, because the chronology is not the story.’ She counted. ‘Four threads. The financial trail, how the governance failed, what the regulator did not do, and the people.’

‘And events appear in several threads.’

‘The same month appears in three threads and means something different in each, which is why chronology is useless to me as a structure.’ Lena was definite. ‘A reader needs the financial trail explained as a sequence and the governance failure explained as a sequence, and those two sequences overlap in time and not in logic.’

‘So you have four books.’

‘I have four threads that will be interleaved at the end, which is the hardest part and which I have deferred.’ She was frank. ‘And the deferral is deliberate. I cannot decide the interleaving until each thread is coherent by itself.’


## What is underneath a chapter

‘Show me what a chapter actually is,’ Lena said.

‘One entry, with four sections, and the four are the same every time.’ Felix shared. ‘The draft. The research behind it. Photographs. And revision notes.’

‘Say the fourth.’

‘Which is the one I would not have predicted and which I use most.’ He was matter of fact. ‘Notes to myself about the chapter rather than about its subject. What is not working. What I have tried. What my reader said.’

‘Why not in the draft?’

‘Because I did that for a year and it produces a draft full of instructions to myself in a different colour, which I then have to remove.’ Felix shrugged. ‘And I would occasionally miss one, which is embarrassing.’

‘So the separation is mechanical rather than aesthetic.’

‘The separation means the draft section contains only the book, which means I can read it as a reader would, which is the only way to tell whether a chapter works.’

Lena said hers had a different composition.

‘Mine is not chapters,’ she said. ‘Mine is passages.’

‘Say the distinction.’

‘A chapter is a unit of a book. A passage is a unit of argument.’ She was precise. ‘I have about sixty passages, each of which establishes one thing, and a chapter will eventually be four or five of them.’

‘Which is smaller than my units.’

‘Considerably smaller, and it is because my passages have to be reordered and yours do not.’ Lena counted. ‘Your chapter about a particular decade will be in the same position in the finished book. My passage about a particular transaction might land in any of three chapters depending on how I interleave the threads.’

‘So granularity follows from whether the order is settled.’

‘Which I had not stated and which is exactly right,’ she said. ‘Anything whose position is fixed can be large. Anything that might move should be small enough to move.’

‘What is inside a passage?’

‘The narrative, and a section listing what supports it.’ Lena described it. ‘Not the evidence itself, which is separate. A list of which entries hold the evidence.’

‘Why not the evidence in the passage?’

‘Because one document supports four passages and I am not keeping four copies of it.’ She was definite. ‘The evidence lives once, in the thread it belongs to, and the passages point at it.’

‘Which is a pointer rather than a copy.’

‘Which is the only arrangement that survives a document turning out to mean something different from what I thought.’ Lena was serious. ‘That has happened twice. I re-read something and my understanding changed, and I needed to find every passage resting on it.’

‘And you could.’

‘I could search for the document and find the four passages naming it in about six seconds, and two of them needed rewriting.’ She shrugged. ‘With copies, I would have found the ones I remembered.’


## Being able to be wrong

‘I want to talk about the record of previous versions,’ Felix said, ‘because it has changed how I write rather than how I recover.’

‘Say that properly.’

‘I attempt things I would not otherwise attempt.’ He was definite. ‘There is a chapter about a decision I made on a hiring committee, which I have written three times in three completely different voices.’

‘Three complete rewrites.’

‘Three, and the first two were wrong in ways I could not see until I had written them.’ Felix counted. ‘The first was defensive. The second was so self-critical it was a different kind of defensive. The third is honest and it took the other two to find it.’

‘And you could not have done that.’

‘I could not have done that if each attempt destroyed the previous one.’ He was emphatic. ‘I would have edited cautiously, in place, and produced something that was the first version with the worst parts removed.’

‘Which is not the same as a rewrite.’

‘Which is a completely different activity and produces a completely different chapter, and every writer knows this and very few can act on it.’

‘Because acting on it requires being able to be wrong.’

‘Because acting on it requires knowing that being wrong costs nothing, and that knowledge has to be structural rather than a resolution.’ Felix shrugged. ‘I am not a brave writer. I am a writer who knows the earlier version is retained.’

Lena said hers was different in purpose.

‘I do not rewrite in that way,’ she said. ‘My passages accrete rather than being replaced.’

‘Then what do you use it for?’

‘Tracking my own understanding, which is a peculiar use and which I would defend.’ She was precise. ‘A passage from four months ago is written by somebody who did not know what I know now.’

‘Which is obvious.’

‘Which is obvious and is not visible unless you look.’ Lena was definite. ‘I compare deliberately, perhaps once a month, on the passages that matter. And what I am looking for is whether my position moved because evidence arrived or because I became convinced.’

‘Which is a considerable distinction.’

‘Which is the distinction, in my work, and it is very difficult to assess from inside.’ She was serious. ‘I cannot tell whether I am more certain because I have more evidence or because I have been living with a hypothesis for two years.’

‘And the earlier version helps.’

‘The earlier version is somebody else's opinion, essentially, written by a person with the same materials and less exposure.’ Lena shrugged. ‘If my language has hardened and the evidence has not changed, that is a finding about me.’

‘Has it happened?’

‘Once, substantially, and I softened a passage back toward where it had been.’ She was matter of fact. ‘Which is not a comfortable thing to discover and is exactly what the comparison exists for.’

Felix asked about the retention window.

‘Does the period ever constrain you?’

‘Not for my purposes, because my comparisons are within a few months.’ Lena thought. ‘And for anything longer I do something else, which is to keep a superseded version as its own entry when the change is genuinely significant.’

‘Which I also do and had not mentioned.’

‘Say your case.’

‘The two abandoned versions of that chapter are entries.’ Felix was matter of fact. ‘Not history. Entries, in their own right, in a section of the chapter, because they are documents about my own thinking and I may write about them.’

‘Which is the memoirist's advantage.’

‘Which is the memoirist's peculiar situation, where the process is potentially part of the subject.’


## Asking a question in the middle of a sentence

‘Now the thing I would miss most,’ Lena said.

‘Searching while writing.’

‘Searching while writing, which is not the same activity as searching, and I want to describe why.’

‘Say it.’

‘When I search deliberately, I have stopped working and started looking for something.’ She was precise. ‘When I search mid-sentence, I have not stopped. The sentence is still open and I am filling a gap in it.’

‘Which requires speed.’

‘Which requires about four seconds, and beyond perhaps fifteen the sentence is gone and I am doing something else.’ Lena counted. ‘Which is the actual threshold and I think most writers know it and do not name it.’

‘Give me a case.’

‘I am writing a paragraph about a transaction and I need the exact wording of a clause.’ She described it. ‘I know it exists, I know roughly what it says, and I need the words because I am going to quote them.’

‘And previously?’

‘Previously I would write a marker in the text saying to check it, and continue.’ She shrugged. ‘Which is a reasonable technique and which produces a draft with forty markers, and the checking session is four hours of tedium in which I make mistakes.’

‘Whereas now.’

‘Whereas now I type three words, get the document, take the clause, and the paragraph is finished and correct.’ Lena was definite. ‘And the four-hour session does not exist, which is not a time saving so much as the removal of an activity I dreaded.’

Felix said his use was less precise and more frequent.

‘Mine is a detail from thirty or forty years ago,’ he said. ‘Which I half remember and which I need to be right about.’

‘Say a case.’

‘I was writing about a conference and I could not remember which year a particular person spoke.’ He shrugged. ‘Which matters, because I am about to describe a conversation and the reader will assume I know when it happened.’

‘And you found it.’

‘In a note I made at the time, which I had scanned two years ago and had entirely forgotten, and the search found it because the words were extracted from the scan.’ Felix was slightly amazed by it still. ‘I did not know that note existed. I searched a name and a place and there it was.’

‘Which is the property of searching across everything rather than across the draft.’

‘Which is the property, and it is the whole argument for writing here rather than somewhere else.’ He was definite. ‘If my draft lived in a separate application, that search would have found nothing, because the note is not in the draft.’

‘And the frustration it removes.’

‘The specific frustration of knowing you have written something down somewhere,’ Felix said. ‘Which is a sensation every person doing long work knows and which is now simply absent from my life.’


## Being handed something you did not choose

‘There is a habit of mine that Lena will find frivolous,’ Felix said.

‘Say it before you decide that.’

‘I begin every session by reading whatever the random selection hands me.’

Lena did not react as he expected.

‘I do a version of that and I would not have called it the same thing,’ she said.

‘Then say yours and I will say mine.’

‘Mine surfaces an evidence entry from a thread I am not currently working in.’ Lena was matter of fact. ‘Which perhaps one time in twelve is relevant to what I am doing and I had not made the connection.’

‘Which is a research use.’

‘Which is entirely a research use and it has produced one genuine finding, which is a good return for four seconds a day.’

‘Say the finding.’

‘Something from the regulatory thread that belonged in the financial one, which I had filed correctly and had therefore stopped seeing.’ She shrugged. ‘Correct filing makes things invisible, which is a problem nobody mentions.’

‘Mine is generative rather than investigative,’ Felix said.

‘Say what that means.’

‘It hands me a note from forty years of notes and I read it and something happens or it does not.’ He counted. ‘Most mornings nothing happens and I ask for another. Perhaps twice a week something takes hold.’

‘Give me an example.’

‘A note about a letter from a colleague, four lines, about nothing.’ He was thoughtful. ‘And I sat with it and wrote eleven hundred words about what friendship between colleagues actually consists of, which is now a passage in a chapter.’

‘Which would not have happened.’

‘Which could not have happened, because there is no route from my current thinking to that note.’ Felix was definite. ‘My current thinking does not know it exists. That is the whole mechanism and it is why I keep doing it.’

‘Say why it works.’

‘Because a memoir assembled from what I already consider important is a book that says what I believed before I started.’ He shrugged. ‘Which is the failure mode of the entire form and almost nobody escapes it.’

Lena was quiet for a moment.

‘I have the same failure mode with a different name,’ she said.

‘Say it.’

‘An investigation assembled from what I already suspect is an investigation that confirms what I suspected.’ She was serious. ‘Which is the failure mode of my form, and it is the same mechanism, and I had not connected them.’

‘So we are both using randomness against ourselves.’

‘We are both using something that does not know what we think, deliberately, because the parts of the process that do know what we think are the parts that produce the error.’


## The month Felix could not write

‘I want to describe a period where none of this helped,’ Felix said, ‘because we have been describing an apparatus and an apparatus does not write anything.’

‘Say it.’

‘Six weeks last spring where I opened the file every morning and produced nothing.’

Lena did not offer any sympathy, which he said afterward he appreciated.

‘Say what you did during the six weeks.’

‘Everything except writing.’ He counted. ‘I reorganised two decades. I scanned four boxes. I marked up three chapters with colours. I revised my outline twice.’

‘All of which is real work.’

‘All of which is real work and none of which was the book, and I knew that at the time and did it anyway.’ Felix was frank. ‘Because the chapter I was avoiding was still there and everything else was easier.’

‘Which chapter?’

‘The one about the hiring committee, which we have discussed and which I have now written three times.’ He shrugged. ‘The three versions came after the six weeks. The six weeks were the price of getting to them.’

‘Did anything end it?’

‘My wife asked me on a Sunday how the book was going and I said fine, and then I sat in the kitchen and thought about the fact that I had just lied to her about something that does not matter.’ Felix was matter of fact. ‘Which is a stupid mechanism and it is what happened.’

Lena was quiet for a moment.

‘I have four months rather than six weeks,’ she said.

‘Say yours.’

‘A period where I was archiving, and cross-referencing, and building a chronology of one thread, and producing no narrative at all.’ She was precise. ‘And I told myself it was foundation work, and about half of it was.’

‘And the other half?’

‘The other half was that writing narrative requires committing to what I think, and I did not want to commit, because committing means being wrong in a way that is visible.’ Lena shrugged. ‘Evidence gathering does not require a position. Narrative does.’

‘Which is the same avoidance in a different form.’

‘Which is precisely the same thing and I would not have said so before this conversation.’ She was thoughtful. ‘You avoided a chapter because it was emotionally difficult. I avoided narrative because it was epistemically exposing. Both of us organised instead.’

‘Organising is the universal avoidance.’

‘Organising is the universal avoidance and any tool that makes organising satisfying is a tool that offers a very comfortable place to hide.’ Lena was definite. ‘Which is worth saying plainly, because everything else in this conversation makes organising easier.’

‘Say how you would guard against it.’

‘I have one rule and it is crude.’ She counted. ‘Words before anything else. Whatever I do in a session, the first thing is narrative, however bad, and organising happens afterward or does not happen.’

‘Which is a sequencing solution to a psychological problem.’

‘Which is the only kind that works on me, because I cannot decide to stop avoiding something. I can decide what to do first.’


## Putting it in order

‘Now the practical problem,’ Lena said. ‘Which I suspect you have solved better than I have.’

‘Order.’

‘Order, which a book has and a collection of entries does not.’

‘Mine is a naming convention and it is completely unsophisticated.’ Felix showed it. ‘Every chapter begins with a chapter number, two digits, and then the title.’

‘So sorting by title gives you the book.’

‘Sorting by title gives me the book in order, which is all I need, and I resisted it for a year because it looked crude.’ He laughed. ‘And then I did it and the problem disappeared.’

‘Why two digits?’

‘Because I have more than nine chapters and I learned that lesson a very long time ago.’ Felix was matter of fact. ‘Which is the kind of thing everybody learns once.’

‘Do you ever renumber?’

‘I have renumbered twice, both times when I moved a chapter, and it took about eleven minutes each time.’ He shrugged. ‘Which is an argument for leaving gaps and I have not bothered, because eleven minutes twice in three years is not a problem.’

Lena said hers could not work that way.

‘My order is not settled and will not be until the end,’ she said.

‘So a number would be a lie.’

‘A number would be a claim I cannot support, and I would spend my time renumbering rather than writing.’ She was definite. ‘So mine is a document rather than a convention.’

‘Say it.’

‘One entry at the top of each thread, starred so it is always reachable, containing the intended sequence.’ Lena described it. ‘A list of passages in the order I currently believe they should appear, with a line about what each establishes.’

‘Which is an outline.’

‘Which is an outline that changes, and the changing is the point.’ She was matter of fact. ‘I revise that entry perhaps twice a month, and its history is a record of how I have thought about the shape.’

‘So the order lives in one place rather than in the names.’

‘The order lives in one place, deliberately, because it is the thing most likely to change and I want changing it to be one edit rather than sixty renames.’

Felix considered it.

‘Yours is better for a project whose order is unresolved,’ he said.

‘And yours is better for one whose order is fixed, and I would not use mine if I had yours.’ Lena was definite. ‘An outline entry that never changes is a document you stop reading. A naming convention on a settled order is free.’

‘So the question is whether the sequence is a decision you have made.’

‘Which is a good way to put it, and the answer determines which arrangement is correct rather than which is better.’


## The part Lena has to do that Felix does not

‘There is an asymmetry between us that I want to name,’ Lena said.

‘Say it.’

‘About a third of my material is protected and none of yours is.’

‘Which is true and I had not thought about what it costs you.’

‘It costs me one specific thing and I want to be precise about it.’ She was matter of fact. ‘Protected material is not searchable while protected, which is the point of protecting it.’

‘So your source material is outside the search.’

‘My source compartments are outside the search, deliberately, and that is correct.’ Lena was definite. ‘What it means is that I cannot do the thing Felix does. I cannot type three words mid-sentence and have a source's account appear.’

‘Then what do you do?’

‘I work in two modes and I keep them separate.’ She counted. ‘When I am writing narrative that draws on a source, I open that compartment deliberately, at the start, knowing what I am doing.’

‘Rather than stumbling into it.’

‘Rather than stumbling into it, which is a discipline and which I would keep even if the arrangement permitted otherwise.’ Lena was serious. ‘I do not want to be searching casually across the accounts of eleven people who trusted me. That is not a technical constraint I am working around. It is a professional posture that the arrangement happens to enforce.’

‘Which is the same thing Meera said about clinical records.’

‘Which I read and recognised immediately, and it is the same principle in a different profession.’ She shrugged. ‘Some material should require a deliberate act to reach.’

Felix asked how it affects the writing.

‘Does it slow you down?’

‘It changes the shape of a session rather than slowing it.’ Lena described it. ‘A source-based passage is a planned session. I open what I need, I write, and I close it. Perhaps two hours.’

‘And the other kind?’

‘Everything resting on documents rather than people, which is most of the book, and that is fluid and searchable and exactly like yours.’

‘So you have two writing modes.’

‘Two modes, which I did not design and which I now think is correct.’ She was thoughtful. ‘The passages resting on people are the ones I should be writing deliberately, with the compartment open in front of me, rather than reaching for a fragment mid-sentence.’

‘Because a person's account deserves the whole thing.’

‘Because a person's account deserves to be read as an account rather than mined for a phrase, and I have caught myself doing the second when it was easy to.’


## What neither of them uses

‘I want to be honest about something,’ Felix said, ‘because we have described a considerable apparatus and somebody will assume we use all of it.’

‘Say what you do not.’

‘I have almost no categories on my chapters.’ He was frank. ‘Which for a project of this size is probably wrong and I have never needed them.’

‘Say why not.’

‘Because the structure does the work. A chapter is in a decade, and the decade is the question I ask.’ Felix shrugged. ‘I have never wanted to know which chapters concern a particular theme, which is exactly the thing categories would answer.’

‘Do you think that is a gap?’

‘I think it is a gap that will appear at the end, when I am checking whether a theme is developed evenly, and I will regret not having marked them as I went.’ He was matter of fact. ‘Which is a prediction rather than a complaint.’

‘Then mark them now.’

‘I am going to, this month, on forty-four chapters, which will take an afternoon and which is considerably better than doing it on ninety.’

Lena said hers was the opposite.

‘I use categories heavily and I barely use the structure,’ she said.

‘Say that.’

‘My four threads are pages and I almost never navigate by them.’ She was slightly rueful. ‘I search, or I filter by category, and the pages are where things live rather than how I reach them.’

‘So the structure is storage.’

‘The structure is storage and the categories are retrieval, and I built the structure carefully and use it least.’ Lena shrugged. ‘Which I only noticed by describing it to you.’

‘Is that a problem?’

‘I do not think so. Something has to determine where a thing lives, and that decision has to be simple enough to make quickly.’ She was definite. ‘The structure is doing a real job and it is not the job I thought I was building it for.’

Felix laughed.

‘Which is the third time in this conversation one of us has discovered something about our own arrangement.’

‘Which is what happens when you describe a practice out loud, and it is the reason I proposed this call.’


## What happens at the end

‘There is something I have been avoiding asking,’ Felix said. ‘Which is what happens when this becomes a book.’

‘Say what you mean.’

‘A publisher wants a manuscript. One document, in order, in a form an editor can work in.’ He counted. ‘And I have forty-four entries in a structure.’

‘So the question is the handover.’

‘The question is the handover and I have been quietly worried about it for two years.’

‘I would put your worry to one side and I will tell you why.’ Lena was matter of fact. ‘Because the handover is an afternoon and the writing is three years.’

‘Say more.’

‘Chapters in order, copied into a single document, in whatever form somebody wants.’ She counted. ‘Which is mechanical. It is tedious and it requires no judgment and it happens once.’

‘Whereas the alternative.’

‘Whereas the alternative is three years of writing inside a format chosen to suit a person who will read it for six weeks at the end.’ Lena was definite. ‘Which is what most writers do and it is a strange trade when stated plainly.’

Felix laughed.

‘That is a considerably better framing than the one I had.’

‘Say yours.’

‘That I would eventually have to convert everything and that the conversion was a debt accumulating.’ He shrugged. ‘Whereas you are describing it as a single afternoon at the end of a long project.’

‘Which is what it is, and I would add something.’ Lena was thoughtful. ‘The document you hand over is not the book. It is a copy of the book for somebody else to work on.’

‘And the book stays here.’

‘The book stays here, with its research, its earlier versions, its photographs and its abandoned chapters.’ She was definite. ‘Which matters for me because an editor's queries will require going back to sources, and it matters for you for a different reason.’

‘Say my reason.’

‘Because a memoir is finished when it is published and the material behind it is not.’ Lena paused. ‘You will be answering questions about that book for years and the questions will be from your family.’

Felix did not respond immediately.

‘That is true and I had not thought about it in those terms,’ he said. ‘The manuscript goes to a publisher. The archive goes to my daughter.’


## Writing inside the material

A book-length project stops being a document somewhere around the twenty-thousandth word, at the point where the writer can no longer hold the whole of it in mind. Composition is unaffected, since sentences are sentences at any length. What degrades is everything around composition: locating what has already been said, recovering what a passage contained before it changed, and finding the source behind an assertion.

The conventional arrangement compounds that by separating the draft from its support. A question arising mid-sentence requires leaving the sentence, entering another environment, and returning with the sentence considerably colder. One practitioner named the threshold precisely at around fifteen seconds, beyond which the sentence is gone and something else is happening. A dozen such interruptions in a two-hour session, multiplied across several hundred sessions, is a substantial fraction of a project.

The two structures described here are almost opposite and both follow from the same question, which is whether the sequence is settled. A memoir organised chronologically has fixed positions, which permits large units: one entry per chapter, with the same four sections every time, one of which holds notes about the chapter rather than about its subject so that the draft contains only the book. An investigation organised by thread has no settled sequence until the threads are interleaved at the end, which forces smaller units: sixty passages, each establishing one thing, small enough to move.

That produced the clearest general rule in the conversation. Anything whose position is fixed can be large; anything that might move should be small enough to move. The same logic determines how order is expressed. A settled sequence can live in a naming convention, which is free and which one practitioner resisted for a year because it looked crude before discovering that it dissolved the problem entirely. An unsettled sequence must live in one revisable outline, because a number applied to sixty passages is a claim nobody can support and renumbering becomes the activity.

Evidence is referenced rather than copied, for a reason that emerged from experience rather than principle. A document supporting four passages exists once and is pointed at, so that when a re-reading changes its meaning, every dependent passage can be found in seconds. With copies, somebody finds the ones they remember, which is reliably an incomplete set.

The retained record of earlier versions turned out to serve two entirely different purposes. For the memoirist it makes bold revision possible: a chapter written three times in three voices, where the first two were wrong in ways only visible after writing them, and where the alternative would have been cautious editing producing the first version with its worst parts removed. He is candid that this is structural rather than temperamental, describing himself not as a brave writer but as one who knows the earlier version is retained.

For the journalist it serves as an outside view of her own certainty. A passage from months earlier was written by somebody with the same materials and less exposure, and comparing them answers a question she cannot answer from inside: whether her position moved because evidence arrived or because she has been living with a hypothesis for two years. It has happened once, and she softened a passage back toward where it had been.

Searching while writing is a different activity from searching, which both identified independently. It happens with the sentence still open, requires seconds rather than minutes, and replaces a technique both had used previously: writing a marker to check something later, producing a draft with dozens of markers and a subsequent session of tedium in which errors are made. One describes finding a detail in a note he had scanned years earlier and entirely forgotten, discovered because the words in the scan were extracted and reachable.

Both use randomness deliberately, and both identified the same reason underneath entirely different applications. A memoir assembled from what somebody already considers important says what they believed before starting. An investigation assembled from what somebody already suspects confirms the suspicion. Both are the characteristic failure of their form, and both are addressed by regularly encountering something chosen by a mechanism that does not know what the writer thinks.

The asymmetry between them concerns protected material. Around a third of one practitioner's sources are unreachable by search while protected, which she describes not as a constraint to work around but as a posture the arrangement enforces. Source-based passages become planned sessions with a compartment deliberately opened, rather than fragments retrieved mid-sentence, on the grounds that a person's account deserves to be read as an account rather than mined for a phrase.

The final observation was that both had built something they use differently than intended. One has almost no categories and relies entirely on structure. The other built four careful thread pages and navigates almost exclusively by search and category, with the structure serving as storage rather than retrieval. Neither considers this a failure, and both discovered it by describing their practice out loud, which is the third time in one conversation that articulation produced a finding.


## FAQs

### When does a long project stop behaving like a document?

Somewhere around the point where the writer can no longer hold the whole of it in mind, which practitioners place in the region of twenty thousand words. Composition is unaffected, since sentences are sentences at any length. ### How does somebody decide the boundary of a writing unit?

By whether the parts are read together and whether they need to move independently. Practitioners with fixed sequence use chapters, since a chapter is read as a unit and stays where it is. Practitioners with unsettled sequence use passages establishing one thing each, since those must be reorderable. The same test that governs any splitting decision applies here with an additional constraint about movement. The additional constraint about movement is what distinguishes this from the ordinary splitting decision and is the part people most often overlook. Practitioners describe both adjustments as unremarkable, which is worth knowing for anybody hesitating over the initial decision. Choosing the right one for the project, rather than adopting whichever is more familiar, is what determines whether it stays useful.

### What is the single property everything else depends on?

That the research and the draft occupy one searchable space. Practitioners describe every other benefit as following from it: a question answered without leaving a sentence, material surfacing that the writer had forgotten, evidence findable when its meaning changes, and the absence of the specific frustration of knowing something was written down somewhere. Two practitioners writing entirely different books identify the same foundation. One works from forty years of his own notes and photographs toward a memoir; the other works from several hundred documents and eleven source compartments toward an investigation. The material could hardly be less alike and the requirement is identical, which suggests it belongs to long work rather than to any particular kind of it.
