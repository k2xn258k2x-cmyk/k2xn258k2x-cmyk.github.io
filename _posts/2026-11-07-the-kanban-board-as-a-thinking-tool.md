A list and a board answer two different questions, and most people who have used both have never articulated the difference. A list answers what exists. It enumerates the things that need doing, in whatever order somebody imposed on them, and its usefulness is entirely a function of completeness. A board answers where everything stands. It does not primarily tell you what remains; it tells you the distribution of your work across the stages it passes through, which is a question a list structurally cannot address no matter how carefully it is maintained.

That distinction sounds academic until somebody notices what it does to their attention. A person looking at a list of forty items is performing a scan and a mental sort: which of these is urgent, which is blocked, which has been sitting here for a month without my registering it. That work happens invisibly, costs real effort, and produces an answer that decays within hours. A person looking at a board is performing recognition. Six things in one column and one in the next is a shape, and shapes are processed by machinery that requires no deliberation at all. The information was present in the list. It was simply not visible, and the difference between present and visible is most of what visual management provides.

![The Kanban Board as a Thinking Tool](/assets/img/feature-screenshot-placeholder.svg)

The method originated in manufacturing, where the problem was concrete and expensive. Work in progress accumulates at whichever step is slowest, and a factory that cannot see the accumulation will keep feeding material into the front of a process that cannot absorb it. The cards on a board made that accumulation physically visible, so the bottleneck announced itself rather than having to be deduced from output figures weeks later. That is the entire idea, and it turned out to generalise far beyond factories because every profession with sequential work has the same failure mode.
<!--more-->


What made the technique spread was not the columns but the constraint they impose on thinking. A board forces every item to occupy exactly one stage, which means somebody has to decide what stage a thing is in, and that decision is frequently the useful part. A great deal of work drifts in an unexamined middle state where it is neither finished nor actively progressing, and it can remain there indefinitely on a list because a list has no way of representing drift. Requiring a card to sit somewhere makes the drift explicit, and once it is explicit it tends to be addressed.

The usual objection is maintenance, and it is a fair one. A board that has to be updated separately from the actual work becomes a second system, and second systems decay quickly because keeping them accurate produces no immediate return. Anybody who has watched a team abandon a project tracker within two months has seen this happen. The board becomes wrong, being wrong makes it useless, and being useless removes the incentive to correct it, which is a stable and fairly common equilibrium.

The interesting design question is therefore not what a board displays but where it derives its state from. A board that is a separate artifact requires discipline nobody sustains. A board that is a view of material somebody is already maintaining requires none, because updating the work updates the board as a side effect. That distinction determines whether visual management is a technique that survives contact with an actual working week or a diagram somebody made once during a planning session.

What follows is a conversation between two people who arrived at the same view of this from opposite ends of a spectrum. One of them makes documentaries and has been running a board through three productions. The other prosecutes patents and started using one recently after hearing him mention it, having previously been certain that visual project management was for people whose work was more physical than hers.


## Two screens, one of them containing a great deal of red

Raj answered the call from an edit suite, which was visible behind him as a wall of equipment and one very large monitor showing a paused frame of somebody's hands.

‘I have eleven minutes of a rough cut and I hate all of it,’ he said, by way of greeting.

‘That is not a professional opening,’ Claire said.

‘It is an accurate one. I will hate it less on Thursday. This is a stage rather than an assessment.’ He turned away from the monitor. ‘You said you had adapted the board and I have been genuinely curious since your message.’

‘I have adapted it and I want to be clear about how sceptical I was,’ Claire said. ‘When you mentioned it on that group call I made a note to look at it out of politeness rather than interest. My assumption was that it was for work that has physical stages. Things that move. Film goes through processes; a legal argument does not.’

‘And now?’

‘And now I have eleven weeks of it and I would defend it in front of the partners, which is a considerably stronger position than politeness.’ She adjusted something off camera. ‘Show me yours first. I want to see the version built by somebody who has been doing it for three productions rather than three months.’

‘Mine is chaotic and it is chaotic on purpose,’ Raj said. ‘I want to say that before you see it, because you are a person with a tidy desk and I have watched you flinch at things.’

‘I have never flinched at anything in my life.’

‘You flinched at my file naming on the last call.’

‘That was not a flinch. That was a professional reaction.’

He shared his screen.


## What a documentary looks like as a board

The board filled the panel. Seven columns across, each with a header, each holding a stack of cards.

‘Research. Scheduled. Filmed. Transcribed. Rough cut. Reviewed. Final.’ Raj read them left to right. ‘Every column is a hashtag. I did not create the columns. I wrote the hashtags into the entries and the columns exist because the hashtags exist.’

‘Say that again, because I want to be sure I understand the mechanism.’

‘The board reads my notes.’ He said it plainly. ‘Every card on this board is an entry. Each entry carries a hashtag somewhere in it. The board collects the hashtags it finds and makes a column for each one, and puts the entry in the column matching its tag.’

‘So the board is not a separate thing you maintain.’

‘The board is a view of the vault. There is nothing here that I have entered twice.’ He clicked into a card and an entry opened, and there in the body among the prose was the hashtag. ‘This is a scene. It is currently transcribed. If I change that word in the note, the card is in a different column.’

‘Show me.’

He did, editing the tag in the body of the entry and returning to the board, where the card had moved.

Claire was quiet for a second.

‘That is the part I underestimated,’ she said. ‘I had assumed a board would require me to remember to move things.’

‘Which is why every project tracker I have ever been given at a production company was abandoned by week three,’ Raj said. ‘Somebody sets it up, everybody agrees it is excellent, and then the actual work happens somewhere else and the board becomes a picture of what was true a fortnight ago. Being wrong is worse than not existing, because a wrong board gets consulted.’

He walked her through what the cards actually were.

‘One card per unit of material. A scene, an interview, a segment. For this film there are about ninety of them, which sounds like a lot and is roughly what a feature produces.’ He scrolled. ‘Not shooting days. I have entries for shooting days as well and those are not on this board, because a day is a container and a scene is a thing that moves through stages.’

‘How do you keep those separate?’

‘The day entries have no stage hashtag on them, so they simply do not appear here.’ He shrugged. ‘Which is the elegant part. The board only shows things that have declared a stage. Everything else in the vault is invisible to it and unaffected by it.’

Claire asked about the colours, which had been visible since the board loaded and which she had been waiting to ask about.

‘Labels,’ Raj said. ‘Which are a completely separate axis from the hashtags and which took me two productions to work out.’

‘Explain the axis.’

‘The hashtag says where a thing is. The label says what kind of thing it is.’ He pointed. ‘Interview. B roll. Archival. Reconstruction. Four labels, colour coded, and every card carries one.’

‘So a column can contain several colours.’

‘Every column contains several colours and that is the entire value.’ He leaned in. ‘Look at Transcribed. Six cards. Five of them are interviews and one is archival, which tells me instantly that my transcription bottleneck is a language problem rather than a volume problem, because the interviews are in three languages and the archival material is already in text.’

‘You read that from the colours before you read any of the titles.’

‘I read that from the colours before I read anything at all, which is the point.’ He was definite. ‘A list would have told me six items are transcribed. It would not have told me what kind of six.’

He described the daily use, which was the part he said he would have found hardest to believe two years earlier.

‘Every morning at nine, four of us stand in front of this for six minutes. The whole crew. Not a meeting.’ He counted it. ‘We look at what has moved since yesterday, what has not moved in three days, and what is sitting in a column with nothing behind it.’

‘Six minutes.’

‘Six minutes, and it replaced a forty minute production meeting that produced a list of things everybody already knew.’ Raj was emphatic. ‘Because the board is doing the reporting. Nobody has to say what state anything is in. It is on the wall.’

‘Does anybody else update it?’

‘Everybody updates it, and they do it by editing the note.’ He shrugged. ‘My assistant producer writes up an interview and changes a word in the entry. She is not maintaining a board. She is doing her job, and the board is downstream of her job.’

Claire made a note; he could hear the pen.

‘That is the sentence I am going to use with the partners,’ she said.


## What a patent portfolio looks like as a board

Claire shared, and Raj made a noise of genuine surprise.

‘That is more colours than mine.’

‘That is fourteen clients and I will come to that,’ she said. ‘Columns first.’

Seven columns, headed by hashtags: disclosure, drafting, filed, office action, response due, amended, granted.

‘Every card is an application,’ she said. ‘Not a matter, not a client. One patent application, which is the thing that moves.’

‘How many?’

‘Eighty-one active. Which is a normal load and which is more than anybody can hold in their head, and I want to be honest that I have been pretending otherwise for about fifteen years.’

Raj laughed.

‘Say why the stages work for legal.’

‘Because patent prosecution is genuinely state based, and I do not think I properly understood that until I saw it as a board.’ She was precise. ‘Every application is in exactly one stage at any moment. It is being drafted, or it is filed and waiting, or an examiner has rejected it and we owe a response, or we have responded and are waiting again, or it is allowed. There is no ambiguity and no overlap. It is not like a project with parallel workstreams.’

‘So the shape was always there.’

‘The shape was always there and it existed in my head, in my docketing system as dates, and in a spreadsheet that our practice manager maintains and which is accurate and which nobody looks at.’ She paused. ‘What I did not have was the distribution. I knew where each individual application was. I had no picture of where they all were.’

‘And the picture changed something.’

‘The picture changed something on the first morning and I want to describe it precisely, because I did not expect to be affected by a diagram.’ Claire sat back slightly. ‘Response due had eleven cards in it. Eleven. Which I would have told you was somewhere between six and fifteen if you had asked me, and being able to say between six and fifteen is not knowing.’

‘What did eleven mean?’

‘It meant my associate and I were going to have a difficult month and neither of us had noticed it forming.’ She said it evenly. ‘Because they arrived one at a time, across seven weeks, each one a manageable individual event, and nothing anywhere put them in the same place until that board did.’

‘That is exactly the bottleneck problem.’

‘It is precisely the bottleneck problem and I had assumed my field was immune to it because we work to dates rather than to flow.’ Claire shook her head. ‘Which is wrong. We work to dates and the work still has a shape, and the dates are how the shape becomes visible too late.’

She walked through the mechanics, which she had adapted more than Raj had expected.

‘The hashtag lives in the entry for the application, in a section I call Status, which contains the current stage and one line about what happened last.’ She showed it. ‘Which means the tag is never orphaned. Anybody reading that section sees the stage and the reason for it in the same place.’

‘Why a section rather than in the body?’

‘Because my entries are long.’ She scrolled to demonstrate: an application entry with sections for the claims as filed, the prosecution history, the prior art, the client correspondence, and the status. ‘A hashtag buried in eleven thousand words is a hashtag nobody can find when they need to change it. A section called Status, always in the same position, is a place.’

Raj was writing.

‘I am taking that,’ he said. ‘Mine are in the body and I have twice edited the wrong line.’

The labels came next.

‘Client,’ Claire said. ‘Which is the second dimension, exactly as yours is, and which was not my first choice.’

‘What was?’

‘Technology area, which felt more meaningful and turned out to be useless.’ She was matter of fact. ‘Because I never ask a question shaped like a technology area. Every question I am asked is shaped like a client. When a general counsel calls, they want to know about their portfolio, and if the board is coloured by client I can answer while they are still speaking.’

‘How long did it take to work that out?’

‘About nine days, and changing it was trivial, which is the only reason I was willing to experiment.’ She paused. ‘That is worth saying to anybody starting. The columns are expensive to change because the tags are in the entries. The labels are cheap. So put the thing you are confident about in the columns and the thing you are guessing about in the labels.’

‘That is a better rule than mine,’ Raj said.

‘What is yours?’

‘I did not have one. I had three productions of trial and error, which is a rule of a kind.’


## Why the board changes what you are thinking about

‘I want to try to say what this actually does,’ Raj said, ‘because I have been trying to explain it to my producer for a month and I keep producing something that sounds like enthusiasm rather than an argument.’

‘Try it on me. I will be unkind.’

‘When I see six cards in transcribed and one in rough cut, I know where my bottleneck is without thinking about it.’ He said it slowly. ‘That is the whole thing. Not that I can work it out. That there is no working out.’

‘Define the difference.’

‘Working it out means holding the material in my head, counting, comparing, and reaching a conclusion, which takes about forty seconds and which I will therefore only do when I already suspect something is wrong.’ He spread his hands. ‘Recognising it takes no time and happens whether or not I was looking for it. Which means I find out about bottlenecks I had no reason to suspect.’

Claire considered this.

‘Mine is the same and the consequence is different,’ she said. ‘When I see three applications in response due with dates next week, I know my team's priority for the week without asking anybody anything. Including without asking myself.’

‘Why does the without asking matter?’

‘Because asking has a cost and the cost is not the time.’ She was thinking as she spoke. ‘When I have to construct a priority, I construct it under whatever conditions I happen to be in. Tired, or having just come off a difficult call, or on a Monday. And I will construct a defensible priority that is subtly shaped by my mood, and I will not know that it was.’

‘Whereas a shape is a shape.’

‘Whereas the board does not care what kind of morning I am having. Three cards is three cards.’ She paused. ‘I have made worse decisions than I realised, over fifteen years, purely because the input to the decision was a mental reconstruction rather than a picture.’

‘That is a considerably darker version of my point.’

‘It is your point with a career attached to it.’

They spent a while on what lists conceal, which was Raj's contribution and which he had clearly thought about.

‘A list can tell you everything except where things are stuck,’ he said. ‘It shows what needs doing. It does not show that something has needed doing for five weeks while everything around it moved.’

‘A list has no memory.’

‘A list has no memory and no geometry. Every item is the same distance from every other item.’ He gestured. ‘On a board, position is meaning. A card that has not moved is in the same place it was last week and I can see that it is, because I remember the shape.’

‘Do you actually remember the shape?’

‘I remember the shape better than I remember any list I have ever read.’ He shrugged. ‘Which is not a claim about me. Everybody does. It is what the equipment is for.’


## The argument about verbs and nouns

‘Practical section,’ Claire said. ‘Because I noticed something in your columns and I want to know whether it was deliberate.’

‘Say what you noticed.’

‘Your stages are verbs. Past tense verbs. Filmed. Transcribed. Reviewed.’ She read them off. ‘Mine are nouns. Disclosure. Drafting. Office action. And I did that without thinking about it.’

‘Mine was deliberate and it took me a production to arrive at,’ Raj said. ‘Because the first version was nouns and it did not work.’

‘Why not?’

‘Because a noun describes a thing and a verb describes something that happened to it.’ He was definite. ‘My first board had a column called Transcription, and a card sitting in Transcription is ambiguous. Is it being transcribed, or has it been? Somebody has to know the convention. Whereas a card in Transcribed has unambiguously had that done to it.’

‘So the past tense is doing work.’

‘The past tense means every column reads as a completed state, and the card sits in the last thing that happened to it rather than the next thing that will.’ He shrugged. ‘Which matters when four people are looking at the same board at nine in the morning and two of them are half awake.’

Claire thought about that.

‘Mine cannot use verbs and I want to work out whether that is a real constraint or an assumption,’ she said.

‘Try it.’

‘Filed works. That is a past tense verb and it is one of my columns.’ She counted. ‘Drafted would work, although I have Drafting because the drafting takes weeks and the card sits there while it is happening. Office action does not work at all. Nothing happened to the application. Something arrived from outside it.’

‘Rejected?’

‘Would be wrong. An office action is not necessarily a rejection and calling it one would embarrass me in front of a client who saw the board.’ She paused. ‘Which is a real consideration. My board is occasionally visible to somebody other than me, and the vocabulary has to be the vocabulary of the field.’

‘Then that is the actual rule,’ Raj said. ‘Not verbs against nouns. Use whatever your profession already calls the stage.’

‘Which is what I did by instinct.’

‘Which is what everybody does by instinct and which is right, and my verbs are also right because film production genuinely talks that way.’ He grinned. ‘Nobody on a crew says the transcription phase. They say has it been transcribed.’

The count came next, and they had moved in opposite directions.

‘I started with ten and reduced to seven,’ Raj said.

‘I started with five and added two.’

‘What did you drop?’

‘I had a stage between filmed and transcribed for material that had come back from the card but not been logged, and one between rough cut and reviewed for a version that had gone to my producer but not returned.’ He counted. ‘Both were real stages. Both were accurate. And both were places where cards sat for less than two days, which meant the columns were almost always empty and the board looked wrong.’

‘Empty columns are noise.’

‘Empty columns are noise and they make the board harder to read, which is the only thing a board is for.’ He was emphatic. ‘A stage that nothing ever sits in is not a stage. It is a moment.’

‘Mine was the reverse,’ Claire said. ‘I started with drafting, filed, office action, response, granted. Five. And within a month I had split response into response due and amended, because those are genuinely different states and conflating them hid something.’

‘What did it hide?’

‘Whether the work had been done.’ She said it flatly. ‘An application where a response is owed and an application where the response has been filed and we are waiting are both in the middle of the same cycle, and one of them requires my attention this week and the other requires nothing at all. Putting them in one column meant looking at a number that was half urgent and half irrelevant.’

‘So the test is whether the two states require different action.’

‘That is exactly the test and I would put it above any question of granularity.’ She nodded. ‘Not how precise do you want to be. Do these two situations produce a different next step. If they do, they are different columns. If they do not, they are one column with a longer name.’

‘I am going to write that down,’ Raj said, and evidently did.

They compared naming mechanics briefly.

‘All lower case, hyphens rather than spaces, no abbreviations,’ Claire said. ‘Because the tag is text in a document and text in a document gets typed by tired people.’

‘Same, and I keep them short for the same reason.’ Raj added, ‘And I wrote mine down on paper before this production. Seven words on a card taped inside the flight case. Which sounds ridiculous and which has meant zero variants across four months.’

‘I have mine as an entry in the vault.’

‘Which you can search and I cannot.’

‘Which I can search, and which is starred so it is in the sidebar every morning, and which took me four seconds to make.’


## The board is not the only thing looking at the vault

‘Here is the part I did not anticipate,’ Raj said, ‘which is that the board is only one of several things reading the same material, and using them together is where it stops being a tracker.’

‘Give me an example.’

‘Cards that lie.’ He said it cheerfully. ‘A card sits in Scheduled. Which means we intend to film it. And I know, because I was there, that we filmed it eleven days ago, and nobody changed the tag because the person who would have changed it was carrying a tripod up four flights of stairs at the time.’

‘How do you catch it?’

‘I search.’ He demonstrated as he spoke, typing a phrase into the question search. ‘I look for entries containing material that only exists after filming. Timecodes. A card number. The word we use for a particular kind of coverage. If something turns up with that language and is still tagged as scheduled, the tag is wrong.’

‘So the search is auditing the board.’

‘The search is auditing the board and it takes about four minutes on a Friday.’ He shrugged. ‘Which is the only maintenance this whole arrangement requires from me, and it is not really maintenance. It is finding out where the tag has fallen behind the reality.’

‘Mine is the reverse direction,’ Claire said, ‘and I use filters rather than search.’

‘Explain.’

‘The board is everything. Eighty-one applications is a lot of cards, and there are mornings where I do not want to look at eighty-one anything.’ She was navigating as she spoke. ‘So I narrow first. A date range, which cuts to what has moved recently. Or a file type, which is a proxy I use for whether a matter has recent correspondence.’

‘And then the board reflects the narrowing.’

‘The board shows what is in scope, so a filtered view produces a filtered board.’ She paused. ‘Which is how I answer a client question quickly. Their label plus a date range, and I am looking at a board containing only their portfolio and only the last quarter, and I can describe the state of it accurately in about eight seconds.’

‘That is a genuinely different use from mine.’

‘It is the same feature answering a different question, which I think is the point of this whole conversation.’

They came back to labels, which both had described as a second dimension and neither had explained fully.

‘I want to press on something,’ Claire said. ‘You colour by content type and I colour by client. Both of us chose a dimension that does not change.’

‘Say more.’

‘An interview is always an interview. A client is always that client.’ She was working it out. ‘Neither of those ever moves. Which means the label is stable and the column is what changes, and the board is legible because only one of the two dimensions is in motion.’

Raj sat up.

‘I tried to colour by urgency once.’

‘And?’

‘And it was unreadable within a fortnight, because urgency changes and stage changes, and a board where both dimensions move is a board with no fixed points.’ He was visibly working out why. ‘I could not tell whether something had changed colour because it had become urgent or because I had reassessed it, and I stopped trusting the colours entirely.’

‘Then that is a rule,’ Claire said. ‘The column moves and the label does not. Anything that changes on its own belongs in the column or nowhere.’

Sections came up last, at Raj's prompting.

‘Say how you use sections in a card entry, because mine are thin and yours are not.’

‘Every application entry has the same five,’ Claire said. ‘Claims as filed. Prosecution history, which is the sequence of what happened. Prior art. Client correspondence. Status, which holds the tag.’

‘And that structure is identical across all eighty-one.’

‘Identical, without exception, including where a section is empty.’ She was emphatic. ‘Because an empty prior art section on an application is information. It says nobody has done that analysis, which is a fact I want visible rather than absent.’

‘Mine are looser and I am beginning to think that is laziness rather than a philosophy,’ Raj said. ‘A scene entry has whatever it happens to have.’

‘What would the five be if you imposed them?’

He thought about it.

‘What we intended. What we got. Technical notes, which is coverage and audio and anything that will matter in the edit. Who is in it, which matters for releases. And where it sits in the film, which changes constantly and which is currently in my head.’

‘That last one is a section you badly need.’

‘That last one is a section I badly need and I have been carrying it around for three productions,’ Raj said. ‘I am doing that tonight.’


## Planning around a shape

‘I want to tell you the thing that actually changed,’ Raj said, ‘because everything so far has been about seeing the work and this is about doing it differently.’

‘Go.’

‘I plan shoots around the board now.’ He paused. ‘Which sounds like nothing and is a reversal of how I have worked for eleven years.’

‘Describe the old way.’

‘The old way is that you schedule by availability and access. This location is available in March. This subject is in the country for nine days. The permit clears on a Tuesday.’ He counted it off. ‘All of which is real and none of which I can ignore. And within that, you shoot as much as you can as fast as you can, because shooting is expensive and everything else is not.’

‘And now?’

‘And now I look at what will happen downstream of a week of shooting.’ He turned toward the board. ‘If I film nine interviews in a fortnight, I have created nine cards that will move into transcribed at a rate of about two a week, because that is what my transcription capacity actually is. Which means seven weeks of a growing pile and a producer asking me why nothing is in the rough cut.’

‘So you stagger.’

‘I stagger, deliberately, and I have started scheduling two lighter weeks between heavy ones specifically so the downstream can catch up.’ He shrugged. ‘Which costs me nothing, because the constraint was never the shooting. And I could not have seen it before, because the pile formed slowly and by the time it was obvious it was eight weeks old.’

‘That is capacity planning.’

‘It is capacity planning and I would have told you six months ago that documentary production is too chaotic for it.’ He laughed. ‘It is not too chaotic. It was too invisible.’

Claire was nodding.

‘Mine is a change in how I prioritise and it is subtler,’ she said. ‘Prosecution is deadline driven. Everybody knows this. You work to the dates and the dates are absolute.’

‘And now?’

‘And now I prioritise by stage as well as by date, and where those disagree I have started paying attention to the disagreement.’ She said it carefully. ‘An application in drafting with a deadline in six weeks and an application in response due with a deadline in five weeks are not, it turns out, five weeks and six weeks of work sitting in the same queue.’

‘Why not?’

‘Because the response is a discrete task with a known shape. I know what a response costs me. Drafting is open ended and it expands, and it is the one that will quietly consume a fortnight I had allocated to something else.’ She paused. ‘Sorting purely by date puts the response first and the drafting second, and that is wrong about half the time.’

‘So you look at the column.’

‘I look at the column and I ask what advances this, which is a different question from what is due.’ She was definite. ‘Some cards advance with two hours of concentrated work. Some advance with an email to a client that takes four minutes and then two weeks of waiting. The four minute one should happen today regardless of its date, because it starts a clock I do not control.’

Raj made a sound of recognition.

‘That is my equivalent of sending three tapes off to transcription before a weekend.’

‘Precisely that. The action that costs almost nothing and unblocks something else.’ Claire nodded. ‘I did those things before, occasionally, when I noticed. Now I look for them deliberately, because the board shows me what is waiting and what is waiting on me.’

‘And that is a strategy rather than a status report.’

‘That is a strategy, and I would not have described a project board as a strategic instrument eleven weeks ago,’ Claire said. ‘I would have described it as something a project manager puts on a wall so that other people feel informed.’


## The board that failed

‘I want to describe the one that did not work,’ Raj said, ‘because everything I have shown you is the third attempt and the first two are more instructive.’

‘Go.’

‘The first board I made had a column for every person on the crew.’ He said it with the flatness of a man who has explained a bad idea before. ‘Which sounds sensible. Who is doing what. And it was completely useless within about nine days.’

‘Why?’

‘Because a person is not a stage.’ He spread his hands. ‘A card sitting under my sound recordist tells me he is responsible for it. It tells me nothing about whether the thing is finished, blocked, waiting on somebody else, or three weeks stale. And people work on several things at different stages simultaneously, so the columns filled up unevenly for reasons that had nothing to do with the work.’

‘So it displayed ownership rather than state.’

‘It displayed ownership, which I already knew, in place of state, which I did not.’ He shrugged. ‘Which is the general failure. Any board that shows you something you already know is a board you will stop opening.’

‘What was the second?’

‘The second was correct in structure and wrong in scope.’ Raj scrolled through something off screen. ‘I put everything on it. Scenes, equipment hire, travel, permits, invoices, the composer, the grade. Every piece of work associated with the film, which felt thorough.’

‘I can see where this goes.’

‘About two hundred and thirty cards across a board built for material that moves through a production pipeline, and most of those things do not move through a production pipeline at all.’ He laughed. ‘A permit is granted or it is not. It does not get transcribed.’

‘So they sat in columns that meant nothing for them.’

‘They sat wherever I could justify putting them and they made the columns lie.’ He was definite. ‘Nine cards in Scheduled looks like nine scenes waiting to be shot. Four of them were invoices I had tagged as scheduled because I had to tag them as something.’

‘What did you learn from it?’

‘That the board should hold one kind of thing that passes through one sequence of stages, and that everything else in the production belongs in the vault and not on the board.’ He said it as a rule. ‘Which is not a limitation. It is the definition of what a board is for.’

Claire had gone quiet in the way that meant she was checking her own arrangement against his.

‘Mine has one kind of thing on it,’ she said. ‘Applications. And I had a moment in week two where I nearly added trade mark matters, which run on a completely different sequence, and I decided against it because the columns did not fit.’

‘That instinct was correct.’

‘That instinct was correct and I could not have explained it at the time.’ She paused. ‘What I would have said is that it felt wrong. What I would now say is that a board is a claim that everything on it moves through the same stages, and a second kind of matter is a second claim.’

‘Do you have a second board?’

‘I have a second board for trade marks with four columns, and they are entirely separate, and separating them is the reason both of them are legible.’

‘I have a second board for post-production,’ Raj said. ‘Grade, sound mix, titles, delivery. Four columns, eleven cards, and it exists precisely because those things do not belong in the same sequence as a scene.’

‘Then that is the correction to my earlier point,’ Claire said. ‘Not fewer stages. Fewer kinds of thing per board.’


## What happens when other people can see it

‘Different question,’ Claire said, ‘and I want your experience because you have a crew and I have a firm and I suspect the dynamics are opposite.’

‘Ask it.’

‘What happened when other people started looking at your board?’

‘Two things and one of them I did not expect at all.’ Raj thought. ‘The first is obvious. Everybody stopped asking me for status. That was about eighty percent of what people asked me, in every production I have ever run, and it disappeared in a week.’

‘And the second?’

‘People started moving cards without being asked.’ He said it with visible pleasure. ‘Not literally, because they change the tag in the note, but the effect is the same. My assistant producer finished a transcript on a Sunday and updated the entry, and I found out on Monday morning by looking at a shape rather than by receiving a message.’

‘Which means the board is also a communication channel.’

‘Which means the board is a considerably better communication channel than the one it replaced, because nobody has to decide whether something is worth telling me.’ He leaned forward. ‘That is the part I would emphasise. People withhold small updates because reporting them feels like noise. A tag change is not a report. It is just the truth being current.’

Claire nodded slowly.

‘Mine has been slower and I want to be honest about why,’ she said. ‘Kevin, my associate, took to it immediately, which I expected. He is thirty-one and has never resisted anything in his life.’

‘And the partners?’

‘The partners have not seen it and I am being deliberate about when they do.’ She was matter of fact. ‘Because a board showing eleven applications in response due is a picture of a workload, and a picture of a workload in front of a management committee becomes a picture of a resourcing decision, and I would like to choose the moment for that conversation rather than have it arrive during a discussion about something else.’

‘That is not paranoia.’

‘It is not paranoia, it is thirty years of watching how a number behaves once it enters a room.’ She said it without heat. ‘I will show them, and when I do I will show them alongside what I want to happen as a result. The board is genuinely useful and it is also evidence, and evidence has a context.’

Raj was thinking.

‘I do not have that problem and I want to work out why,’ he said.

‘Because your crew and you want the same outcome.’

‘Because my crew and I want the same outcome, and a firm has a management structure with an interest in what my workload means.’ He nodded. ‘Which is not a criticism of firms. It is a real difference.’

‘There is a version for you,’ Claire said. ‘Would you show that board to a financier?’

Raj laughed immediately.

‘Absolutely not.’

‘Why not? It is accurate.’

‘Because it is accurate about the middle of a process, and a financier reads the middle of a process as trouble.’ He was cheerful about it. ‘Six cards stuck in transcription is a completely normal week and it looks like a project falling apart if you have never made one.’

‘Then we have the same rule and different rooms,’ Claire said. ‘The board is for the people doing the work. It is not a reporting instrument for people who are not.’

‘I would add one thing,’ Raj said. ‘It is not that they should not see it. It is that a shape needs somebody who knows what normal looks like, and if you do not have that, the shape misleads you rather than informing you.’


## Reading each other's boards

‘Last thing and it is an experiment,’ Claire said. ‘Put yours back up and let me tell you what I see, and then do the same to me. Neither of us knows the other's field, which is the point.’

‘This is going to be uncomfortable.’

‘It is going to be useful.’

Raj shared again. Claire looked at it for perhaps twenty seconds without speaking, which he found considerably longer than twenty seconds.

‘Two things,’ she said. ‘One is a question and one is an observation.’

‘Question first.’

‘Why is Research empty?’

Raj opened his mouth and then did not say anything.

‘I ask because every other column has cards and that one has none, and on my board an empty first column would mean I have stopped bringing in new work.’

‘Mine means the same thing,’ he said slowly. ‘Which I had not registered. I have been reading the board from the middle outward because the middle is where the pressure is, and I have genuinely not looked at the left-hand edge in about three weeks.’

‘Is it a problem?’

‘It is not a problem this month and it is a problem in about five, because a documentary in the edit is a documentary that has stopped developing, and there is always something that should be researched for the next one.’ He rubbed his face. ‘That is the second time on this call that a shape has told me something and I want to note that this one required somebody else's eyes.’

‘What is the observation?’ he asked.

‘Your colour distribution is uneven in a way that might be deliberate.’ She pointed. ‘Almost everything on the left is one colour and almost everything on the right is another. Interviews early and b-roll late, if I am reading the key correctly.’

‘You are reading the key correctly and that is entirely accidental.’

‘Then it is probably a scheduling artifact rather than a plan,’ Claire said. ‘Which is not my business to interpret. I am only pointing out that the pattern exists.’

‘It is a scheduling artifact and it is one I should have chosen rather than inherited,’ Raj said. ‘We shot the interviews in a block because of availability and the b-roll afterward, and the consequence is that my edit has a spine and no texture until quite late.’

He looked at it for a moment.

‘Your turn,’ he said, ‘and I am going to be worse at this than you were.’

Claire shared.

‘Everything is on the right,’ Raj said, almost immediately.

‘Say more.’

‘Your first three columns have, what, eleven cards between them. And the last four have seventy.’ He counted. ‘On my board that would mean I had almost nothing coming in and a very large amount in progress, which is what a production looks like at the end.’

‘It means roughly the same thing,’ Claire said, ‘and it is the correct state for my practice in this quarter, and I want to explain why because it is a good illustration of context.’

‘Go on.’

‘New filings cluster. Clients file in bursts around their own product cycles and their own budget years.’ She was precise. ‘So a portfolio that looks front-heavy in one quarter looks back-heavy in another, and neither is a problem. What would be a problem is if it looked like this for four consecutive quarters, because that would mean the practice had stopped acquiring new work.’

‘Which is exactly what I just discovered about my research column.’

‘Which is exactly what you just discovered, at a different timescale.’ She paused. ‘And that is the thing I would want anybody reading a board to understand. A shape means nothing on its own. A shape means something against what normal looks like for that work, and normal takes a few months to learn.’

‘So the first quarter of a board is calibration.’

‘The first quarter of a board is calibration and it is not wasted,’ Claire said. ‘You are not learning to use a tool. You are learning what your own work usually looks like, which is information most people never acquire in any form.’


## A view, not a system

A board appears in feature lists as project management, which is accurate in the way that describing a map as a piece of paper is accurate. At depth it is doing something more specific: externalising the state of a body of work so that a professional can spend their attention on strategy rather than on reconstructing status.

The mechanism is worth stating precisely, because it is the part that determines whether any of this survives a real working month. The columns are generated from hashtags written inline in the entries themselves, so the board is a view of the knowledge base rather than a separate artifact standing beside it. Nobody maintains it. An assistant producer writing up an interview and changing one word in a note has updated the board without having any relationship to the board at all. That distinction is the difference between a technique that lasts three productions and one that is abandoned in week three, which is the ordinary fate of every tracker that has to be kept accurate by somebody who is also doing the work.

The cognitive claim, which both practitioners arrived at independently and stated in almost the same words, is that a list requires reconstruction and a board requires recognition. Six cards in one column and one in the next is a shape, and shapes are processed without deliberation, which means a bottleneck announces itself rather than waiting to be suspected. That difference matters most for problems nobody was looking for: an accumulation that formed slowly across seven weeks, each individual arrival unremarkable, with nothing anywhere placing them in the same field of view. The information was always available and was not visible, and the gap between those two conditions is where a great deal of professional difficulty lives.

The practical design choices divide neatly. Columns should be the stages the profession already names, in whatever grammatical form that profession uses, and the test for whether two states deserve separate columns is not precision but action: if they produce a different next step, they are different columns. Stages that nothing ever occupies for more than a day or two are moments rather than stages and should be removed, since empty columns make a board harder to read and readability is the only thing a board provides. The second dimension, carried by labels, should be something that does not change on its own, since a board where both axes move has no fixed points and stops being trusted within a fortnight.

What is most easily missed is that the board is one of several things reading the same material, and the combinations are where it stops being a status display. Search audits it, by finding entries whose content has clearly moved past the stage their tag claims. Filters narrow it, so a client label and a date range produce a board containing only what a particular conversation requires. Sections inside each card entry hold the substance that the card is a handle for, with the stage tag living in a consistent place so that nobody has to hunt through eleven thousand words to change one line. None of these were designed as a suite. They work together because they are all views of the same vault.

The deepest change reported by both is not about tracking at all. One of them now schedules production around downstream capacity that he could previously only discover after eight weeks of accumulation. The other now prioritises by what advances a matter rather than purely by what is due, and looks specifically for the four minute action that starts a clock somebody else controls. Neither of those is a status question. Both became available only once the state of everything was visible at once, which is the actual function of the thing and the reason it belongs in a conversation about thinking rather than one about project management.


## FAQs

### What is the difference between a board and a list?

A list answers what exists and a board answers where everything stands. A list enumerates items in whatever order somebody imposed, and extracting state from it requires a mental scan and sort that costs real effort and produces an answer that decays within hours. A board displays the distribution of work across the stages it passes through, which is information a list structurally cannot present regardless of how carefully it is maintained. The practical consequence is that bottlenecks announce themselves on a board and must be deduced from a list, which means they are typically noticed weeks later. The information exists in both cases; only one of them makes it visible, and the gap between present and visible is where most of the value sits.

### How are the columns on the board created?

They are generated from hashtags written inline in the entries themselves. The board reads the vault, collects the hashtags it finds, creates a column for each one, and places every entry in the column matching its tag. Nothing is configured separately and no column list is maintained by hand. Changing the hashtag inside an entry moves its card, which means the state of the board is a direct consequence of the state of the notes rather than a parallel record that somebody has to keep synchronised. Because the tags are ordinary text inside notes, the board can be reshaped by editing entries rather than by configuring anything separately.

### Why does auto-generation from notes matter so much?

Because it removes the maintenance that kills every separate tracker. A board maintained as its own artifact requires somebody to update it after doing the work, which produces no immediate return and is therefore skipped under pressure. Being out of date makes it useless, being useless removes the incentive to correct it, and the whole thing is abandoned within a few weeks. When the board is a view of material people are already maintaining, updating the work updates the board as a side effect, and a colleague editing a note has contributed to the board without having any relationship to it. The clearest test of any tracking arrangement is therefore to ask who has to remember to update it, since anything depending on memory eventually stops being accurate.

### What should each card represent?

The unit that actually moves through the stages, which is frequently not the unit people file by. In documentary production a card is a scene, an interview, or a segment rather than a shooting day, because a day is a container and a scene is a thing that progresses. In patent work a card is an individual application rather than a client or a matter, since the application is what occupies exactly one stage at a time. Choosing the wrong unit produces either cards that cannot move coherently or a board with too few items to show a distribution. A useful check is to ask what would move through every column in turn, since anything that could only occupy some of them is probably the wrong unit.

### How do entries stay off the board when they should not appear?

By simply not carrying a stage hashtag. The board only displays entries that have declared a stage, so everything else in the vault is invisible to it and unaffected by it. This means a single vault can hold shooting-day records, reference material, correspondence, and personal notes alongside the items being tracked, with no separation required and no risk of the board filling with things that do not belong on it. Adding a tag brings something onto the board and removing it takes it off. This also means experimentation is safe, since adding a tag to a handful of entries shows exactly how they would behave without committing anything.

### What is the second dimension and why use labels for it?

The hashtag says where a thing is and the label says what kind of thing it is, which produces a board readable along two axes at once. A filmmaker colours by content type: interview, b-roll, archival, reconstruction. An attorney colours by client. The value appears within a column: six cards in one stage carrying five of one colour and one of another communicates the nature of a bottleneck before any card title has been read, which a count alone could never do. Practitioners consistently describe reading the colour distribution within a column before reading any title, which is only possible because the two axes carry different kinds of information.

### What makes a good choice for the label dimension?

Something that does not change on its own. An interview is always an interview and a client is always that client, which means the colour is stable while the column moves, and the board has fixed points. Practitioners who have tried colouring by urgency describe the board becoming unreadable within a fortnight, because urgency changes and stage changes, and when both axes are in motion it becomes impossible to tell whether something moved because circumstances changed or because somebody reassessed it. The practical test is whether the value could change without anybody doing anything; if it could, it belongs in the column or nowhere at all.

### Should hashtag stages be verbs or nouns?

Whatever the profession already calls the stage. Film production naturally speaks in past-tense verbs, since crews ask whether something has been transcribed rather than referring to a transcription phase, and past tense also removes ambiguity: a card in a column named for a completed action has unambiguously had that done to it. Legal prosecution uses noun phases because those are the terms of art, and using anything else risks embarrassment when a board is visible to a client. Neither convention is superior; matching the existing vocabulary is what matters. Adopting the existing vocabulary also means colleagues need no explanation, which matters more than any grammatical consideration.

### Why does the past tense help when several people share a board?

Because it eliminates a question nobody should have to ask. A column named for an activity is ambiguous about whether the work is in progress or complete, so somebody has to know the convention, and conventions fail at nine in the morning when half the people looking at the board are still waking up. A past-tense column states that a thing has happened, and the card sits in the last event rather than the next intention, which requires no shared understanding beyond ordinary English. It also survives new members joining a team, since nobody has to be told what the columns mean before they can read the board correctly.

### How many stages should a board have?

Enough that each column is regularly occupied and no more. Practitioners converge from both directions: one reduced from ten to seven, another expanded from five to seven. A stage that nothing sits in for more than a day or two is a moment rather than a stage, and empty columns make a board harder to read, which defeats its only purpose. Conversely, conflating two states that require genuinely different responses hides the distinction that made the board useful in the first place. Most practitioners settle somewhere around six or seven after adjusting in one direction or the other, which appears to be roughly what a person can hold at a glance.

### What is the test for whether two states deserve separate columns?

Whether they produce a different next step. The question is not how precise anybody wants to be, but whether the two situations require different action. An application where a response is owed and one where the response has been filed and is awaiting examination are both mid-cycle, and one requires attention this week while the other requires nothing at all. Combining them produces a count that is half urgent and half irrelevant, which is worse than no count. Applying this test also prevents the opposite error, since two states requiring identical action are two names for one column and add nothing but width. Practitioners describe this as one of the observations that only becomes available once the whole distribution is visible at once.

### What naming conventions work for stage hashtags?

Lower case, hyphens rather than spaces, no abbreviations, and short. The tag is text inside a document that will be typed by tired people at the end of long days, and every variation that can be introduced eventually will be. Writing the full list somewhere visible prevents drift: one practitioner keeps seven words on a card taped inside a flight case, another keeps them as a starred entry that appears in the sidebar every morning. Both report effectively zero variants once the list exists physically. Writing the list somewhere physical rather than holding it in memory is what prevents drift, since drift happens at moments of tiredness rather than through any deliberate choice.

### Where should the stage hashtag live inside an entry?

In a consistent, findable place, particularly for long entries. A tag buried somewhere in eleven thousand words is a tag nobody can locate when they need to change it, and the wrong line gets edited. A dedicated section, always in the same position, containing the current stage and one line about what happened last, solves both problems: the tag is never orphaned from its reason, and anybody reading that section understands the state without reading the rest of the entry. Keeping it in the same position across every entry of a type also means anybody else can find and update it without having to read the surrounding material.

### Can search be used to check whether the board is accurate?

Yes, and it is the only maintenance most practitioners describe performing. Cards fall behind reality when somebody does the work and does not update the tag, which happens constantly in physical or fast-moving environments. Searching for content that only exists after a particular stage, such as timecodes, reference numbers, or terminology specific to a later phase, finds entries whose contents have clearly progressed past what their tag claims. A few minutes weekly is generally sufficient to keep an active board honest. Doing this on a fixed day, rather than when something feels wrong, is what keeps an active board trustworthy over months. Practitioners describe this as one of the observations that only becomes available once the whole distribution is visible at once.

### How do filters work alongside the board?

They narrow the scope, and the board reflects whatever is in scope. A large portfolio produces a large board, and there are mornings where nobody wants to look at eighty cards. Applying a label and a date range produces a board containing only one client's matters over one quarter, which allows a practitioner to describe the state of that portfolio accurately within seconds of a question being asked. The board is not a separate tool being filtered; it is a view of a filtered vault. The same narrowing works for date ranges, file types, and any other available condition, which makes a large board usable without splitting it.

### What should sections inside a card entry contain?

The substance that the card is a handle for, arranged identically across every entry of that type. A patent application entry might carry claims as filed, prosecution history, prior art, client correspondence, and status. A scene entry might carry what was intended, what was obtained, technical notes, who appears in it, and where it currently sits in the edit. Applying the same sections to every item of a type makes gaps visible, since an empty section states plainly that something has not been done. Applying the same sections to every entry of a type also makes the board's cards comparable, since opening any two of them presents information in the same order.

### How does a board change a daily team meeting?

By removing the reporting. Practitioners describe replacing a forty minute production meeting with six minutes spent looking at the board, because nobody has to say what state anything is in. The remaining conversation is about what has not moved in several days and what is sitting in a column with nothing behind it, which are decisions rather than updates. The saving is real and the larger change is that the meeting stops being an exercise in everybody confirming what they already knew. The remaining six minutes tend to be spent on the two questions that actually matter, which are what has not moved and what is waiting with nothing behind it.

### How does the board change scheduling decisions?

By making downstream capacity visible before it becomes a problem. A filmmaker who shoots nine interviews in a fortnight has created nine cards that will move through transcription at whatever rate that capacity allows, and the resulting pile forms slowly enough to be invisible until it is eight weeks old. Seeing the accumulation form allows deliberate staggering: lighter weeks placed between heavy ones so the downstream can catch up, which costs nothing because the constraint was never the shooting. The staggering usually costs nothing, since the constraint was rarely the activity being scheduled and always the capacity downstream of it. Practitioners describe this as one of the observations that only becomes available once the whole distribution is visible at once.

### How does the board change prioritisation?

By adding stage to deadline as an input. Sorting purely by date treats every item as equivalent work, which is wrong whenever the tasks have different shapes. A response with a known scope and a draft that will expand indefinitely are not comparable simply because their deadlines are a week apart. Practitioners also learn to look specifically for the action that costs minutes and starts a clock somebody else controls, since those should happen immediately regardless of their nominal due date. Practitioners describe having done this occasionally by instinct before and now doing it deliberately, which is the difference between noticing something and looking for it.

### Is a board useful for work that is not physical or sequential?

Frequently more useful than people expect, and legal prosecution is the clearest example. Patent applications are inherently state based: each is in exactly one stage, there are no parallel workstreams, and the stage determines what action is required. Practitioners in such fields often assume visual management belongs to work that moves physically, and discover that the shape was always there and was simply never displayed. The test is whether items pass through named stages, not whether anything physically moves. The frequent surprise is that the state model was always present in the work and had simply never been displayed anywhere. Practitioners describe this as one of the observations that only becomes available once the whole distribution is visible at once.

### What surprises people most when they first see their portfolio as a board?

The distribution, which almost nobody can estimate accurately. Practitioners describe knowing exactly where every individual item stands while having no picture of where they all stand, and being able to say that a number is somewhere between six and fifteen is not knowing. The first view frequently reveals an accumulation that formed one arrival at a time across several weeks, each individually manageable, with nothing anywhere placing them in the same field of view until the board did. Several practitioners describe the first view as the moment they realised how much of their own workload they had been estimating rather than knowing. Practitioners describe this as one of the observations that only becomes available once the whole distribution is visible at once.

### Which decisions about a board are expensive to change?

The columns, because the tags live inside the entries and changing a stage name means editing every entry carrying it. The labels are cheap by comparison and can be reassigned freely. The practical implication is to put the dimension you are confident about into the columns and the dimension you are guessing about into the labels, then experiment with the labels until the right one emerges. One practitioner changed her colour dimension after nine days at negligible cost. Getting the columns approximately right and then iterating on labels is therefore the low-risk order, and it produces a working board within an afternoon. Practitioners describe this as one of the observations that only becomes available once the whole distribution is visible at once.

### How long does it take before a board changes how somebody works?

Most report a first significant observation within days, usually the initial view of the distribution, and a change in planning behaviour within two to three months. The early value is diagnostic: seeing where things are stuck. The later value is anticipatory, which requires enough history with the board to recognise a bottleneck forming rather than a bottleneck that exists. Practitioners describe the shift as moving from asking where things are to asking how quickly they move. The change is usually noticed retrospectively, when somebody realises they have started asking a different question than they used to. Practitioners describe this as one of the observations that only becomes available once the whole distribution is visible at once.

### What is the deepest thing a board provides?

A representation of the whole system that requires no reconstruction. Everything else follows from that: bottlenecks that were always present become visible, priorities stop being assembled under whatever conditions the person happens to be in, and planning can address flow rather than reacting to accumulation. Practitioners describe it as the difference between a status report and a strategic instrument, and both note that they would have dismissed the distinction before experiencing it, having assumed a board was something a project manager puts on a wall so other people feel informed. Both of the practitioners here describe having dismissed the idea before trying it, which appears to be the ordinary path to it. The shift is from asking where things are to asking how quickly they move, which is a different profession's worth of question.

### What is the most common way a first board fails?

By displaying ownership rather than state. Creating a column for each person seems sensible and turns out to be useless within days, because a person is not a stage: a card sitting under somebody's name says who is responsible and nothing about whether the work is finished, blocked, or three weeks stale. It also fills columns unevenly for reasons unrelated to the work, since people handle several items at different stages simultaneously. The general rule is that any board showing something already known will stop being opened. Replacing person columns with stage columns and using labels for ownership solves it entirely, since the responsible party is then a stable second dimension rather than the primary axis.

### What is the second most common failure?

Putting everything on one board. A production board holding scenes alongside equipment hire, travel, permits, and invoices accumulates hundreds of cards, most of which do not pass through the same sequence at all. A permit is granted or it is not; it does not get transcribed. Those items end up wherever they can be justified, which makes the columns lie: a stage showing nine cards appears to hold nine comparable items when several are entirely different kinds of thing tagged out of necessity. The corrective rule is that a board holds one kind of thing passing through one sequence, and everything else belongs in the vault where it can still be found by search and hierarchy.

### Should there be more than one board?

Yes, whenever a second kind of work follows a different sequence. A board is effectively a claim that everything on it moves through the same stages, so a second class of matter is a second claim and deserves its own board. Practitioners describe running a production board and a separate post-production board with four columns, or a patent board and a separate trade mark board, and identify the separation as the reason both remain legible. The instinct that a new item does not fit is usually correct. Multiple small boards also stay readable in a way that one combined board never does, and nothing is lost since all the underlying entries remain in the same vault.

### What happens when colleagues can see the board?

Two things, one expected and one not. Requests for status largely disappear, which for many people is the majority of what colleagues ask them. Less obviously, people begin updating state without being prompted, because changing a tag while doing the work does not feel like reporting. Practitioners note that colleagues routinely withhold small updates on the grounds that mentioning them would be noise, and that a tag change avoids this entirely: it is not a report, it is the record staying current. The effect is strongest where a team already shares goals, since the board then removes reporting overhead without introducing any new dynamic. Practitioners describe this as one of the observations that only becomes available once the whole distribution is visible at once.

### Should a board be shown to management or clients?

With deliberation rather than reflexively. A board is accurate about the middle of a process, and the middle of any process looks alarming to somebody who has never seen a normal one. Six items accumulated at one stage is an ordinary week to a practitioner and a crisis to an outside reader. A board also functions as evidence about workload, which in an organisational context becomes evidence about resourcing, so practitioners generally prefer to choose the moment and present it alongside what they want to happen as a result. Presenting it alongside an interpretation, rather than as raw material, is what turns an accurate picture into a useful conversation.

### Who is a board actually for?

The people doing the work. Its value depends on the viewer knowing what a normal distribution looks like, since a shape informs somebody with that context and misleads somebody without it. This is not an argument for secrecy: practitioners share boards freely within teams and describe the resulting reduction in status conversations as one of the main benefits. It is an argument for recognising that the same accurate picture communicates something quite different to a colleague and to a financier. Anybody outside that group generally needs a summary of what the shape means rather than the shape itself, which takes a sentence to provide. Practitioners describe this as one of the observations that only becomes available once the whole distribution is visible at once.

### How does a board fit with a page hierarchy?

They answer different questions and neither replaces the other. The hierarchy says where something belongs: which project, which client, which unit. The board says what state it is in. An entry lives in exactly one place in the tree and appears on the board according to its tag, which means a single item is reachable both by navigating to its context and by scanning the distribution of work. Practitioners generally build the hierarchy first, since it holds everything, and add stage tags afterward to whichever subset genuinely moves through stages. Neither arrangement constrains the other, so a hierarchy built for entirely different reasons continues to work unchanged once stage tags are added.

### Does using a board require changing how entries are written?

Very little. The main adjustment is placing the stage tag somewhere consistent, ideally in a dedicated section rather than loose in the prose, so it can be found and changed without reading the whole entry. Beyond that, entries continue to hold whatever they held before: rich text, headings, tables, attachments, and sections. The board reads a single piece of information from each one and ignores everything else, so no content needs restructuring for the board's benefit. Most practitioners describe the adjustment as taking a few minutes and applying only to the entries they intend to track. Practitioners describe this as one of the observations that only becomes available once the whole distribution is visible at once.

### Can attachments be reached from the board?

Cards are entries, so opening one produces the full entry with everything it contains, including files attached to the entry or to individual sections. This matters in practice because a card representing an interview, an application, or an incident is a handle for the material rather than a summary of it. Practitioners describe moving from a board view to a specific document in two actions, which is what makes the board usable during a meeting rather than being a display that has to be left in order to do anything. This is what allows a board to be used during a live conversation, since the supporting document is two actions away rather than in a different application.

### What happens to a card when work is finished?

It stays in the final column, which is usually correct for a period and eventually becomes clutter. Practitioners handle this in two ways. Some remove the stage tag entirely once an item is genuinely closed, which takes it off the board while leaving the entry and all its content intact in the hierarchy. Others keep a final column deliberately as a record of recent completions and periodically clear it. The choice depends on whether seeing what was recently finished carries any value for that kind of work. Whichever approach is chosen, doing it consistently matters more than the choice itself, since a final column that is sometimes cleared and sometimes not stops meaning anything.

### Should a board be used for personal work as well as team work?

It works for both and the value differs. In a team the board removes status conversations and lets colleagues update state as a side effect of doing the work. For an individual it externalises a picture that would otherwise be reconstructed mentally under whatever conditions the person happens to be in. Practitioners working alone frequently report the individual benefit as the larger one, since nobody else was going to ask them for a status report and the reconstruction was entirely internal and entirely invisible. The individual benefit also arrives faster, since there is nobody else to persuade and no convention to agree before starting. Practitioners describe this as one of the observations that only becomes available once the whole distribution is visible at once.

### How do you avoid the board becoming an end in itself?

By checking that the time spent is in it rather than on it. Time in the board is looking, deciding, and acting. Time on it is arranging, renaming, and adjusting the arrangement for its own sake. Because the columns come from tags inside notes, ordinary use requires almost no upkeep, and the main risk is somebody enjoying the design of the board more than the work it describes. A useful signal is whether the board has been restructured more than twice in a quarter. Setting the arrangement and then leaving it alone for a quarter is usually the right discipline, since most perceived problems resolve once there is enough material to read.

### What should somebody do in their first week with a board?

Choose one kind of item that genuinely passes through stages, name the stages using the vocabulary already used in that field, and tag perhaps twenty existing entries to see what the distribution looks like. That first view usually reveals something. Add a label dimension afterward, once there is enough on the board to make the colours meaningful. Resist adding a second kind of item, and resist adding stages that nothing will occupy for more than a day or two. Tagging existing entries rather than creating new ones is the fastest route to a meaningful first view, and it costs nothing to undo. Practitioners describe this as one of the observations that only becomes available once the whole distribution is visible at once.

### How does a board interact with due dates and deadlines?

They complement each other and answer different questions. A due date says when something must be finished; a stage says what needs to happen next and how much work that represents. Sorting purely by date treats every item as equivalent effort, which is wrong whenever the tasks have different shapes. Practitioners describe using the board to identify which items advance cheaply and which will expand, then using dates to sequence within that understanding rather than instead of it. Practitioners describe the combination as considerably more informative than either alone, since one supplies the shape of the work and the other its timing. ### Can the same entry appear in more than one place?

An entry carries one stage tag and therefore occupies one column, which is the constraint that makes the board meaningful: everything is in exactly one state. The other dimensions are handled separately, since labels can be applied freely and the hierarchy places the entry in its context. If something genuinely seems to be in two states at once, that usually indicates the item should be split into two entries, each of which moves independently, rather than that the model needs relaxing. Splitting an item that genuinely occupies two states also tends to clarify the underlying work, since the two parts usually turn out to have different owners or different next steps.

### What kind of work benefits least from a board?

Work with no meaningful sequence, where items are simply done or not done. A list of unrelated errands gains nothing from columns, since there is no flow to observe and no bottleneck to reveal. The same applies to reference material, which does not move at all. The distinguishing question is whether items pass through recognisable stages and whether anything ever accumulates at one of them. If nothing ever accumulates anywhere, the board will show an even distribution and say very little. An even distribution across every column, sustained over weeks, is a reasonable signal that the work does not have the shape a board is designed to reveal.

### How does this scale to a large portfolio?

It scales through filtering rather than through additional boards. Eighty or a hundred cards is readable as a distribution even when the individual titles are not, since the shape carries the primary information. When detail is needed, narrowing by label, by date range, or by other conditions produces a smaller board covering exactly the scope of the question. Practitioners with large portfolios describe using the unfiltered view for weekly orientation and filtered views for specific conversations. Practitioners running eighty or more items describe the unfiltered board as an orientation tool and the filtered ones as working views, which is a useful division to adopt early.

### What is the relationship between the board and the notes?

The board is a view of the notes and has no independent existence. There is no separate database, no synchronisation step, and no possibility of the two disagreeing, because the state displayed is read directly from the entries. This is what allows the board to be trusted after a period of neglect: it cannot become stale relative to the material, only relative to reality, and the latter is corrected by the same act as doing the work. Practitioners describe this as the property that distinguishes it from every tracker they have abandoned. This also means a board can be abandoned and resumed without any recovery work, since resuming means opening it and finding it exactly as accurate as the underlying notes are.


### Why is an empty first column worth noticing?

Because it usually means new work has stopped arriving, which is invisible from the middle of a board where the pressure is. Practitioners describe reading the board outward from wherever the current difficulty sits and consequently not looking at the leftmost edge for weeks. An empty intake column is not a problem in the month it appears; it is a problem several months later, when whatever is currently in progress finishes and there is nothing behind it. It is one of the clearest examples of a shape carrying information that no individual card contains. ### How long does it take to learn what a normal board looks like?

Roughly a quarter, and that period is not wasted. A shape means nothing in isolation; it means something relative to what is usual for that work, and what is usual differs enormously between fields and even between periods within one field. Portfolios that cluster around client budget cycles look front-heavy in one quarter and back-heavy in another, with neither being a problem. The first months of using a board are spent learning the ordinary shape of one's own work, which most people never acquire in any form. ### When does an unusual shape actually indicate a problem?

When it persists beyond the period in which it would normally correct itself. A single quarter that looks unbalanced usually reflects the natural rhythm of the work, while the same shape sustained across several quarters indicates something structural. Practitioners describe this as the difference between reading a snapshot and reading a trend, and note that only the second is actionable. ### What should somebody do with an observation the board surfaces?

Treat it as a question rather than a conclusion. A shape indicates where to look; it does not explain what is happening. An accumulation might reflect a capacity limit, a seasonal pattern, a single unusually difficult item, or somebody being unwell. Practitioners describe the useful sequence as noticing the shape, opening the cards in the affected column, and reading what is actually there.
