There is a moment in most careers when somebody changes institutions, and it arrives with an opportunity almost nobody takes. Everything they have built is behind them, everything ahead is unbuilt, and for a few weeks they have the rarest condition in professional life: the chance to arrange things according to what they now know rather than according to what they knew when they started.

What people do instead is carry everything forward. The whole accumulated arrangement moves across, including the structure that made sense in a building they have left, the vocabulary that grew around a curriculum they no longer teach, and the several hundred items that were never any good and were never quite worth removing. The move preserves the material and preserves every decision underneath it.

![Starting Fresh at a New School](/assets/img/feature-screenshot-placeholder.svg)

The instinct behind that is understandable and largely mistaken. It rests on a fear of losing something, which is a fear about material rather than about arrangement, and the two are separable. Nothing has to be destroyed for somebody to begin again: an existing arrangement can be preserved intact, complete and searchable, while a new one is built alongside it.
<!--more-->


Once that separation is clear, the question becomes what to carry forward rather than whether to carry anything. And the honest answer for most people is a small fraction: the items that have proved themselves, the conventions that survived contact with reality, and the principles somebody could state in a sentence. Everything else is either specific to a situation that has ended or was never load-bearing.

The more interesting property of a fresh start is that the new arrangement can begin at a standard the old one never reached. An arrangement that grows organically over years contains every intermediate decision, including the ones somebody would not make again and cannot economically undo. An arrangement built deliberately by somebody who has already made those mistakes starts where the old one ended up, without the archaeology.

There is a cost, and it is not the one people expect. It is not the work, which is a few days. It is the discomfort of an empty structure, which feels like having lost something even when nothing was lost, and which lasts until enough material accumulates for the new arrangement to feel inhabited.

There is also an emotional dimension that most accounts of this skip. An accumulated body of work is a record of somebody becoming competent, including the parts that document them being bad at something. Setting it aside is a small act of closing a chapter, and people who do it describe a mixture of relief and something less comfortable that they generally do not name.

What follows is one week in the life of somebody doing exactly this, at a new school, with six years behind him and an empty folder in front of him.


## Monday, and a decision made in four minutes

Tomas arrived in the new building on a Monday in the middle of a term, which is the wrong time to arrive anywhere and which is when the position had come up.

The classroom was larger than his old one and had a fume cupboard that worked. There were two courses on his timetable he had never taught. And there was a robotics programme with three teams, which the previous teacher had run and which was now his.

He sat down at lunchtime with his laptop and a decision he had been postponing for a fortnight.

The old folder had six years in it. Around six hundred records covering chemistry and general science, several thousand files, and a structure that had grown around a building he had left.

He had assumed he would open it and continue.

What changed his mind took about four minutes and consisted of looking at his own top level. Six pages, one of which was called Miscellaneous and contained ninety items. One called Old Labs which was distinct from Labs for reasons he could no longer reconstruct. And nothing at all for two of the three subjects he was now teaching.

He renamed the folder to mark it as the previous school's material and made a new one.

He telephoned Raj that evening, largely because Raj had done something similar and had described it at the time as unexpectedly emotional.

‘Say what you actually did,’ Tomas asked.

‘Moved a finished production into an archive and started the next one clean.’ Raj was matter of fact. ‘Which took four seconds and which changed how the whole thing felt.’

‘Say the feeling.’

‘Like clearing a desk.’ He laughed. ‘Which is an absurd thing to say about a folder and which is accurate.’

‘Mine is not a finished project. It is six years of a job.’

‘Which is different and which I think makes the case stronger.’ Raj was thinking. ‘Because a project ends and a career does not, and if you carry the structure forward you are still working in a building you have left.’

‘Say what you would keep.’

‘Almost nothing structurally and quite a lot of material.’ He counted. ‘I kept my naming convention because it worked. I kept nothing else about how it was arranged.’

‘Say why not the arrangement.’

‘Because my arrangement had eleven top-level areas and four of them never accumulated anything.’ Raj shrugged. ‘Which I only knew after four years of watching them not accumulate.’

Tomas said something he had been thinking all afternoon.

‘I have a page called Miscellaneous with ninety things in it.’

‘Which is the same information in a different form.’

‘Say what you mean.’

‘Ninety things in Miscellaneous is ninety things whose home you never decided.’ Raj was definite. ‘Which is a structure telling you where it failed, and you have the opportunity to design against it before you start.’


## Tuesday, designing against six years of evidence

He built the structure on Tuesday evening in about ninety minutes, which he later described as the most useful ninety minutes of the whole move.

The principle he worked from was one he had not been able to state six years earlier: put the fewest kinds at the top and the most instances beneath.

Three courses at the top level. Chemistry, physics, robotics. Which is three items he takes in without reading, replacing six he had stopped reading.

Beneath chemistry, units. Beneath units, topics. Which is a genuine hierarchy rather than a flat list with a hierarchy implied by naming.

‘I had six years of records with names beginning with unit numbers,’ he said afterward. ‘Which is a structure I had built inside the titles because the structure I had built in the structure did not fit.’

Two additional areas at the top, both deliberate.

The first is templates, which he had never had. One record per kind of thing he produces, with the sections already in place and nothing else: a lesson plan, a laboratory procedure, an assessment, a project brief.

‘Say why that matters,’ Priya asked him later.

‘Because at the old school every lesson plan had slightly different sections.’ Tomas was frank. ‘Not deliberately. I built each one from whatever I could remember of the last one.’

‘Which means they drift.’

‘Which means six years of drift, and by the end my early lesson plans and my recent ones did not resemble each other at all.’ He counted. ‘Which matters because I could not scan them. I had to read each one to find out what was in it.’

The second additional area is for material coming across from the old school, kept separate rather than distributed.

‘Say why separate,’ Priya asked.

‘Because I want to know what is imported.’ Tomas was definite. ‘Something written for a different school with different equipment and a different examination board is a thing I should check before using, and I would like to be able to see at a glance which those are.’

‘So it is a quarantine.’

‘It is a quarantine and things graduate out of it once I have taught them here and adjusted them.’ He shrugged. ‘Which for most of them will take one term.’

The icons and colours went on immediately rather than months later.

‘I did this last at the old school and I would do it first now,’ he said. ‘Because I orient by colour without reading, and reading a list is what I was doing for six years without noticing that it was work.’


## Wednesday, and a vocabulary built before anything to describe

Priya telephoned him on Wednesday, which he had asked her to do because he wanted an argument.

‘Say what you are proposing,’ she said.

‘Building the whole vocabulary before I create a single record.’

‘Which is unusual.’

‘Which is the opposite of what I did last time.’ Tomas was frank. ‘Last time I created a description whenever I needed one, which produced sixty of them across six years, of which about twenty were used more than twice.’

‘Which is the standard failure.’

‘Which is apparently the standard failure and I would like to know whether the opposite is a different failure.’

Priya was quiet for a moment.

‘It is a different failure and it is smaller,’ she said. ‘The risk of designing in advance is that you build categories for questions you never ask.’

‘Which is what happened to you.’

‘Which is exactly what happened to me. Four descriptions about methodology, applied to two hundred papers, and I have never once filtered by them.’ She was matter of fact. ‘So my rule now is that a category has to answer a question I have actually asked out loud.’

‘Say how that applies to me.’

‘You have six years of asking questions.’ Priya was definite. ‘Which is the difference. You are not designing from imagination. You are designing from evidence about what you actually looked for.’

Tomas read her his list.

Four families. What kind of thing something is, meaning lesson, laboratory, assessment, project, reference. What it cuts across, meaning safety, homework, examination preparation, demonstration. Which course it belongs to, three of those. And what state it is in, meaning draft, ready, needs revision.

‘Fifteen,’ Priya said. ‘Which is a reasonable number and I want to test one family.’

‘Say which.’

‘The last one. State.’

‘Say the objection.’

‘That state changes and descriptions are for things that do not.’ She was precise. ‘Which is a rule you told me you had learned from Claire.’

Tomas thought about it.

‘Which is right and I am keeping it anyway, and I want to say why.’

‘Go on.’

‘Because a lesson plan has exactly three states and moves between them perhaps twice in its life.’ He counted. ‘Draft to ready when I have finished it. Ready to needs revision when something went wrong.’

‘Which is slow.’

‘Which is slow enough that maintaining it costs me nothing, and the alternative is not knowing which of two hundred lesson plans I have actually finished.’ Tomas was definite.

‘Then the rule is about frequency rather than about change.’

‘Which is a better statement of it than I had,’ he said. ‘Something changing weekly does not belong in a description. Something changing twice does.’

Priya said the thing he quoted afterward.

‘Design the vocabulary the way you would design a periodic table. Systematic, complete, and with the gaps visible.’

‘Say what the gaps do.’

‘Tell you what you have not thought about.’ She was matter of fact. ‘If you have three courses and four kinds of thing, that is twelve combinations, and if one of them is empty in September that is either a fact about your teaching or a hole in your arrangement.’


## Thursday, two folders open at once

Thursday evening he did the part he had been anticipating, which was going back into the old arrangement and deciding what came across.

Two windows. The archive on the left and the new folder on the right.

He had expected this to be difficult in a sentimental way and it was difficult in a different way entirely, which was that most of it was obviously not worth taking and admitting that was uncomfortable.

Six hundred records. He brought across forty-one.

‘Say how you chose,’ Raj asked him on the telephone afterward.

‘One question. Have I taught this more than twice and did it work every time.’ Tomas was matter of fact. ‘Which is a brutal test and it eliminated about ninety percent immediately.’

‘Say what survived.’

‘The stoichiometry laboratory that has worked with every group I have ever run it with.’ He counted. ‘A thermodynamics demonstration that students remember two years later. A project brief that won a regional competition and that I have refined four times.’

‘And the rest?’

‘The rest is a record of me learning to teach.’ Tomas was frank. ‘Which is worth keeping and is not worth carrying into a classroom.’

‘Which is a distinction I would not have made.’

‘Which I did not make until I was looking at two hundred lesson plans from my first two years and recognising that I would not use any of them.’ He shrugged. ‘They are not bad. They are the work of somebody who had not yet worked out what he was doing.’

The copying was not simply copying, which he had not anticipated.

Each of the forty-one arrived and then had to be reshaped: descriptions replaced with the new vocabulary, sections adjusted to match the templates, and in about a third of cases substantial rewriting because the equipment here is different.

‘Say how long per record.’

‘Between four and twenty minutes.’ Tomas counted. ‘Which across forty-one is most of a Saturday, and which is the correct amount of effort for material I will use for years.’

‘Say what you learned doing it.’

‘That reformatting something forces me to read it properly.’ He was thinking. ‘Which I have not done with most of these in three years, and I found two errors in a safety procedure that I have been issuing to students.’

‘Which is a reason to do this on its own.’

‘Which is a reason I would give anybody, and it is not the reason I did it,’ he said.

The attachments came across with the records: the safety documents, the student handouts, the data tables. Which he described as the least eventful part of the entire week.


## The thing he nearly did

There was one decision Tomas made on the Wednesday and reversed on the Thursday, and he describes it as the closest he came to spoiling the whole exercise.

He had intended to import everything and then delete.

‘Say what that means,’ Priya asked.

‘Bring all six hundred records into the new folder and then work through removing what I did not want.’ He was frank. ‘Which felt safer.’

‘Say why you changed your mind.’

‘Because I did about eighty of them and realised what I was doing.’ Tomas counted. ‘I was not selecting. I was importing and intending to select, which are different activities separated by about a year.’

‘Which is the standard failure.’

‘Which is apparently a failure everybody makes and I nearly made it in a week where I was specifically trying to avoid it.’ He was rueful. ‘Because deleting something requires a reason and keeping something requires nothing.’

‘Say the asymmetry properly.’

‘To remove a record I have to decide it is not worth keeping, which is a judgement, and to keep it I have to do nothing.’ Tomas was definite. ‘So an import-then-prune approach means the default outcome is keeping everything and the pruning is optional work I will not do.’

‘Whereas selecting.’

‘Whereas copying one at a time means the default is nothing and every item requires a reason to exist.’ He shrugged. ‘Which is the same six hundred decisions with the burden on the opposite side.’

Priya said something he wrote down.

‘Which side the effort falls on determines the outcome more than the criteria do.’

‘Say what you mean.’

‘You could have had the same test in both approaches.’ She was precise. ‘Taught more than twice and worked every time. And in the import version you would have ended with six hundred records and in the copy version you ended with forty-one.’

‘Which is the same rule producing opposite results.’

‘Which is the same rule producing opposite results because in one case the rule has to be applied to act and in the other it has to be applied to refrain,’ Priya said. ‘And nobody applies rules to refrain.’


## Friday, the things he did not do last time

There were four capabilities he had used at the old school only after eighteen months, and he set all four up before term.

The first is the schedule, which he entered on his first Friday: class periods, laboratory sessions, robotics meetings, department meetings, and the four school deadlines he already knew about.

‘Say why that matters,’ Priya asked.

‘Because at the old school I entered it in my second year and spent the first year with everything in my head.’ Tomas was frank. ‘Which worked until the week I had a parents' evening and a laboratory preparation deadline on the same afternoon.’

‘Which is a foreseeable collision.’

‘Which was entirely foreseeable to anybody looking at a calendar and which I was not doing, because building the calendar felt like an afternoon I did not have.’

Second, dates on the work rather than on reminders about the work. Every lesson plan carries the date it will be taught, which means the plan surfaces when it is needed rather than requiring him to go looking.

Third, stage markers driving a board, which he had never used at all.

‘Say what your columns are.’

‘Four. Planning, ready, taught, reviewed.’ He counted. ‘Which are the states a lesson actually passes through across a term.’

‘Say what the board tells you.’

‘Whether I am ahead or behind, which is the only question I have in October and which I previously answered by feeling anxious.’ Tomas was matter of fact. ‘Eleven things in planning and two in ready during the first week of a unit is a fact rather than an anxiety.’

‘And the last column?’

‘Reviewed is the one nobody would predict and it is the most valuable.’ He was definite. ‘It means I have taught it and written down what went wrong.’

‘Which you did before?’

‘Which I did in a line at the top of every lesson entry saying what I got wrong last time, and which I did inconsistently because nothing prompted me.’ Tomas shrugged. ‘Now a lesson sits in taught, visibly, until I have done it, and a column filling up is a prompt.’

Fourth, accepting the suggestions from the beginning rather than dismissing them for a year.

‘Say why you dismissed them before.’

‘Because they were wrong at first, which is what everybody says about them.’ He was frank. ‘And they were wrong because nothing had been trained, and dismissing them is what keeps them wrong.’

‘Which is circular.’

‘Which is entirely circular and I was inside it for a year.’ Tomas laughed. ‘This time I am accepting or rejecting every one deliberately from the first week, which costs a second each and which means by Christmas they will describe how I actually work.’


## The Tuesday of the second week

The structure held for eight days and then something happened that Tomas said afterward was the most useful event of the whole month.

The robotics teams did not fit.

He had built them as courses beneath the top level, with teams beneath and projects beneath teams, matching the chemistry arrangement.

‘Say what went wrong,’ Raj asked.

‘Nothing went wrong. It simply does not describe robotics.’ Tomas was matter of fact. ‘Chemistry has units in a sequence and every student does all of them. Robotics has three teams doing three unrelated things at the same time.’

‘Which is a different shape.’

‘Which is a completely different shape and I built it as though it were the same shape because I built all three on one Tuesday evening.’ He shrugged. ‘Which is what you do when you design three things at once.’

‘Say what you changed.’

‘Very little, which is the interesting part.’ Tomas counted. ‘I removed a level. Teams at the top of robotics, projects beneath, and no units at all because there are none.’

‘Which took how long?’

‘Four minutes.’ He laughed. ‘Which is why I am telling you about it as a good thing rather than a mistake.’

Raj asked the question that produced the useful part.

‘Would you have caught it by thinking harder on the Tuesday?’

Tomas took a moment.

‘No. And I have been thinking about that.’

‘Say why not.’

‘Because I had never taught robotics.’ He was frank. ‘I designed it from an analogy with a subject I know, which is the only material I had, and the analogy was wrong in a way I could not have detected without teaching it for a week.’

‘So the design had to be provisional.’

‘Which is a thing I would not have accepted a fortnight ago, because I was treating the design as the deliverable.’ Tomas was definite. ‘And it is not. The design is a first attempt informed by evidence, and for two of my three courses I had six years of evidence and for the third I had none.’

‘Which is worth saying to anybody doing this.’

‘Which is the thing I would say most strongly.’ He counted. ‘Build carefully where you have evidence. Build loosely where you do not. And expect the loose part to be wrong within a fortnight, which is fine, because moving something takes four minutes.’


## What it felt like

He wrote the first record in the new arrangement on the Friday evening, sitting in an empty classroom.

Three sections. What he had observed in his first week. Notes about the department. Impressions of his students.

He starred it, which he said afterward was slightly sentimental and which he did anyway.

‘Say what the week felt like,’ Raj asked him.

‘Two things at once and they did not resolve.’

‘Say both.’

‘Excited, because everything I built is better than anything I had.’ Tomas counted. ‘The structure fits. The vocabulary is complete. The templates mean every lesson plan I write from now on will look like every other one.’

‘Which is the payoff.’

‘Which is the payoff and it is real, and the new arrangement on its first day is better than the old arrangement was on its best day.’ He was definite. ‘Which is a strange thing to be able to say about something empty.’

‘Say the other thing.’

Tomas took a moment.

‘That six years is in a folder I have renamed.’

‘Which is not gone.’

‘Which is entirely intact and searchable and four seconds away, and it still felt like closing something.’ He was frank. ‘Because it is a record of me becoming a teacher, and that includes being bad at it, and there is a version of me in those first two years that I am slightly fond of.’

‘Say more.’

‘There is a lesson plan from my first term with a timing that is completely unrealistic.’ Tomas laughed. ‘Twelve activities in fifty minutes. Written by somebody who had never taught a fifty-minute lesson.’

‘And you did not bring it across.’

‘I did not bring it across and I read it twice on Thursday evening.’ He shrugged. ‘Which is what an archive is for, and it is not what a working arrangement is for, and holding both of those at once is the whole of what this week was.’

Raj said something that Tomas repeated later.

‘Everybody talks about the fresh start as though the difficulty is the work.’

‘Say what it actually is.’

‘Sitting in front of something empty and knowing that the version of you that filled the last one took six years,’ Raj said. ‘Which is a feeling rather than a problem and it goes away in about a fortnight.’


## What did not come with him

There is a category Tomas decided about on the Tuesday and did not revisit, and he describes it as the only decision of the week he made without hesitating.

Records about specific students.

‘Say what those are,’ Priya asked.

‘Observations. Which pupils struggle with which concepts. Conversations with parents. Notes about a student who was having difficulty at home.’ He counted. ‘Perhaps eighty records across six years.’

‘Which are useful.’

‘Which are extremely useful and which are not mine.’ Tomas was definite. ‘They are about children at a school I no longer work at, and I have no reason to hold them and no right to move them somewhere else.’

‘Say what happened to them.’

‘They stay in the archive, which stays on my machine, and I will delete them at the end of the academic year when there is no possibility that the school needs anything from me about them.’ He was matter of fact. ‘Which I decided in about eleven seconds and which I would have found difficult four years ago.’

‘Say why easier now.’

‘Because I have thought about it before.’ Tomas shrugged. ‘Somebody in the group asked once what happens to material about people who did not choose to be in your records, and I have been carrying that question since.’

Priya said hers was the same shape.

‘I have interview material from people who consented to a study that has concluded.’

‘Which is the same problem.’

‘Which is the identical problem with a different vocabulary, and my institution has a policy about it and I would have retained it out of habit if the policy did not exist.’ She was frank.

‘So the policy is doing work.’

‘The policy is doing work I should be doing myself, which is a slightly uncomfortable thing to notice,’ Priya said.

Tomas made one further distinction.

‘My lesson material is mine and the student material is not, and the line is completely clear.’

‘Say what makes it clear.’

‘Whether it is about my practice or about a person.’ He was definite. ‘A laboratory procedure is about my practice. A note about a boy who needed the procedure explained three times is about a boy.’

‘Which is the same test Meera uses.’

‘Which is apparently the test everybody arrives at, and I did not know that when I made the decision on Tuesday,’ he said.


## Beginning again, deliberately

A change of institution arrives with an opportunity almost nobody takes: the chance to arrange things according to what somebody now knows rather than according to what they knew when they started. What most people do instead is carry everything forward, preserving the material and every decision underneath it.

The instinct behind that is a fear of losing something, which is a fear about material rather than about arrangement. The two are separable, and recognising that removes the obstacle: an existing body of work can be preserved intact, complete and searchable, while a new arrangement is built beside it. The practitioner here renamed a folder and made another, which took four minutes.

What prompted the decision was looking honestly at his own top level. Six areas, one called Miscellaneous containing ninety items, one whose distinction from another he could no longer reconstruct, and nothing at all for two of the three subjects he was about to teach. As a colleague put it to him, ninety items in Miscellaneous is ninety items whose home was never decided, which is a structure telling somebody exactly where it failed.

The new structure took ninety minutes and was built from principles rather than from the old arrangement. The fewest kinds at the top and the most instances beneath, which produced three courses replacing six areas. A genuine hierarchy rather than a flat list with a hierarchy implied by naming, which is what six years of unit-numbered titles had actually been. Icons and colours applied immediately rather than eventually, on the grounds that reading a list is work somebody does for years without noticing.

Two additions had not existed before. A templates area holding one record per kind of thing he produces, with sections already in place, addressing six years of drift during which early and recent lesson plans stopped resembling each other. And a quarantine area for imported material, kept separate so that anything written for a different school with different equipment is visibly flagged until it has been taught here and adjusted.

The vocabulary was built entirely before any material existed, which is the opposite of what produced sixty descriptions across six years of which twenty were used more than twice. A colleague tested the approach and identified its risk, which is building categories for questions nobody asks, and then identified why it did not apply: he was designing from six years of evidence about what he actually looked for rather than from imagination.

One family survived a challenge and refined a rule in the process. State descriptions were objected to on the grounds that states change and descriptions are for stable properties. His defence was that a lesson plan has three states and moves between them perhaps twice, which is slow enough to cost nothing to maintain. The rule that emerged is better than the original: frequency rather than change is what matters.

Selection was the uncomfortable part. Six hundred records produced forty-one, chosen by a single brutal question about whether something had been taught more than twice and had worked every time. What survived is a small set of proven material. What did not is a record of somebody learning to teach, which he describes as worth keeping and not worth carrying into a classroom.

Copying turned out not to be copying. Each item required its descriptions replaced, its sections reshaped to match a template, and in about a third of cases substantial rewriting for different equipment, at four to twenty minutes each. The unplanned benefit was that reformatting forces genuine reading, which surfaced two errors in a safety procedure he had been issuing to students for three years.

Dates on the work itself. A board with four columns whose last one, for lessons taught but not yet reviewed, prompts a habit he previously performed inconsistently because nothing prompted him. And accepting or rejecting suggestions deliberately from the first week, having previously dismissed them for a year because they were wrong, which is what keeps them wrong.

One category did not travel at all and was decided in seconds. The test he applies is whether something is about his practice or about a person, which several practitioners in unrelated professions have arrived at independently.

The emotional account is the part most descriptions of this omit. Two things at once and they did not resolve: genuine excitement that the new arrangement on its first day is better than the old one was on its best day, alongside the recognition that six years sits in a renamed folder. It is intact, searchable, and four seconds away, and setting it aside still felt like closing something, because it is a record of somebody becoming competent and that includes the parts documenting them being bad at it.

What a colleague told him is the useful summary. Everybody treats the difficulty of a fresh start as the work, and the work is a few days. The actual difficulty is sitting in front of something empty, knowing that the version of you who filled the last one took six years, and that feeling goes away in about a fortnight.


## FAQs

### Why is a change of institution an opportunity?

Because everything built is behind and everything ahead is unbuilt, which practitioners describe as the rarest condition in professional life. For a few weeks somebody can arrange things according to what they now know rather than what they knew when they started, and that window closes as soon as material begins accumulating in whatever structure exists. Practitioners who have made the same move in unrelated professions arrive at the same conclusion, which suggests it follows from how arrangements age rather than from any particular field. It is one of the realisations that only becomes available after somebody has lived with the consequences of the alternative. Anybody uncertain can test the question against their own current arrangement in a few minutes rather than reasoning about it.
