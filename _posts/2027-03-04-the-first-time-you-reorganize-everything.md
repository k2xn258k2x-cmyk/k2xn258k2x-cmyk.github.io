Every arrangement for holding information is designed against a situation, and situations change. A structure that suits fifty items becomes unnavigable at five hundred, not because anybody chose badly but because the properties that made it work at the smaller scale stop applying. A list somebody can take in at a glance becomes a list they must read. A distinction that separated two kinds of thing stops separating anything once a third kind arrives.

The usual response is to patch. Somebody adds a category that does not quite fit the scheme, or creates a location whose name explains what it is not, or adopts a naming convention for new material that differs from the one governing old material. Each patch is individually reasonable and takes four minutes. Collectively they produce an arrangement nobody could describe, which somebody navigates by memory rather than by structure.

![The First Time You Reorganize Everything](/assets/img/feature-screenshot-placeholder.svg)

That drift is difficult to notice because it is gradual and because each individual accommodation was sensible at the time. What people notice instead is a symptom: a growing reluctance to file things, a habit of leaving material in a temporary location, or a sense that finding something requires knowing where it went rather than knowing what it is. The structure has stopped doing its job and nobody has declared it.
<!--more-->


The reason people avoid the obvious remedy is that restructuring feels dangerous. Somebody has years of material arranged in a way they understand, and moving it means a period during which they understand it less. There is a fear of losing things in the process, a fear of breaking retrieval habits built over months, and a suspicion that the effort will not be worth it.

Most of that fear turns out to be misplaced, and understanding why removes the obstacle. Where an arrangement is one dimension among several, moving something between locations changes only its location. Its title is unchanged, its descriptions are unchanged, its content and attachments are unchanged. Everything that determines whether search reaches it is untouched, which means retrieval continues working throughout a reorganisation regardless of how confused the intermediate state becomes.

That property converts restructuring from a risky operation into a reversible one. Somebody who moves material and dislikes the result can move it back, and during the interval nothing is unreachable. The only genuine risk is the human one: beginning something substantial at the wrong moment and abandoning it halfway, leaving an arrangement that is neither the old one nor the new one.

There is a psychological dimension that people rarely anticipate and that both practitioners here describe in opposite terms. For somebody restructuring a working system under pressure, the experience is anxious. For somebody archiving completed work and starting fresh, it is unexpectedly satisfying. The difference is not the operation, which is identical, but whether the person is rearranging their present or closing their past.

What follows is a conversation between two people who restructured for entirely different reasons within a few months of each other, one because her professional situation changed and one because a project ended.


## Two reorganisations, two reasons

Raj had suggested it after Claire mentioned hers in the group.

‘You said you had spent a weekend moving things,’ he said, ‘and you said it in a way that suggested it had not been enjoyable.’

‘It was not enjoyable and it was necessary, and those are different.’ Claire was matter of fact. ‘Say why you asked.’

‘Because I did the same thing about six weeks ago and I enjoyed it enormously, and I want to know whether we did the same operation.’

‘Say what prompted yours.’

‘Finishing something.’ Raj counted. ‘The Oaxaca documentary is delivered. Two new projects are starting. And my structure was built entirely around one production.’

‘Which had ended.’

‘Which had ended and which was still occupying the whole of the top level, alongside two things that had barely begun.’ He shrugged. ‘So the arrangement was describing a situation that no longer existed.’

‘Mine was the reverse.’ Claire was precise. ‘Nothing of mine ended. The firm took on a new practice area and my structure had no room for it.’

‘Say what your structure was.’

‘One location per client, with matters beneath.’ She counted. ‘Which was correct for twenty clients and became unusable at forty.’

‘Say what unusable means.’

‘That the top level was forty items in a list and I had stopped reading it.’ Claire was frank. ‘I was navigating by search entirely, which works and which means the structure had become storage rather than navigation.’

‘Which is not a failure.’

‘Which is not a failure and it is a waste, because the structure was doing nothing for me and I was maintaining it.’ She shrugged. ‘And then a new practice area arrived and the question of where it went had no good answer.’

‘Because the top level was already full.’

‘Because the top level was organised by client and the new thing was a kind of work, and those do not combine.’ Claire was definite. ‘Which is when I accepted that the organising principle was wrong rather than the capacity.’


## Forty clients into four practice areas

‘Say what you did,’ Raj said.

‘Inverted it.’ Claire shared. ‘Practice area at the top. Clients beneath.’

‘Which is four items instead of forty.’

‘Four at the top level, which I can take in without reading, and each one containing between six and eighteen clients.’ She was matter of fact. ‘Which is a list I can read.’

‘Say why four is the right number.’

‘Because it is the number of kinds of work I do, which is a fact about my practice rather than a decision.’ Claire was precise. ‘Which is the thing I got right this time and wrong originally.’

‘Say the distinction.’

‘My original structure organised by the thing that had the most instances, which is clients.’ She counted. ‘My current one organises by the thing that has the fewest, which is kinds of work.’

‘And the fewest is better.’

‘The fewest is better at the top and the most is better at the bottom, which sounds obvious stated plainly and was not obvious to me for four years.’ Claire shrugged.

‘Say how you moved things.’

‘Location by location, using the menu on each one, which offers moving it elsewhere.’ She was matter of fact. ‘Which took a Saturday.’

‘Say the volume.’

‘Forty client locations and everything beneath them, which is several hundred matters.’ Claire counted. ‘And the moving is per location rather than per entry, which is what made it feasible.’

‘Because a client moves as a unit.’

‘Because a client location moves and everything inside it comes along, which means forty operations rather than several hundred.’ She was definite. ‘If I had had to move entries individually I would not have done it.’

‘Say what took the longest.’

‘Deciding, not moving.’ Claire was frank. ‘About eleven clients do work in two practice areas and I had to choose where each of them lives.’

‘Which is a real question.’

‘Which is the only real question in the whole exercise and it occupied most of the Saturday.’ She shrugged. ‘The moving itself is a few seconds each.’

‘How did you decide?’

‘By volume. Whichever practice area accounts for most of their work.’ Claire counted. ‘Which is arbitrary in about four cases and is a decision that had to be made somehow.’

‘And the other practice area?’

‘Finds them by searching or by category, which is what the second dimension is for.’ She was definite. ‘Which is the thing I kept reminding myself of throughout. The location is one answer and it does not have to be the only one.’

Raj asked about the visual changes.

‘You mentioned making them look different.’

‘Which I did afterward and which I would do first if I did it again.’ Claire was matter of fact. ‘Each practice area has an icon and a colour.’

‘Say what that does.’

‘It means I identify where I am without reading.’ She shrugged. ‘Which at four items is a small benefit and which compounds, because everything beneath inherits the visual association.’

‘So you know which area a client sits in.’

‘I glance at the panel and I know, which I did not expect to matter and which turns out to be how I orient several times an hour.’


## An archive and two fresh starts

‘Now yours,’ Claire said.

‘Mine was three operations and the first one was the whole of it.’ Raj shared. ‘I made a location called Archive and moved the finished production into it.’

‘Which is one move.’

‘Which is one move and about four seconds and which changed the entire feel of the thing.’ He was cheerful about it. ‘Because the top level went from a completed project and two barely started ones to two active projects and a container.’

‘Which describes your actual situation.’

‘Which describes my situation accurately for the first time in about four months.’ Raj counted. ‘Because the documentary had been finished since November and had been occupying the top level as though it were current.’

‘Say why you had not moved it.’

‘Because there was nowhere to move it to and because I had not thought about it.’ He was frank. ‘Which is the honest answer. It sat there because it had always sat there.’

‘And now?’

‘Now it is one item at the bottom of a short list and I know exactly what it is.’ Raj shrugged. ‘Which sounds trivial and I open the panel now and see two projects, which is what I am doing.’

‘Say the second operation.’

‘Building the new structures, which is where the interesting part is.’ He was precise. ‘Because I built them knowing what I had learned from the first one.’

‘Say what you learned.’

‘That my original structure had locations for things that never accumulated anything.’ Raj counted. ‘I made about eleven locations at the beginning of the Oaxaca project and four of them ended up holding almost nothing.’

‘Which is over-building.’

‘Which is over-building at the start, based on what I imagined the project would need rather than what it turned out to need.’ He shrugged. ‘So the new ones have four locations each and I will add more when something demands one.’

‘Which is the correct approach.’

‘Which is the approach everybody recommends and nobody follows the first time, because building a structure at the start of a project feels productive.’ Raj laughed. ‘It feels like beginning.’

‘Say the third operation.’

‘The markers, which was the fiddly part.’ He was matter of fact. ‘Because my inline markers drive the board and some of them were specific to the finished production.’

‘Say what you did.’

‘Kept the general ones, which describe stages that apply to any production.’ Raj counted. ‘And left the project-specific ones alone rather than removing them.’

‘Why not remove them?’

‘Because they are correct for the material they are on, which is finished material in an archive.’ He was definite. ‘Removing them would be editing a completed project's record to tidy an active project's view.’

‘Which is the wrong direction.’

‘Which is entirely the wrong direction and I nearly did it before stopping.’ Raj shrugged. ‘The board shows what is active. The archive keeps its own markers and nobody looks at them.’


## The vocabulary needed work too

‘I want to talk about the descriptions,’ Claire said, ‘because the structure was the visible half and the vocabulary was the harder one.’

‘Say what was wrong.’

‘Ambiguity that had crept in.’ She counted. ‘I had one meaning active, which was clear when I did one kind of work and means two different things now.’

‘Because active in one practice area.’

‘Because active in a patent context means under examination and active in a trademark context means something else entirely.’ Claire was precise. ‘And I had been applying the same word to both for about eight months without noticing.’

‘Which produces a filter that returns two kinds of thing.’

‘Which produces a filter I stopped trusting, and the not trusting is what eventually made me look.’ She shrugged. ‘I filtered for active work and got a set that was wrong and I could not immediately say why.’

‘Say what you did.’

‘Renamed it into two, each specific to its context.’ Claire counted. ‘Which took about ten minutes and which I had been avoiding for months because renaming felt drastic.’

‘Is it drastic?’

‘It is entirely undramatic. The description changes and everything carrying it carries the new one.’ She was matter of fact. ‘Which I did not know, and the not knowing is what made me avoid it.’

‘Say what else you found.’

‘Duplicates.’ Claire was rueful. ‘Two descriptions meaning the same thing, created about a year apart, applied to different material.’

‘How many?’

‘Four pairs, which had accumulated because I had forgotten what I already had.’ She shrugged. ‘Which is the standard failure and it is invisible until somebody looks at the whole vocabulary at once.’

‘Say how you looked.’

‘The distribution view, which shows every description with its count.’ Claire was precise. ‘And the small ones are where the problems are.’

‘Say what small means.’

‘Anything under about five entries, which is either something I created and abandoned or one half of a duplicate pair.’ She counted. ‘I found eleven of those. Four were duplicates, five were abandoned, and two were legitimate and rare.’

‘So most were problems.’

‘Nine out of eleven were problems and I had never looked, and looking took four minutes.’

Raj described his approach.

‘Mine was retirement rather than repair,’ he said.

‘Say it.’

‘Some of my descriptions are universal and some belonged to one production.’ He counted. ‘Which is a distinction I did not make when I created them.’

‘Say the universal ones.’

‘What kind of material something is. Interview, research, footage record, correspondence.’ Raj was matter of fact. ‘Which apply to any project I will ever do.’

‘And the specific ones.’

‘Names of regions, names of processes, names of people.’ He shrugged. ‘Which apply to one production and which cluttered the vocabulary once that production ended.’

‘Say what you did with them.’

‘Prefixed them with an abbreviation for the project.’ Raj was precise. ‘Which means they sort together, at the end, and they are still correct on the material they describe.’

‘Rather than removing them.’

‘Rather than removing them, because the material still carries them accurately and removing them would make the archive less searchable.’ He was definite. ‘The prefix moves them out of the way without destroying anything.’

‘Which is a good solution.’

‘Which is a solution I arrived at after starting to delete them and stopping,’ Raj said. ‘Which seems to be how I make most decisions.’


## What it felt like

‘I want to ask about the experience rather than the operation,’ Raj said. ‘Because you said yours was not enjoyable and mine was, and I am curious about why.’

‘Say yours first.’

‘Mine felt like clearing a desk.’ He was cheerful. ‘Genuinely satisfying. I moved the finished production into an archive and the panel looked like my actual life.’

‘Which is a small thing.’

‘Which is a small thing and I felt better about my work for about a week.’ Raj laughed. ‘Which is an absurd sentence about moving a folder and it is accurate.’

‘Say why you think it worked that way.’

‘Because I was closing something rather than rearranging it.’ He was thoughtful. ‘The documentary is finished. Putting it away is a completion rather than a disruption.’

‘Whereas mine.’

‘Whereas yours was moving your current working material while continuing to work.’

‘Which was anxious for two specific reasons and I will say both.’ Claire was frank. ‘The first is that I was afraid of losing something.’

‘Which is the obvious fear.’

‘Which is the obvious fear and it was unfounded, and I knew it was unfounded and felt it anyway.’ She shrugged. ‘Moving a location moves everything in it. Nothing was lost. I checked repeatedly.’

‘Say the second fear.’

‘That I would break how I find things.’ Claire was precise. ‘Which is a subtler worry and it is about habit rather than data.’

‘Say what you mean.’

‘I have four years of knowing where things are.’ She counted. ‘Which is a form of expertise, and reorganising deliberately destroys it.’

‘Which is a real loss.’

‘Which is a genuine loss and it lasted about a fortnight, and I underestimated how disorienting it would be.’ Claire was honest. ‘For two weeks I went to the wrong place first, constantly.’

‘And then?’

‘And then the new structure became the one I know, and I do not think about it.’ She shrugged. ‘Which is what everybody says will happen and which does not make the fortnight pleasant.’

Raj asked what got her through it.

‘Say what stopped you reversing it.’

‘Searching, entirely.’ Claire was definite. ‘Because during the fortnight when I did not know where anything was, I searched for it and found it immediately.’

‘Which is unaffected by the moving.’

‘Which is completely unaffected, because moving something changes where it lives and nothing else.’ She counted. ‘The title is the same. The descriptions are the same. The content is the same.’

‘So retrieval never degraded.’

‘Retrieval never degraded at any point during a reorganisation that touched several hundred items.’ Claire was emphatic. ‘Which is the thing I would tell anybody who is hesitating, because it removes the actual risk.’

‘Say it as advice.’

‘That you can make the structure temporarily worse without making anything harder to find.’ She was precise. ‘Which means the exercise is reversible and low-stakes, and it took me four years to believe that.’


## The reorganisation Claire got wrong

‘I want to describe a restructure that failed,’ Claire said, ‘because I have made this sound orderly and my first attempt was not.’

‘Say it.’

‘About three years ago I reorganised by stage rather than by client.’ She was frank. ‘Top level was drafting, examination, granted, and lapsed.’

‘Which sounds reasonable.’

‘Which sounds extremely reasonable and which lasted about five weeks.’

‘Say what went wrong.’

‘That things move.’ Claire counted. ‘An application in drafting becomes an application under examination, which means the entry has to move, which means somebody has to move it.’

‘Which is you.’

‘Which is me, several times a week, for eighty-one matters.’ She shrugged. ‘Which is an administrative task attached to every state change, and administrative tasks attached to state changes do not happen.’

‘So things stopped being where they said they were.’

‘Within a month my drafting location contained four things that were under examination and my examination location contained two that had been granted.’ Claire was matter of fact. ‘Which is worse than no structure, because it is a structure that lies.’

‘Say what you learned.’

‘That location should be determined by something that does not change.’ She was definite. ‘A client does not stop being that client. A practice area does not change. A stage changes constantly.’

‘Which is a rule.’

‘Which is the rule I now apply to any structural question, and it is the only thing I took from those five weeks.’ Claire counted. ‘Anything that changes belongs in a description or a marker rather than in a location.’

Raj was nodding.

‘Which is exactly why my stages are inline markers.’

‘Say it.’

‘Because a shot moves from unfilmed to filmed to edited, and the marker changes on the entry.’ He was matter of fact. ‘Which is editing a word rather than moving an object.’

‘Which happens.’

‘Which happens because it costs four seconds and moving something costs a decision.’ Raj shrugged. ‘Which sounds like a small difference and determines entirely whether anybody keeps it accurate.’


## When to do it and how

‘Practical,’ Raj said. ‘Because somebody reading this will do it badly at the wrong moment.’

‘Say your timing rule.’

‘Between things.’ He was definite. ‘I did mine after delivering a film and before starting production on the next, which is a gap of about three weeks and which is the only sensible window.’

‘Say why.’

‘Because reorganising in the middle of production means being disoriented while under deadline, which is how somebody abandons a restructure halfway.’ Raj counted. ‘And a half-finished restructure is worse than either state.’

‘Which is the actual risk.’

‘Which is the only real risk in the whole exercise, and it is a human one rather than a technical one.’ He shrugged.

Claire said hers was less clean.

‘I did not have a gap,’ she said. ‘I have eighty-one active matters and there is no moment when nothing is happening.’

‘So how did you choose?’

‘A weekend, deliberately, with nothing due on the Monday.’ Claire counted. ‘Which is the closest thing to a gap that my work permits.’

‘And it was enough?’

‘It was enough for the moving and not enough for the settling, which took a fortnight of ordinary work afterward.’ She was matter of fact. ‘Which I should have anticipated and did not.’

‘Say what you would do differently.’

‘Test the new structure before committing to it.’ Claire was precise. ‘Which I did not do and which would have taken an hour.’

‘Say what testing means.’

‘Build the new top level, move three or four recent clients into it, and work that way for a week.’ She counted. ‘Before moving anything historical.’

‘Which would have caught what?’

‘Whether four practice areas is actually the right division, which I was fairly confident about and had not verified.’ Claire shrugged. ‘It was right. I got lucky rather than being careful.’

Raj mentioned the comparison.

‘I did something during mine that helped.’

‘Say it.’

‘Kept the old structure and the new one open at the same time.’ He was matter of fact. ‘Two contexts, one showing what I had, one showing what I was building.’

‘Which is a comparison.’

‘Which is a comparison and it meant I could see what I had not yet accounted for.’ Raj counted. ‘Because moving things one at a time, it is easy to lose track of what remains.’

‘Which the old view shows you.’

‘Which the old view shows shrinking, which is also satisfying in a way I did not expect.’ He laughed.

Claire raised something about continuity.

‘There is a thing that carried me through the fortnight and I should mention it.’

‘Say it.’

‘My starred items did not move.’ She was precise. ‘Which is about eight things I open constantly, and they were exactly where they had always been throughout.’

‘Because starring is independent of location.’

‘Because starring is a property of the entry rather than of where it sits, so my working set was undisturbed while everything around it changed.’ Claire was definite. ‘Which is what made the fortnight survivable.’

‘That is a genuinely useful detail.’

‘It is the detail I would give anybody undertaking this. Whatever you use daily, star it before you begin,’ she said.

Raj added one about attachments.

‘There is a step at the end I nearly skipped.’

‘Say it.’

‘Rebuilding the attachment index after moving a substantial amount of material.’ He was matter of fact. ‘Which takes a few minutes and which I did on somebody's advice rather than my own judgement.’

‘Say why it matters.’

‘Because I moved several hundred entries carrying several thousand files, and the index that maps them is worth rebuilding after that much movement.’ Raj shrugged. ‘Everything was fine before I did it and it took four minutes and I would do it again.’

‘Which is cheap insurance.’

‘Which is cheap insurance at the end of an operation where insurance costs nothing.’


## What Raj kept from the old structure

‘There is something I did not change and I want to explain it,’ Raj said, ‘because it looks like laziness.’

‘Say it.’

‘My naming convention.’ He was matter of fact. ‘Which is the same as it was on the first production and which I considered changing and did not.’

‘Say what it is.’

‘Project abbreviation, then what the thing is, then enough words to say what it is about.’ Raj counted. ‘Which I arrived at in about my third month and which has needed no adjustment.’

‘Say why you considered changing it.’

‘Because everything else was changing and it felt inconsistent to leave one thing alone.’ He was frank. ‘Which is a terrible reason and I nearly acted on it.’

‘Which would have produced what?’

‘Two naming conventions in one collection, with a boundary somewhere in the middle that only I would know about.’ Raj shrugged. ‘Which is precisely the drift I was reorganising to fix.’

‘So changing it would have created the problem.’

‘Changing it would have created exactly the problem I was solving, in a different dimension, out of a desire for tidiness.’ He was definite. ‘Which is a thing I think happens during reorganisations constantly.’

‘Say what you mean.’

‘That the mood of restructuring is a mood in which everything looks like it should be improved.’ Raj was precise. ‘And most things should not be, and the ones that are working are the easiest to break because there is no evidence of a problem to stop you.’

Claire made a noise.

‘I did the same thing in a smaller way.’

‘Say it.’

‘I nearly renamed my four never-varying sections.’ She was rueful. ‘Which have been the same in every entry for four years and which work perfectly.’

‘Say what stopped you.’

‘Realising I would have to touch every entry to keep them consistent.’ Claire counted. ‘Which is several hundred entries for a change that improves nothing.’

‘So the cost stopped you.’

‘The cost stopped me and the cost was the only thing that stopped me, which is slightly worrying.’ She shrugged. ‘If it had been free I would have done it, and it would have been a mistake.’

‘Which is an argument for things being slightly expensive.’

‘Which is an argument I have never made about anything and which I am now considering seriously,’ Claire said.


## What Claire would not reorganise

‘There is a part I deliberately left alone,’ Claire said, ‘and I want to say why.’

‘Say it.’

‘My concluded matters.’ She was precise. ‘Which is about six hundred entries covering work that finished years ago.’

‘Say why not.’

‘Because reorganising them would change nothing about how I use them.’ Claire counted. ‘I reach concluded material by searching, always, because I do not know which client it was.’

‘Which is a different access pattern.’

‘Which is an entirely different access pattern from active work, where I know exactly whose it is.’ She was definite. ‘Active work I navigate to. Concluded work I search for.’

‘So the structure serves one and not the other.’

‘The structure serves the material I navigate and does nothing for the material I search, which means restructuring the second category is effort with no return.’ Claire shrugged.

‘Which is a good reason.’

‘Which is a good reason and I want to note that I only worked it out on the Saturday, having intended to move everything.’ She was frank. ‘I stopped about a third of the way through the concluded material.’

‘Say what made you stop.’

‘Realising I had moved two hundred entries and could not name a single occasion when it would help me.’ Claire laughed. ‘Which is a considerable amount of work for nothing and I had done it enthusiastically.’

Raj said his equivalent was the archive.

‘I did not restructure inside the archive at all,’ he said.

‘Say why.’

‘Because it is finished and its structure describes how the project actually ran.’ He was matter of fact. ‘Which is a record rather than a working arrangement.’

‘So changing it would falsify something.’

‘Changing it would produce a tidier account of a production than the production actually was.’ Raj was definite. ‘And in four years, when I want to know how I worked on that film, the messy version is the true one.’

‘Which is an argument I have not heard.’

‘Which is an argument I made up on the spot and which I believe,’ he said. ‘An archive should preserve how something was, including the parts of the arrangement that were wrong.’


## Growing out of something is not failing

Every arrangement for holding information is designed against a situation, and situations change. A structure suiting fifty items becomes unnavigable at five hundred. A distinction that separated two kinds of thing stops separating anything once a third arrives. Neither of those is a design failure; both are consequences of the arrangement having been used long enough to matter.

What people do instead of restructuring is patch, and the patches are individually reasonable. A category that does not quite fit the scheme. A location named after what it is not. A naming convention for new material differing from the one governing old material. Collectively these produce an arrangement nobody could describe, navigated by memory rather than by structure, and the drift is invisible because each accommodation made sense at the time.

Both practitioners here identified the same underlying error in their original designs, from opposite directions. One had organised her top level by the thing with the most instances, meaning clients, which produced a list of forty she had stopped reading. Inverting it so that the top level holds the thing with fewest instances, meaning kinds of work, produced four items she can take in without reading. She describes that as obvious stated plainly and as having taken four years to see.

The other had built eleven locations at the start of a project based on what he imagined it would need, four of which never accumulated anything. His new projects have four locations each and will grow when something demands it. He notes that everybody recommends this and nobody follows it the first time, because building a structure at the start of a project feels productive: it feels like beginning.

The operation itself is smaller than the anticipation. Moving a location moves everything inside it, which turns several hundred item-level operations into a few dozen container-level ones. What takes the time is deciding, particularly for the items that legitimately belong in two places, which one practitioner resolved by volume and describes as arbitrary in a handful of cases and as a decision that had to be made somehow. Her reminder to herself throughout was that location is one answer among several and does not have to be the only one.

The vocabulary needed as much attention as the structure and was harder. One practitioner found a single description meaning two different things in two contexts, which had been producing filter results she had stopped trusting without being able to say why. She also found four pairs of duplicates accumulated a year apart, invisible until she examined the whole vocabulary at once, which took four minutes and revealed that nine of eleven sparsely used descriptions were problems.

The other retired project-specific descriptions by prefixing rather than deleting them, having started to delete and stopped. His reasoning is that the material still carries them accurately and that removing them would make an archive less searchable in order to tidy an active view. The prefix moves them out of the way without destroying anything.

The emotional difference between the two experiences was substantial and neither operation differed. Archiving completed work felt like clearing a desk, and he describes feeling better about his work for a week afterward, which he acknowledges is an absurd sentence about moving a folder. Restructuring live working material felt anxious, for two distinct reasons: a fear of losing something, which was unfounded and felt anyway, and the destruction of four years of knowing where things are, which is a genuine loss of expertise and lasted a fortnight.

What made the fortnight survivable was that retrieval never degraded at any point. Moving something changes where it lives and nothing else: the title, the descriptions, the content, and the attachments are untouched, which means search continues finding everything throughout however confused the intermediate state becomes. One practitioner describes this as the thing she would tell anybody hesitating, because it converts the exercise from risky to reversible.

Two practical mechanisms carried people through. A starred working set is a property of entries rather than of locations, which means the eight things somebody opens constantly stay exactly where they are while everything around them changes. And holding the old arrangement and the new one visible together shows what remains unaccounted for, which is easy to lose track of when moving things individually.

The timing advice is unanimous and simple: between things rather than during them. The only genuine risk in the whole exercise is human, which is beginning something substantial under deadline pressure and abandoning it partway, leaving an arrangement that is neither the old one nor the new one. The corresponding advice from somebody who did not test first is to build the new top level, move a few recent items into it, and work that way for a week before touching anything historical.

Both eventually declined to restructure part of their material, for the same reason expressed differently. Concluded work is reached by searching rather than by navigating, which means the structure does nothing for it and restructuring it is effort with no return. One describes having moved two hundred entries enthusiastically before realising she could not name an occasion when it would help her. The other declines to tidy an archive on the grounds that its structure records how a project actually ran, and that in several years the messy version will be the true one.


## FAQs

### Why do knowledge structures stop working?

Because they were designed against a situation and situations change. Practitioners describe arrangements suiting fifty items becoming unnavigable at five hundred, and distinctions that separated two kinds of thing ceasing to separate anything once a third arrives. Neither is a design failure; both are consequences of an arrangement having been used long enough for the circumstances to move. Practitioners in unrelated professions arrive at the same conclusion independently, which suggests it follows from how structures behave rather than from any particular kind of work. It is one of the realisations that arrives late and seems obvious the moment somebody states it plainly. Anybody uncertain can test the question against their own arrangement in a few minutes rather than reasoning about it.

### What does structural drift look like?

A series of individually reasonable patches. A category that does not quite fit the scheme, a location named after what it is not, or a naming convention for new material differing from the one governing old material. Practitioners describe each accommodation as taking a few minutes and being sensible at the time, and the accumulation as producing something nobody could describe. The difficulty is consistently smaller than the anticipation, which is why practitioners describe having delayed far longer than the work warranted. Stating the reasoning rather than the rule is what allows somebody to adapt it to an arrangement the rule was not written for. ### How does somebody notice that their structure has failed?

Through symptoms rather than through inspection. Practitioners describe a growing reluctance to file things, a habit of leaving material in temporary locations, and a sense that finding something requires knowing where it went rather than what it is. One describes having stopped reading her own top level entirely and navigating by search, which she identifies as the structure having become storage. It is one of the realisations that arrives late and seems obvious the moment somebody states it plainly. ### Is navigating entirely by search a problem?
