Every application with a substantial amount of material eventually grows a panel down one side, and almost nobody examines what that panel is for. The conventional answer is navigation: a place to hold the structure so somebody can move between parts of it. That answer is accurate for a tool somebody opens occasionally to retrieve one thing, and it becomes inadequate the moment a tool becomes the place where somebody works.

The difference is between finding and knowing. Navigation answers a question somebody already has: where is the thing I am looking for. What a working environment requires is something prior to that, which is an understanding of the current state. What is pressing. What is unresolved. What has changed since yesterday. What is coming. Those are not retrieval questions, and a structure of nested folders answers none of them.

![The Sidebar as Your Command Center](/assets/img/feature-screenshot-placeholder.svg)

A panel that answers them is doing something categorically different from navigation. It is presenting the material through several lenses at once, each organised around a different question, none of which is where something lives. Time asks what is imminent. Priority asks what matters now. Category asks what kind of thing exists. Scale asks how much there is. And each of those views cuts across the structure rather than descending through it.
<!--more-->


What makes this arrangement powerful is that the lenses are simultaneous rather than sequential. Somebody opening a tool and seeing structure, urgency, priority, and incoming events in one glance has understood their situation before making any decision about what to do. The alternative, which is navigating to each of those in turn, costs time and, more importantly, requires somebody to remember to ask each question. Questions people must remember to ask are questions that go unasked during busy periods.

There is a second property that follows from having several lenses available, and it is the more revealing one. Different professions need different questions answered first. Somebody managing many ongoing situations needs urgency before anything else. Somebody producing a single substantial piece of work needs continuity: where was I. Somebody whose work is fed by external material needs to know what has arrived. The panel supports all of these and nobody uses all of them equally, which means the order in which somebody consults it describes what their work actually consists of.

That description turns out to be unusually honest, because nobody constructs it deliberately. A person settles into a sequence over months without deciding on it, and the sequence reflects what they have found themselves needing rather than what they would say they need if asked. Two professionals comparing their sequences are comparing their working lives, in a form neither of them assembled and neither can easily argue with.

Finally there is the ability to remove the panel entirely, which is a smaller feature carrying a disproportionate weight. Awareness and concentration are opposed states. Somebody writing something demanding does not want to know what is due; somebody orienting at the start of a day does not want a blank screen. A workspace that can be either is a workspace supporting both modes rather than compromising between them.

What follows is a conversation between two people who use the same panel in almost exactly reversed order, and who both discovered something about their own work in the comparison.


## Two orders

Marcus had suggested it, which David said afterward was characteristic.

‘You said something about the sidebar in the group and it was the third thing you mentioned,’ Marcus said. ‘And for me it would have been the first, and I have been wondering why since.’

‘Say your first.’

‘Deadlines. Always. Before anything else, every morning, without any decision on my part.’

David laughed.

‘Which is roughly my fourth and occasionally my never.’

‘Say yours.’

‘Reading, with coffee, for about fifteen minutes.’ He was matter of fact. ‘Which I know sounds like a leisure activity described as work.’

‘I would not have said that.’

‘Everybody says it eventually and I have an answer for it, which we will come to.’ David counted. ‘Then whatever I have starred, which is what I am currently working on. Then today's events. Then what I touched yesterday.’

‘And deadlines nowhere.’

‘Deadlines nowhere, because almost none of my obligations attach to a document.’ He shrugged. ‘A funeral is not a document with a date. It is an event, and events are held elsewhere.’

Marcus was quiet for a second.

‘That is the whole difference and you have just said it.’

‘Say what you mean.’

‘My obligations are attached to things.’ He counted. ‘An inspection is attached to a property. A lease renewal is attached to a unit. A certificate is attached to a building. Every deadline I have points at a document.’

‘And mine point at rooms and people.’

‘Which is not a difference in discipline. It is a difference in what our work is made of,’ Marcus said. ‘And it explains why we have opposite habits and why I assumed mine was the sensible one.’


## Marcus, in order

‘Walk me through yours properly,’ David said. ‘Because I want to understand it rather than being told the sequence.’

‘Four things and it takes about five minutes.’ Marcus shared. ‘Deadlines first, for the reason we have established.’

‘Say what you are looking for.’

‘Anything appearing in the next fortnight that I had not anticipated.’ He was precise. ‘Which is the whole purpose. I know about most of what is coming. What I do not know is that a certificate on one of the smaller buildings expires in eleven days.’

‘And that appears.’

‘And that appears without my having gone looking, which is the property that matters, because I would not have gone looking.’ Marcus shrugged. ‘Thirty-one properties is more than anybody holds in their head.’

‘Then the tabs.’

‘Then I open whatever needs attention this week, which at the moment is four properties, and they stay open.’ He described it. ‘Which means the rest of the morning I am moving between four things rather than searching for them repeatedly.’

‘Say why that matters.’

‘Because my questions are comparative.’ Marcus was definite. ‘Almost nothing about a portfolio is a question about one building. It is whether this building is behaving like the others, which requires several open at once.’

‘Which a single view cannot do.’

‘Which a single view makes into a sequence of remembering, and I have thirty-one buildings and a limited memory.’

‘And third?’

‘Filtering by category, across everything, which is a different question again.’ He counted. ‘Anything marked as maintenance. Anything marked urgent. Regardless of which building.’

‘Which cuts across your structure.’

‘Which cuts across it completely, and it is how I find out that three buildings have the same problem.’ Marcus was matter of fact. ‘Which has happened twice and which is the difference between three repairs and one conversation with a manufacturer.’

‘And fourth?’

‘The numbers, which I check weekly rather than daily.’ He shrugged. ‘How many entries, how many have files, how much space. Which is not operational. It is orientation.’

‘Say what you get from it.’

‘Whether the collection is growing the way I expect.’ Marcus was thoughtful. ‘If the count has jumped by two hundred in a month, something happened that I should know about, and twice it has been somebody sending me a batch of documents I had not processed.’

David asked about the collapsing.

‘You mentioned once that you hide it.’

‘Constantly, and it is the part I would defend hardest.’ Marcus was definite. ‘When I am actually working on one property, going through six years of records, I do not want to know what is due.’

‘Because it is a distraction.’

‘Because awareness and concentration are opposite states and I cannot be in both.’ He shrugged. ‘So the panel is for the first ten minutes and then it goes away, and it comes back when I change context.’

‘Which is a mode rather than a preference.’

‘Which is two modes, deliberately, and the ability to switch between them in one action is what makes the arrangement work.’


## David, in order

‘Now yours,’ Marcus said, ‘and I want the defence of the reading.’

‘Then I will start there.’ David was cheerful. ‘Fifteen minutes, most mornings, from a small number of sources.’

‘Say what they are.’

‘People I know who write. Some theology. And a small amount of practical material about running things, which I read least and keep because it occasionally saves me a fortnight.’

‘And the defence?’

‘That my work is made out of what I have been thinking about.’ He was serious under it. ‘I stand up on a Sunday in front of four hundred people and say something. If I have read nothing for three months, I say what I said last year.’

‘So it is input.’

‘It is the raw material, and a minister who has stopped reading is a minister who has stopped having anything to say.’ David shrugged. ‘Which people understand immediately when I put it that way and find indulgent when I say I read in the morning.’

‘That is a better framing.’

‘It is the same activity described accurately rather than apologetically.’ He counted. ‘And I write something the moment anything provokes me, before finishing the piece, because a thought has a life of about four minutes.’

‘Then the starred items.’

‘Which for me means what is currently alive.’ David shared. ‘Twelve of them at the moment. The sermon series I am working on. Two pastoral situations I am carrying. The planning entry for a large event in March.’

‘Say how they change.’

‘Constantly, and that is the point.’ He was definite. ‘A sermon comes off the list when it is delivered. A pastoral matter comes off when it resolves or when it becomes ongoing rather than acute.’

‘So it is a working set.’

‘It is a working set of about a dozen things and I rotate it deliberately, perhaps twice a week.’ David shrugged. ‘Which means opening it shows me what I am actually doing rather than what I have accumulated.’

Marcus said his was similar and meant something different.

‘Mine is eight and it means what needs attention rather than what I am working on,’ he said.

‘Say the distinction.’

‘Yours is a to-do list of substantial things. Mine is a watch list.’ He counted. ‘Properties with an unresolved problem. The financial tracking entries. Things that are not currently occupying me and that I want to see every day.’

‘So mine is what I am inside and yours is what you are watching.’

‘Which is the same mechanism doing two different jobs, and I would not have described mine as a watch list until you described yours,’ Marcus said.

‘And third for me is events,’ David continued. ‘Which is a ticker rather than a list.’

‘Say what it shows.’

‘What is happening today and tomorrow, in the sidebar, without my opening anything.’ He was matter of fact. ‘Which is where my deadlines live, because they are appointments rather than documents.’

‘Which is the difference we identified.’

‘Which is the whole difference, and it means the panel you check first and the panel I check third are doing the same job in our two lives.’

‘And fourth?’

‘What I touched yesterday, which is continuity.’ David described it. ‘Because a sermon is written across four sittings over a week and I need to recover where I stopped.’

‘Which for me is unnecessary.’

‘Say why.’

‘Because my work resumes rather than continues.’ Marcus was thinking about it. ‘I finish a property and start a property. You are eighty percent through one thing on Wednesday and you need to know what the first eighty percent said.’

‘Which is exactly it and I had not articulated it.’


## Four lenses on the same material

‘I want to go through the temporal views properly,’ Marcus said, ‘because between us we use all of them and neither of us uses all of them.’

‘Say the list.’

‘Recent, due, expiring, and the tools.’ He counted. ‘And I use two heavily, one occasionally, and one as a launcher.’

‘Which two heavily?’

‘Due and expiring, which for me are genuinely different questions.’ Marcus was precise. ‘Due is what I must act on. Expiring is what stops being valid.’

‘Say your expiring case.’

‘Leases, principally, and certificates.’ He described it. ‘A lease has an end date, and the action is not on that date, it is ninety days earlier when I decide whether to renew and on what terms.’

‘So the date is a cliff.’

‘The date is a cliff and the work is a quarter before it, which means I look at that view monthly rather than daily, because the horizon is months.’

‘I have never opened it,’ David admitted.

‘Say whether you have anything that expires.’

‘I have insurance for the building. I have several certifications for the people who work with children.’ He stopped. ‘Which are handled by somebody else, and I have just realised that means they are handled by somebody else until they are not.’

‘Which is the problem with delegated expiries.’

‘Which is a problem I am going to think about, because a volunteer's clearance lapsing is a serious matter and it currently depends on one person's diligence.’ David was matter of fact. ‘That is a genuine finding from a conversation about a sidebar.’

Marcus moved on.

‘Recent, which you use and I do not.’

‘Which is continuity, and it takes me about forty seconds each morning.’ David described it. ‘The eight or nine things I touched yesterday, in order, which puts me back inside whatever I was doing.’

‘And you read them all?’

‘I read the top two or three and the rest are recognition rather than reading.’ He shrugged. ‘Seeing the list is enough. My memory does the rest once prompted.’

‘Which is why it is fast.’

‘Which is why forty seconds replaces perhaps ten minutes of reconstruction, and the ten minutes used to happen and I thought it was normal.’

‘And the tools view?’

‘A launcher, purely.’ David was matter of fact. ‘Which sounds unremarkable and has one property I value.’

‘Say it.’

‘That the tools are in the same place as everything else.’ He counted. ‘When I want to look at what a spreadsheet contains, or generate a password for somebody, or trim a recording of a service, I do not leave.’

‘Which for me is the audio one.’

‘Say your use.’

‘Recordings from contractors, occasionally, and a walkthrough somebody sent me that was forty minutes long with four minutes of relevance.’ Marcus shrugged. ‘Which I trimmed and attached, and the alternative is a separate application and a file in a downloads folder.’


## What is worth a star

‘I want to spend a moment on the starred list,’ David said, ‘because it is the panel I would miss most and it is the one that took me longest to use properly.’

‘Say the wrong version.’

‘Everything important, which is about eighty entries.’ He laughed. ‘Which is not a list. It is a second collection.’

‘What broke it?’

‘Realising I had stopped opening it, which is the same failure everybody describes about every over-full list.’ David was matter of fact. ‘And the fix was a number.’

‘Say the number.’

‘Twelve, arbitrarily, and I hold myself to it.’ He shrugged. ‘Which means adding something requires removing something, and that decision is the entire value.’

‘Because it forces an assessment.’

‘Because it forces me to ask whether the thing I have been carrying for three weeks is still alive.’ David was definite. ‘And about half the time it is not, and I take it off, and the taking off is a small act of completion.’

‘Mine is eight and I did not set the number.’

‘Say how you arrived at it.’

‘It settled there.’ Marcus was thinking. ‘Which is different from choosing, and I am not sure it is better. Yours is deliberate and mine happens to be small.’

‘Would yours grow?’

‘It would grow if I had a bad quarter, and it has been up to fifteen, and fifteen was noticeably less useful.’ He shrugged. ‘Which suggests you have identified something and I have been getting away with it.’

David asked how he uses it during a day.

‘Constantly, and passively.’ Marcus described it. ‘It is on screen whenever the panel is open, so I see it forty times without consulting it, and what that produces is a background awareness of four unresolved things.’

‘Which you would otherwise have to hold.’

‘Which I would otherwise be holding mentally, which is exactly the load I described about intervals in another conversation.’ He was definite. ‘Anything I can put on a screen is something I am not carrying.’

‘That is my sermon list precisely.’

‘Say it.’

‘I am carrying two difficult pastoral situations at any moment and I do not want to be thinking about them continuously.’ David was quieter. ‘Seeing them listed means I am not forgetting them, which is what allows me to stop thinking about them between the moments when I am actually doing something.’

‘Which is the opposite of what people assume a reminder does.’

‘Which is exactly the opposite, and it took me a long time to understand. Writing something down is not so that I remember it. It is so that I can stop remembering it.’


## The panel David hides from himself

‘There is one thing I do that I have never told anybody,’ David said.

‘Then this is a strange place to start.’

‘It is the right place, because it is about the panel.’ He was slightly awkward. ‘I hide it during pastoral conversations.’

‘Say what you mean.’

‘Somebody comes into my office in difficulty and I am going to write something afterward.’ David counted. ‘And while they are there, the screen is either off or the panel is closed.’

‘Because they can see it.’

‘Because they can see the edge of my screen from the chair, and what is on that edge is a list of what I am carrying.’ He was serious. ‘Twelve items, four of which are people, and two of those are named.’

Marcus sat forward.

‘I had not thought about that at all.’

‘Which is reasonable, because your list says building names.’ David shrugged. ‘Mine says names of people in the congregation, and a person sitting opposite me should not learn that somebody else is in difficulty.’

‘Even without reading it.’

‘Even without reading a word, because a list of twelve items includes a name they know and that is all it takes.’ He was definite. ‘Which is the same reasoning as locking a screen and applies to a panel that is never in focus.’

‘So the panel is the exposure rather than the content.’

‘The panel is the exposure precisely because it is always visible, which is what makes it useful and what makes it a risk.’ David was matter of fact. ‘Everything else is behind something. The panel is the one part of the interface that is permanently on display.’

Marcus thought about it.

‘That applies to me and less severely,’ he said.

‘Say your case.’

‘A contractor in my office and a list of properties with problems.’ He counted. ‘Which is commercially sensitive rather than personally sensitive, and which I would not choose to show somebody I am about to negotiate with.’

‘So the same action for a different reason.’

‘The same action, and I have been leaving it open in front of people for six years without considering it.’ Marcus was frank. ‘Which is a genuinely useful thing to have learned this afternoon.’


## The numbers at the bottom

‘Say more about the numbers,’ David said, ‘because I have started looking at mine after a conversation somebody had in the group and I do not entirely know what I am looking for.’

‘Neither did I for about a year.’ Marcus was frank. ‘I looked at them, found them mildly interesting, and did nothing.’

‘What changed?’

‘I started looking at the change rather than the value.’ He was precise. ‘Which is the whole thing. The count is uninteresting. The count compared with last month is not.’

‘Say what a change tells you.’

‘Three things.’ Marcus counted. ‘An unusual jump means something arrived that I did not process deliberately. A flat month on a portfolio that is active means I have stopped recording, which has happened during two busy periods.’

‘And the third?’

‘The proportion with attachments, which for me is high and should be.’ He shrugged. ‘If that proportion drops, I am writing about properties without keeping the documents, which is exactly the failure that makes a record unusable in a dispute.’

‘So it is a check on your own practice.’

‘It is a check on whether I am doing the thing I believe I am doing, which nothing else provides.’ Marcus was definite. ‘Everything else shows me the material. This shows me my behaviour.’

David said his finding had been about consistency.

‘I looked at mine expecting to be embarrassed,’ he said.

‘Say why.’

‘Because I do not think of myself as systematic.’ He was slightly rueful. ‘I am a pastor. I think of myself as somebody who responds to whatever arrives.’

‘And?’

‘And the record is remarkably even.’ David laughed. ‘Month after month, a similar number of entries, a similar proportion with files. Which describes somebody considerably more methodical than the person I would have described.’

‘Which is a pleasant discovery.’

‘Which was a genuinely pleasant discovery and I had it from a panel I had never opened.’ He shrugged. ‘Four years of evidence that I have been doing something consistently, produced without my having intended to produce it.’

Marcus mentioned the storage figure.

‘The size number I use for one purpose.’

‘Say it.’

‘Deciding when to look at what is taking up room.’ He was matter of fact. ‘Which is a maintenance activity I do twice a year and would otherwise never think about, because nothing prompts it.’

‘So it is a trigger rather than information.’

‘It is a number that tells me when to go and look properly, which is the correct role for anything in a panel.’ Marcus was definite. ‘Panels should tell you when to do something. They should not be where you do it.’


## Making it disappear

‘We have both mentioned collapsing it,’ David said. ‘And I want to say why it matters more to me than any individual panel.’

‘Go on.’

‘Because writing a sermon and knowing what is due are incompatible activities.’ He was serious. ‘I need about ninety minutes of not being aware of anything, and a screen edge full of information is a screen edge full of information.’

‘So you remove it.’

‘I remove it and the content fills the space, and what is in front of me is the thing I am writing.’ David shrugged. ‘Which is a small mechanical change producing a considerable difference in what I can do.’

‘Mine is the same and less emotional.’

‘Say yours.’

‘When I am examining one property in detail I want the width.’ Marcus was matter of fact. ‘Six years of records, several sections, and comparisons within one entry. The panel is taking space I need.’

‘So yours is spatial and mine is attentional.’

‘Yours is attentional and mine is spatial and both are solved by the same action, which is a reasonable definition of a well-designed control.’

David raised something else.

‘There is a version of this I use that you probably do not,’ he said.

‘Say it.’

‘A tablet, at church, during events.’ He described it. ‘Which is a smaller screen and the layout adapts rather than shrinking.’

‘Say what adapts means.’

‘Everything stacks into one column instead of two.’ David was matter of fact. ‘Which means I get the content properly and reach the panel when I want it, rather than getting a miniature version of a desktop arrangement.’

‘Which is the usual failure.’

‘Which is the usual failure and it is why I stopped taking a laptop to events.’ He shrugged. ‘I am standing in a hall with two hundred people and I need the running order and the names of four people I am about to thank.’

‘Which is a retrieval task rather than a working task.’

‘Which is retrieval under mild pressure, and it works, and that is all I need from it.’ David was definite. ‘I would not write anything on it. I would not want to. But standing in a hall it is the difference between knowing somebody's name and not.’

Marcus said his portable use was different.

‘Mine is a property inspection,’ he said. ‘Standing in a unit with a phone, adding photographs and a few lines.’

‘Capture rather than retrieval.’

‘Capture rather than retrieval and the same adaptation makes it possible.’ He shrugged. ‘And the quick capture button is what I actually use, because I am holding a phone in one hand and looking at a boiler.’

‘So neither of us is doing the full thing on a small screen.’

‘Neither of us is trying to, and I think that is the honest description.’ Marcus was definite. ‘A small screen is for one task under pressure. The panel exists for a considered morning at a desk.’


## What Marcus stopped using

‘I want to describe something I abandoned,’ Marcus said, ‘because we have both been positive for an hour and somebody should hear about a panel that did not work.’

‘Say it.’

‘The category filtering, which I described earlier as my third step and which is not.’

‘You said you used it.’

‘I use it, perhaps twice a week, and for about a year I used it every morning as a fixed part of the sequence.’ He was matter of fact. ‘And it was wrong as a habit and is right as a tool.’

‘Say the difference.’

‘A daily filter by urgency tells me the same thing every day.’ Marcus shrugged. ‘The set changes slowly. Looking at it on Tuesday when I looked on Monday produces nothing, and after three weeks I was looking without reading.’

‘Which is worse than not looking.’

‘Which is considerably worse, because I was performing a check and receiving no information and believing I had checked.’ He was definite. ‘A habit that produces no information is a habit that teaches you to ignore a surface.’

‘So you moved it.’

‘I moved it from the morning to the moment I have a question, which is what it was always for.’ Marcus counted. ‘Something occurs to me about maintenance across the portfolio, and I filter, and I get an answer.’

‘So the panels divide into two kinds.’

‘Which is the thing I want to say and which I only worked out by getting it wrong.’ He was precise. ‘Some panels are for checking on a rhythm because their contents change daily. Others are for asking a question when you have one.’

‘Say which are which for you.’

‘Deadlines change daily and should be checked daily. The starred list changes weekly and is passive rather than checked.’ Marcus counted. ‘Categories change monthly and should never be a habit. Numbers change monthly and should be examined quarterly.’

David was writing something down.

‘That is a considerably more useful framework than the order we spent forty minutes on,’ he said.

‘Say why.’

‘Because the order is personal and that is general.’ He was thoughtful. ‘Match the frequency of consulting something to the frequency at which it changes, and anything consulted faster than it changes will train you to stop seeing it.’

‘Which is true of a great deal beyond this.’

‘Which is true of every dashboard anybody has ever built, and I have sat in meetings looking at figures updated annually being reviewed monthly by people who had stopped reading them.’


## The order is the finding

‘I want to come back to where we started,’ David said, ‘because I think the comparison has told me something and I want to state it.’

‘Go on.’

‘Your first panel is urgency and mine is input.’ He counted. ‘Your second is what you are watching. Mine is what I am inside. Your third is category across everything. Mine is what is happening today. And your fourth is scale and mine is continuity.’

‘Which is almost perfectly reversed.’

‘Which is reversed and neither of us chose it, and I think the order describes what our work is made of more accurately than either of us would if asked.’

‘Say what mine describes.’

‘That you are responsible for a large number of things that do not need you until they do.’ David was precise. ‘Which means your first question is always which of them needs you today, and everything else follows from that.’

‘That is uncomfortably accurate.’

‘And mine describes somebody producing a small number of substantial things.’ He shrugged. ‘Which means my first question is what am I bringing to it, and my second is where was I.’

‘So the panel order is a description of the work.’

‘The panel order is a description of the work, arrived at accidentally over years, which is what makes it worth trusting.’ David was thoughtful. ‘I did not design my mornings. They settled.’

Marcus said something he had been considering.

‘There is a thing I want to say about people who are new to this,’ he said.

‘Say it.’

‘That they will copy somebody's order and it will be wrong for them.’ He was definite. ‘Because an order that suits somebody managing thirty properties is actively unhelpful for somebody writing one thing carefully.’

‘Which is the general problem with any advice about routines.’

‘Which is the general problem and I would add something specific.’ Marcus counted. ‘The panel supports every order and none of them is the intended one, which means the correct approach is to use it badly for a month and observe what you reach for.’

‘Rather than deciding.’

‘Rather than deciding, because deciding produces something aspirational.’ He shrugged. ‘My order is not what I would have designed. It is what I found myself doing, and it is better than anything I would have designed because it reflects what my work actually requires rather than what I think it requires.’

David was nodding slowly.

‘Which is the same argument you made about the numbers.’

‘Say it back.’

‘That the accumulated evidence describes you more accurately than your own account of yourself.’ He was thinking. ‘Which appears to be true of the numbers, of the order, and probably of several other things we have not examined.’

‘Which is either reassuring or unsettling.’

‘Which is both, and I have decided to find it reassuring,’ David said, ‘on the grounds that I would rather be described by four years of behaviour than by an opinion I formed about myself in my thirties.’


## Several questions, one panel

The conventional account of a side panel is navigation, which is accurate for a tool somebody opens occasionally and inadequate for one where somebody works. The difference is between finding and knowing. Navigation answers a question somebody already has. A working environment must answer something prior: what is pressing, what is unresolved, what has changed, and what is coming.

Those are separate questions requiring separate views, and the arrangement described here presents several at once. Time in two forms, meaning what must be acted on and what ceases to be valid. Priority, in the form of a deliberately short working set. Category, cutting across the whole collection. Scale, which describes behaviour rather than material. External input. Immediate events. And the structure itself, which is the only genuinely navigational element among them.

The most interesting finding in this conversation was not any individual panel but the order. Two practitioners consult almost exactly reversed sequences. One begins with urgency because he is responsible for a large number of things that do not need him until they do, which makes his first question always which of them needs him today. The other begins with input because his work is made out of what he has been thinking about, and continues to continuity because a substantial piece of writing is produced across several sittings. Neither designed their sequence. Both settled into one, and both describe it as a more accurate account of their working life than they would have given if asked.

Several specific practices emerged that transfer regardless of order. A deliberately capped working set, held at around a dozen items, where adding something requires removing something and the removal is a small act of assessment. Both practitioners describe having previously starred everything important, producing eighty items and therefore a second collection nobody opened. The cap is what makes the panel a working set rather than an archive.

The reasoning behind that panel is worth extracting because it reverses a common assumption. Neither practitioner uses it to remember things. Both use it in order to stop remembering them: a difficult situation visible on a screen is a difficult situation somebody is not carrying mentally between the moments when they can act on it. One states it directly, that writing something down is not so that you remember it but so that you can stop.

The numerical view produced findings both had not anticipated, and the technique in both cases was to read the change rather than the value. An unusual jump indicates material arrived without being processed. A flat month during an active period indicates somebody has stopped recording, which both describe having happened during demanding stretches. A falling proportion of entries carrying documents indicates a practice degrading in a way that only becomes visible in a dispute. The panel shows behaviour where everything else shows material.

The ability to remove the panel entirely carries more weight than its size suggests, and the two practitioners want it for different reasons. One needs the width when examining a single substantial record in detail. The other needs the absence of information when writing something demanding, on the grounds that awareness and concentration are opposed states. The same control serves a spatial need and an attentional one, which is a reasonable definition of a well-designed feature.

The advice both would give somebody starting is to resist deciding. An order copied from somebody managing many concurrent situations is actively unhelpful for somebody producing one careful thing, and an order somebody designs for themselves will be aspirational rather than accurate. The recommendation is to use the panel badly for a month and observe what gets reached for, since the resulting sequence reflects what the work requires rather than what somebody believes it requires.

What runs underneath all of it is a claim about self-description that appeared three times in the conversation from three directions. The counts describe somebody's practice more honestly than their own account. The order describes their working life more accurately than they would. And the working set describes what is genuinely alive rather than what somebody has accumulated. In each case the description was produced as a side effect of doing something else, which is precisely what makes it worth trusting.


## FAQs

### What is the difference between navigation and orientation?

Navigation answers a question somebody already has: where is the thing I want. Orientation answers something prior: what is the state of my situation. Practitioners describe a structure of nested pages as excellent at the first and incapable of the second, since a hierarchy has no way to express urgency, priority, or change. A panel presenting several lenses simultaneously addresses the second, which is what a working environment requires. Practitioners in unrelated professions arrive at the same conclusion independently, which suggests it follows from how these surfaces behave rather than from any particular kind of work. It is one of the checks that costs a moment and addresses something most people have never examined about their own arrangement.

### Why does presenting several views at once matter?

Because sequential consultation requires somebody to remember to ask each question, and questions people must remember to ask go unasked during busy periods. Practitioners describe seeing urgency, priority, incoming events, and structure in one glance, which means they have understood their situation before making any decision. The simultaneity is the property rather than the individual views. Anybody uncertain can settle the question against their own material in a few minutes rather than reasoning about it. The failure it prevents develops gradually enough to be invisible without somebody naming it explicitly. Stating the rule rather than relying on instinct is what keeps it holding through a demanding period. ### How does the arrangement handle a change of role?

Poorly at first, and practitioners describe that as informative rather than as a fault. A sequence built around one kind of obligation stops producing anything useful when the obligations change, and practitioners describe the resulting sense of a pointless routine as the signal that the work has moved. The remedy is to return to observing rather than attempting to maintain something that no longer fits. ### What should somebody do this week?

Notice which panels they open, in what order, without changing anything. Practitioners describe most people being unable to state their own sequence and being surprised by it once observed. Then ask two questions of each panel: whether its contents change faster than the person consults it, and what a visitor sitting opposite would learn from seeing it. Both take a moment and both frequently produce something worth acting on. Neither question requires any change to how somebody works, and both were the source of genuine findings in this discussion: one practitioner discovered a category of delegated obligations nobody was watching, and the other discovered that his working set had been visible to every visitor for six years.


### Is any of this transferable outside a knowledge tool?

The cadence principle certainly is. Practitioners describe recognising the same failure in organisational reporting, where figures updated annually are reviewed monthly by people who long ago stopped attending to them. Any recurring review consulted faster than its subject changes trains its audience to ignore it, and the remedy is adjusting the cadence rather than exhorting anybody to pay closer attention. The working-set cap transfers similarly, since any list of current priorities held without a limit will accumulate until nobody reads it. Practitioners describe both principles as having changed how they think about material they produce for other people, not only about how they arrange their own.
