Collaboration is almost universally understood to mean simultaneity. Two people working on the same document at the same moment, seeing each other's changes appear, with the shared state maintained by something in the middle that both of them are connected to. That model is so dominant that its assumptions have become invisible, and the central assumption is that everybody involved has a reliable connection at all times.

For a substantial proportion of collaborative work, that assumption holds and the model works well. For anything conducted in the field, across substantially different time zones, or in places where connectivity is available and unreliable, it does not. And the failure mode is worse than an absence, because a tool built around continuous synchronisation behaves unpredictably when synchronisation is intermittent: somebody's work is present, then absent, then present in a form they did not expect.

![Co-Directing a Documentary With Shared Systems](/assets/img/feature-screenshot-placeholder.svg)

Documentary production is a case where the assumption fails comprehensively. A production spans locations chosen for their subject rather than their infrastructure. Crews work in places with intermittent power. Two directors may be on separate continents for months, in time zones that overlap for two hours a day, either of whom may be somewhere with no signal for a week.
<!--more-->


What such a production needs is not a smaller version of simultaneous collaboration. It needs a different model entirely, one in which two people work independently for extended periods and reconcile deliberately at intervals. That model existed before continuous connectivity and was abandoned rather than superseded, and its properties are worth recovering.

The mechanism that makes it work is convention rather than technology. If two people agree in advance on how their material will be structured, what things will be called, and how work will be described, then each of them produces material the other can read immediately. Nothing needs to be merged automatically because nothing was ever incompatible. The compatibility was established at the outset by agreement rather than enforced continuously by software.

That approach has a property the simultaneous model lacks, which is that it degrades gracefully. If two people using a shared convention lose contact for a month, they each continue working and reconcile when they next meet. If two people using a continuously synchronised system lose contact for a month, they have a problem whose size grows with the duration.

There is a second consideration that applies particularly to documentary work and to anything involving people who agreed to be recorded. Some material should not travel. A convention-based arrangement makes that straightforward: each person's material is theirs, protected by them, and the reconciliation covers what both parties agreed it would cover.

What follows is a conversation between two people designing exactly such an arrangement, before either of them has created a single record, at the beginning of a production that will run across three countries.


## Berlin and Nairobi, before anything exists

Raj had scheduled it for the only two hours that suited both of them, which was late morning in Nairobi and mid-morning in Berlin.

Idris Okonkwo appeared with a whiteboard behind him that had been wiped and re-covered several times.

‘I want to start by saying what I am worried about,’ he said. ‘Because everybody starts these conversations with what they will do and the interesting part is what they are afraid of.’

‘Say it,’ Raj said.

‘That we will build something elaborate now, while we are excited, and abandon it in week four when we are exhausted.’ Idris was matter of fact. ‘Which has happened to me on two productions.’

‘Which is the correct thing to be worried about.’

‘Say why you agree so quickly.’

‘Because it has happened to me as well and I would like to know what went wrong for you before we design anything.’ Raj counted. ‘Mine was a shared board that three people were supposed to maintain and that one person maintained.’

‘Which was you.’

‘Which was me for about six weeks and then nobody, and by the end of production it described a version of the film from March.’ He shrugged. ‘Which is worse than nothing, because people looked at it and believed it.’

Idris nodded slowly.

‘Mine was a document that four people edited simultaneously and which became unreadable.’

‘Say how.’

‘Everybody adding their own headings, everybody using slightly different words for the same thing, and nobody deleting anything.’ He counted. ‘Which took about two months and which nobody noticed happening.’

‘So we have two failures and they are opposite.’

‘Which I noticed while you were describing yours.’ Idris was thinking. ‘Yours failed because one person carried it. Mine failed because everybody did.’

‘And the common element?’

‘That neither of us agreed anything at the start.’ He was definite. ‘We both started working and let the arrangement emerge, which is how you get an arrangement nobody chose.’

Raj said that was exactly why he had asked for this call.

‘Two hours before either of us makes a single record,’ he said. ‘Which feels like an absurd thing to do and which I now think is the only thing that matters.’


## What they agreed before anything else

‘Say what you are proposing,’ Idris said.

‘Four agreements and nothing else.’ Raj counted. ‘Structure, descriptions, stages, and names.’

‘Say the first.’

‘The same top-level arrangement in both of our material.’ He shared a list. ‘Pre-production. Production. Post. Research. Contacts.’

‘Which is five and which is conventional.’

‘Which is deliberately conventional, because the value is not in the divisions being clever.’ Raj was definite. ‘It is that when you tell me something is in research, I know where it is in my own arrangement without asking.’

‘So identical rather than good.’

‘Identical is the property. Good is a bonus.’ He shrugged. ‘If we had chosen a strange arrangement and both used it, that would work too. If we choose an excellent arrangement and use different versions of it, that fails.’

Idris was writing.

‘Say the second.’

‘Descriptions, and this is the one I care about most.’ Raj listed them. ‘Interview. Location footage. Archival. Graphics. Script. Logistics.’

‘Six.’

‘Six, with the same colours, which sounds trivial and is not.’ He was precise. ‘Because when we sit down together in eight months and look at each other's material, the colours will mean the same thing to both of us.’

‘Say why the colours matter more than the words.’

‘Because I will read the words for about a month and then I will stop reading them.’ Raj was matter of fact. ‘After that I see a colour and know what something is, which is faster and which only works if we agree now.’

‘And if we disagree later?’

‘Then we change both at once, deliberately, in a conversation.’ He was definite. ‘Which is the only rule about the vocabulary. Neither of us changes it alone.’

Idris raised something.

‘I want to add one and I want you to tell me if it is a mistake.’

‘Say it.’

‘A description for who is responsible.’ Idris counted. ‘Because there will be scenes I am leading and scenes you are leading, and I would like that visible.’

‘Which is not what descriptions are usually for.’

‘Which is not what they are usually for and it is a fact about a piece of work rather than about its content, which I think qualifies.’ He was matter of fact.

Raj considered it.

‘I think it is right and I want to say the reason it is right, because it is not obvious.’ He counted. ‘Ownership does not change. A scene I am leading is a scene I am leading for the whole production.’

‘Which makes it a description rather than a stage.’

‘Which makes it exactly a description, and if it changed weekly it would belong somewhere else entirely.’

‘So two of those. One for each of us.’

‘Two, and that is the last one we add, because eight is already more than most people can hold.’


## Stages and names

‘Third agreement,’ Raj said. ‘Stages, which drive the board.’

‘Say what you use.’

‘Seven, and they are the actual phases a scene passes through.’ He listed them. ‘Research. Scheduled. Filmed. Transcribed. Rough cut. Reviewed. Final.’

‘Which is a pipeline.’

‘Which is a pipeline and which is the thing that makes a board useful rather than decorative.’ Raj was definite. ‘Because a scene is always in exactly one of those and moving between them is what production is.’

‘Say why these are not descriptions.’

‘Because they change constantly.’ He counted. ‘A scene is scheduled and then filmed and then transcribed, sometimes within a fortnight. Which means it is a state rather than a property.’

‘And states go in markers.’

‘States go in markers written into the material, and properties go in descriptions.’ Raj was precise. ‘Which is a rule I would apply to any organisational question and which I learned by getting it wrong.’

Idris asked about a case.

‘What happens when something goes backward?’

‘Which happens constantly and which is the reason a board is worth having.’ He was matter of fact. ‘A scene comes out of rough cut and goes back to filmed because we need a pickup shot.’

‘So the marker changes.’

‘The marker changes, the card moves, and both of us can see it moved when we next compare.’ Raj shrugged. ‘Which is a considerably better record of a production than anybody's memory of what happened in June.’

‘Fourth agreement,’ Idris said. ‘Names.’

‘Which is the one people skip and which is the one that determines whether any of this works.’

‘Say what you propose.’

‘Three parts.’ Raj counted. ‘Who made it, what it is, and what it is about.’

‘Say the first properly.’

‘Two letters at the front. Yours or mine.’ He shrugged. ‘Which is not about credit. It is that when we sit together with two arrangements open, I need to know at a glance whose record I am looking at.’

‘Which the colours will not tell me.’

‘Which nothing else tells you, because the descriptions describe the content and the content is the same production.’ Raj was definite.

‘And the rest?’

‘A number for the segment, and then enough words to say what it actually concerns.’ He counted. ‘Which is the part people economise on and which determines whether anything is findable.’

‘Say what you mean by enough.’

‘An interview named after a person tells me nothing about what is in it.’ Raj was precise. ‘An interview named after a person and a subject is reachable from either direction, and the difference is four words.’

Idris made a noise.

‘I have four years of material named after people.’

‘Which is a problem you will have later and not on this production,’ Raj said. ‘And which I had, and which cost me an afternoon per project to correct partially.’


## Setting up in Nairobi

‘I want to actually do this rather than agree to it,’ Idris said. ‘Because I have agreed to arrangements in meetings and never implemented them.’

‘Then do it now while I watch.’

Idris opened a file.

‘That is the whole thing?’

‘That is the whole thing.’ Raj was matter of fact. ‘One file. Nothing installed.’

‘Say where my material goes.’

‘Wherever you choose, on your machine.’ He counted. ‘It will ask you for a folder and then it writes there.’

Idris chose a folder and something explained what was happening.

‘I have read that,’ he said. ‘And I want to check I have understood it. This is on my laptop.’

‘Entirely on your laptop.’

‘Nothing goes anywhere.’

‘Nothing goes anywhere at any point.’ Raj was definite. ‘Which for what we are making matters more than it usually would.’

‘Say why more.’

‘Because we will be filming in communities where people are agreeing to be recorded on the basis of what we tell them.’ He was serious. ‘And I would like to be able to tell them accurately.’

Idris built the structure, which took about four minutes.

‘Five locations, six descriptions, two more for who leads.’

‘Say the colours back to me.’

Idris read them out and they matched.

‘Now the board,’ Raj said.

‘Which requires material.’

‘Which requires one record with a marker in it, and then you will see what it does.’

Idris created something, typed a stage marker into it, and a column appeared.

‘It made the column.’

‘It made the column from the marker, which means neither of us configures a board.’ Raj was matter of fact. ‘We write markers and the board assembles itself.’

‘Which means our boards will match.’

‘Which means our boards will match automatically as long as our markers match, which is why we agreed them.’ He shrugged. ‘That is the entire mechanism and there is nothing else to it.’

Idris was quiet for a moment.

‘I have used something that required four people to maintain a board manually,’ he said.

‘Everybody has.’

‘And the reason it failed is that maintaining a board is a separate task from doing the work.’ Idris was working it out. ‘Whereas this is the same task. I write the marker because I am recording what happened, and the board is a consequence.’

‘Which is why it survives a difficult month.’

‘Which is why I believe this one might,’ he said.


## The power cuts

‘There is something I should tell you about working here,’ Idris said.

‘Say it.’

‘The connection is good and the power is not.’ He was matter of fact. ‘Which means my internet is excellent for six hours and then absent for two, unpredictably.’

‘Which is the worst version.’

‘Which is considerably worse than not having it, because everything I use assumes it and then behaves strangely.’ Idris counted. ‘A document that saves to somewhere else and then does not. A board that shows me a version from an hour ago and does not say so.’

‘Say what happens when the power returns.’

‘Sometimes it reconciles and sometimes it asks me which version I want, which is a question I cannot answer because I do not know what the other version contains.’ He shrugged. ‘I have lost about a day's work to that across the last year.’

‘None of that will happen here.’

‘Say why not, precisely, because I have been told that before.’

‘Because nothing is being fetched.’ Raj was definite. ‘The file is on your machine. Your material is on your machine. There is no other version anywhere for it to disagree with.’

‘So a power cut is a power cut.’

‘A power cut is a machine turning off, and when it comes back your material is where it was.’ He counted. ‘Which is what happens with a text file and which is what this is.’

Idris tested it, which Raj said afterward was the correct instinct.

He disconnected his machine from the network entirely, worked for four minutes, and reported that nothing had changed.

‘That is a considerably better answer than an explanation,’ he said.

‘Which is what I would have done in your position.’

‘Say what happens in the field,’ Idris asked. ‘Because that is the real question.’

‘Say your field.’

‘Rooftop farms in a district with no coverage at all, four days at a time, with a laptop in a bag.’ He was matter of fact. ‘Which is where the actual production happens.’

‘Then you work exactly as you would anywhere.’ Raj was definite. ‘Which is the property that made me adopt this and which I did not appreciate until I was on a hillside in Portugal wanting a regulatory detail.’

‘And you had it.’

‘I had it, from inside an attached document, with no signal, which is when I stopped thinking of this as a note-taking arrangement.’


## Reconciling

‘Now the difficult part,’ Idris said. ‘Because we have two separate arrangements and at some point they have to agree with each other.’

‘Which is the question I have thought about most.’

‘Say what you propose.’

‘That we do not attempt continuous agreement and instead reconcile deliberately.’ Raj counted. ‘Which is a decision rather than a limitation and I want to defend it.’

‘Go on.’

‘Because continuous agreement between two people on two continents with intermittent power is a thing that will fail at the worst moment.’ He was definite. ‘And a thing that fails intermittently is worse than a thing that does not exist, because you rely on it.’

‘Which is exactly my experience.’

‘So instead: we work independently, and we reconcile when we are together, and between those we trust the convention.’

Idris asked what reconciling actually involves.

‘Say the mechanics.’

‘Sitting in a room with two machines and comparing.’ Raj was matter of fact. ‘Which sounds primitive and takes about two hours and is the most useful two hours of a production month.’

‘Say what happens in the two hours.’

‘We each open our boards and look at what has moved.’ He counted. ‘Anything you have moved to filmed, I move on mine. Anything I have moved, you move on yours.’

‘Which is manual.’

‘Which is manual and which is about forty updates across a month, and each one takes four seconds.’ Raj shrugged. ‘Which is a considerably smaller amount of work than the arrangement it replaces.’

‘And the material itself?’

‘Anything either of us has written that the other needs, we copy.’ He was precise. ‘Which is a matter of copying files, because that is what these are.’

‘Say more about that.’

‘Every record has a file. The attachments are files. It is a folder with files in it and copying is copying.’ Raj was definite. ‘Which is why a drive works and why nothing more complicated is required.’

Idris was thinking.

‘Say what happens between meetings, because we will not meet for four months at a time.’

‘A drive that travels with whoever is moving, and a protocol for what goes on it.’

‘Say the protocol.’

‘Only what the other person needs.’ Raj counted. ‘Not everything. Because a copy of everything is a second copy of everything, and two complete copies that both change is exactly the problem we are avoiding.’

‘So a selection.’

‘A selection, made deliberately, of the things that affect the other person's work.’ He was precise. ‘Which for us is scene notes, transcripts, and anything about scheduling.’

‘And not?’

‘Not my personal reflections on a shoot. Not your correspondence with a funder.’ Raj shrugged. ‘Which are ours and which do not affect what the other person does next week.’

Idris asked the question Raj said afterward was the sharpest of the call.

‘What happens if we both edit the same thing?’

‘We agree in advance that we do not,’ Raj said. ‘Which is the whole answer and which sounds evasive and is not.’

‘Say why not.’

‘Because ownership is a description on every record and we agreed it ten minutes ago.’ He counted. ‘A scene I lead is mine to write. You read it and comment separately, in your own material, and we discuss.’

‘So there is no shared editing.’

‘There is no shared editing and I regard that as a feature.’ Raj was definite. ‘Two people editing the same paragraph produces a paragraph neither of them would have written.’

‘Which I have seen.’

‘Which everybody has seen and which nobody calls a problem because the tool permitted it.’


## The board as a shared instrument

‘I want to come back to the board,’ Idris said, ‘because I think it is doing more than either of us has said.’

‘Say what you mean.’

‘It is the only place where the whole production is visible at once.’ He counted. ‘Everything else is a record about one thing. The board is the state of everything.’

‘Which is exactly what I use it for.’

‘Say how often.’

‘Daily during production, weekly otherwise.’ Raj was matter of fact. ‘And the thing I look at is not the columns. It is the distribution.’

‘Say the distribution.’

‘How many are stuck.’ He was precise. ‘If eleven scenes are sitting in filmed and none has moved to transcribed for a fortnight, that is a fact about the production that no individual record contains.’

‘Which is a bottleneck.’

‘Which is a bottleneck visible only in aggregate, and which I would previously have noticed about three weeks later.’

Idris raised the ownership marking.

‘With the responsibility descriptions on the cards, I will be able to see whose scenes are stuck.’

‘Which is a thing I want you to be able to see about mine.’ Raj was definite. ‘Because a co-director who cannot see that my half of the production has stalled is a co-director who finds out in a meeting.’

‘Which is late.’

‘Which is late and which is how the relationship deteriorates.’ He was frank. ‘I would rather you see it on a board in Nairobi than hear it from me defensively in April.’

Idris was quiet for a moment.

‘That is the most useful thing you have said this morning and it is not about software.’

‘Say what you mean.’

‘That you are choosing to be visible.’ He was matter of fact. ‘Which is a decision about how we work together, and the arrangement just happens to support it.’

‘Which is what all of this is,’ Raj said. ‘We spent an hour agreeing on colours and what we were actually agreeing was how honest we intend to be with each other about what is going wrong.’


## What Idris brought that Raj did not have

‘I want to describe something I do that you have not mentioned,’ Idris said, ‘because I suspect it is the thing I will contribute to this arrangement.’

‘Say it.’

‘I keep a record of who introduced me to whom.’

Raj had not expected that.

‘Say what you mean.’

‘Every person I film was reached through somebody.’ Idris counted. ‘A cooperative officer. A neighbour. Somebody's cousin. And I record the chain.’

‘Say why.’

‘Because in this kind of work the introduction is the permission.’ He was serious. ‘Somebody agrees to talk to me because a person they trust asked them to, and if I forget who that was, I have lost the thing that made the conversation possible.’

‘Which matters when?’

‘When I go back.’ Idris was matter of fact. ‘Which on a production like this is four or five times, and each time I am arriving as somebody's guest rather than as a filmmaker.’

Raj was thinking.

‘I do not do this and I should.’

‘Say what you do instead.’

‘I remember it, for the duration of a production, and then it evaporates.’ He was frank. ‘Which means that four years later I have footage of somebody and no idea how I came to be in their kitchen.’

‘Which is a problem if you ever want to go back.’

‘Which is a problem I have actually had, on a production I would like to return to, and I could not work out who to contact first.’

Idris described the mechanism.

‘One record per person, with a section for the chain.’ He counted. ‘Who introduced us, what the relationship is, and what I owe them.’

‘Say the last one.’

‘Somebody who introduces you has spent something.’ Idris was precise. ‘Their standing with the person, usually. And I record what I have been given, because it is a debt and I would rather know I have it.’

‘That is not a production record.’

‘That is not a production record and it is the most important thing in my material,’ he said. ‘Because everything else can be reshot and this cannot be rebuilt.’

Raj was quiet for a moment.

‘We are adding it to the convention.’

‘Say what you mean.’

‘Both of us, in Contacts, with the same structure.’ He was definite. ‘Because if I go back to a location alone in eighteen months I need to know who you spoke to first, and that is not something I can ask you at short notice from Berlin.’

‘Which is exactly why I keep it.’


## What does not travel

‘There is a category we have not discussed and it is the one that matters most,’ Raj said.

‘Say it.’

‘Material about people who agreed to be filmed on particular terms.’

Idris became noticeably more attentive.

‘Say the specific cases.’

‘Somebody who asked not to be identified.’ Raj counted. ‘Location details for a community that would rather not be found. Contact information for people who introduced us to somebody else.’

‘Which on this production will be substantial.’

‘Which on a production about people growing food in a city where some of them are doing it on land they do not formally hold will be a considerable proportion.’ He was serious. ‘Which I want to establish before we film anything.’

‘Say what you propose.’

‘That sensitive records are protected individually, in each of our arrangements, with a password only the person who created it knows.’

Idris considered that.

‘So you cannot open mine.’

‘I cannot open yours and you cannot open mine, and that is the point rather than a limitation.’ Raj was definite.

‘Say why that is the point.’

‘Because if I could open yours, then somebody who obtained my machine could open yours.’ He counted. ‘Which extends the risk from one person's equipment to two, for no benefit.’

‘And when we need to discuss something sensitive?’

‘We discuss it. Verbally, or on the encrypted messaging we already use.’ Raj was matter of fact. ‘Which is where that conversation belongs and which leaves no permanent record in either arrangement.’

‘So the sensitive material never travels on the drive.’

‘The sensitive material never travels on the drive, which means a lost drive is a lost drive rather than a disclosure.’ He was definite. ‘Which is a thing I want us both to be able to say to somebody who asks.’

Idris said something after a moment.

‘I want to add something to that and I want to be careful about how I say it.’

‘Go on.’

‘Some of what we will hold is not sensitive in a security sense and is sensitive in a human sense.’ He was precise. ‘Somebody telling me about their family while we are setting up. Somebody's reason for growing what they grow.’

‘Which is context.’

‘Which is context and which they offered while a camera was not running, and which does not belong in a production record at all.’ Idris was definite.

‘So you would not record it.’

‘I would not record it, and I want us to agree that neither of us does, because I have worked with people who wrote down everything on the grounds that it might be useful.’

‘Which it might be.’

‘Which it might be and which is not a good enough reason.’ He shrugged. ‘Somebody talking to me while I set up a tripod is not giving an interview and I would rather they did not have to know the difference.’

Raj was quiet for a second.

‘That is a better standard than mine and I am adopting it,’ he said.

‘Say what yours was.’

‘Record it and decide later.’ Raj was frank. ‘Which is defensible and which puts the decision at the wrong moment, because later I am editing and looking for material.’

‘Which is when the temptation is strongest.’

‘Which is exactly when it is strongest, and deciding at the moment of hearing is a considerably better arrangement.’


## Where the creative work sits

‘We have spent ninety minutes on logistics,’ Idris said, ‘and I want to talk about the part I actually care about.’

‘Say it.’

‘Where does the thinking go.’

‘Into records with the same structure every time, which is the thing I would recommend most strongly.’ Raj shared one. ‘Four sections on every scene.’

‘Say them.’

‘Visual concept. What is said. Sound. Logistics.’ He counted. ‘Which never vary and which are in that order for a reason.’

‘Say the reason.’

‘Because visual comes first in a documentary and logistics comes last, and putting logistics at the top of a scene record makes it a production document rather than a creative one.’ Raj was definite. ‘Which sounds like nothing and determines what I think about when I open it.’

‘So the order is an argument.’

‘The order is an argument about what a scene is, made once, and then made silently every time either of us opens anything.’

Idris was interested.

‘Say what happens when I read one of your scene records.’

‘You read the visual concept first, because it is at the top, which is what I want.’ He shrugged. ‘And you know exactly where the logistics are if that is what you came for.’

‘Which means I can read it in two ways.’

‘Which means you can read it as a creative document or consult it as a production one, from the same record, depending on what you need.’ Raj was precise. ‘Which only works because the structure never changes.’

Idris asked about disagreement.

‘What happens when I think your visual concept is wrong?’

‘You write that in your own material and we talk about it.’ Raj was matter of fact. ‘Which is what I said earlier about not editing each other's records.’

‘Say why that is better than commenting.’

‘Because a comment on somebody's document is a note in the margin of their thinking.’ He counted. ‘And a record in your own material is your own argument, developed properly, which is a different thing and usually a better one.’

‘Which is more work.’

‘Which is considerably more work and produces a considerably better conversation.’ Raj was definite. ‘I would rather you spent twenty minutes writing why you disagree than four minutes annotating.’

Idris laughed.

‘You would rather I disagreed with you at length.’

‘I would rather you disagreed with me having thought about it, which is what length is a proxy for,’ he said. ‘And I would rather have that in writing before we are in an edit suite with a deadline.’


## Collaboration at the speed of trust

Collaboration is almost universally taken to mean simultaneity: two people in the same document at the same moment, with a shared state maintained by something in the middle that both are connected to. The assumptions inside that model are invisible because it works so widely, and the central one is continuous connectivity for everybody involved.

Documentary production is a case where the assumption fails completely. Locations are chosen for their subject rather than their infrastructure, power is intermittent, and two directors may be on separate continents with a two-hour daily overlap. What such a production needs is not a reduced version of simultaneous collaboration but a different model: independent work over extended periods, reconciled deliberately at intervals.

The mechanism that makes it work is agreement rather than software. The two practitioners here spent two hours deciding four things before either created a single record: an identical structure, an identical vocabulary with identical colours, an identical set of production stages, and a naming convention with three parts. The property that matters is identity rather than quality, as one of them stated directly: a strange arrangement both people use works, while an excellent arrangement used in two variants does not.

Their diagnosis of previous failures shaped the design. One had maintained a shared board alone for six weeks and then not at all, leaving something that described a production from months earlier and that people still consulted. The other had watched a shared document become unreadable as four people added their own headings and their own words for the same things. The failures were opposite, one from a single person carrying it and one from everybody carrying it, and both had the same cause: nothing was agreed at the start and the arrangement emerged rather than being chosen.

Two rules distinguish what goes where. Properties that do not change become descriptions, which is why responsibility for a scene is a description rather than a stage. States that change constantly become markers written into the material, which is why production phases are markers, and which means the board assembles itself from work somebody was doing anyway rather than requiring separate maintenance. One of them identifies that as the reason the arrangement will survive a difficult month: recording what happened and updating the board are the same action rather than two.

Reconciliation is deliberate and periodic rather than continuous, which is a decision rather than a constraint. Two hours in a room comparing boards, moving perhaps forty cards, and copying whatever the other person needs, which is a matter of copying files because that is what the material is. Between meetings, a drive carries a deliberate selection rather than everything, since two complete copies that both change is precisely the problem being avoided.

The question of simultaneous editing is answered by not doing it. Ownership is recorded on every item, agreed at the outset, and a person disagreeing with somebody else's work writes their own argument in their own material rather than annotating. One practitioner defends this as producing a better conversation, since a marginal comment is a note on somebody's thinking while a developed argument is an actual position.

Sensitive material does not travel at all. Each person protects their own with a password the other does not have, which means neither can open the other's records, which is the point rather than a limitation: a route between two people's material extends the risk from one machine to two for no benefit. Sensitive matters are discussed verbally, which leaves no permanent record anywhere and means a lost drive is a lost drive rather than a disclosure.

The most significant exchange was not about mechanism. One practitioner proposed that neither of them record what people say while a camera is not running, on the grounds that somebody talking during setup is not giving an interview and should not have to know the difference. The other adopted it immediately, having previously operated a record-and-decide-later standard, and identified the problem with his own approach: later is when he is editing and looking for material, which is when the temptation is strongest.

What the two hours produced was not primarily a system. One of them says so directly at the end: they spent an hour agreeing on colours and what they were actually agreeing was how honest they intended to be with each other about what was going wrong. The visible ownership on a shared board means a stalled half of a production is visible to a partner in another country, which he describes as preferable to explaining it defensively in a meeting several weeks later.


## FAQs

### Why does simultaneous collaboration fail for field production?

Because it assumes continuous connectivity for everybody involved. Practitioners describe locations chosen for their subject rather than their infrastructure, intermittent power, and collaborators on separate continents. One notes that a tool built around continuous synchronisation behaves unpredictably when synchronisation is intermittent, which is worse than an absence because people rely on it. Practitioners working across unreliable infrastructure arrive at the same conclusion independently, which suggests it follows from the conditions rather than from any particular field. It is one of the agreements that costs an afternoon at the outset and cannot be retrofitted once two people have been working separately for months. Anybody uncertain can test the question against a previous collaboration rather than reasoning about it in the abstract.

### What is the alternative model?

Independent work reconciled deliberately at intervals. Practitioners describe this as a model that existed before continuous connectivity and was abandoned rather than superseded. Its principal property is that it degrades gracefully: two people who lose contact for a month continue working and reconcile later, rather than accumulating a problem whose size grows with the duration.
