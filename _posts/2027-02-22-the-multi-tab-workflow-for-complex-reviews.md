Almost every professional decision of any consequence is comparative. Which of these should receive the resource. Which of these is behaving unlike the others. Whether this document says the same thing as that one. The question is rarely about a single item in isolation, and yet almost every tool for holding information presents one item at a time and expects the person to supply the comparison from memory.

That expectation is where the cost sits, and it is larger than it appears. A person examining one thing, then another, then a third is not comparing them. They are forming an impression of each and then comparing the impressions, which is a different operation with different failure modes. Impressions compress, they simplify, and they are influenced by the order in which they were formed.

![The Multi-Tab Workflow for Complex Reviews](/assets/img/feature-screenshot-placeholder.svg)

The specific distortions are well understood and rarely applied to one's own work. Whatever was examined most recently carries disproportionate weight. Whatever was striking about the first item becomes the frame through which the others are read. Details that were present in one and absent in another become invisible, because absence is not something an impression records.
<!--more-->


There is a further cost that people register as fatigue rather than as anything structural. Holding two detailed pictures in mind while forming a third is genuinely demanding, and the demand grows faster than the number of items. Somebody comparing five things sequentially is doing something close to impossible, which is why most people quietly reduce the comparison to two or three even when the decision concerns more.

The alternative is to stop requiring the memory step. If several things can be present at once, each in its own state, the comparison happens by looking rather than by recalling. That sounds like a matter of convenience and is not. It changes which differences are noticeable, because a difference visible side by side may be entirely invisible in sequence, particularly when the difference is an absence.

What that requires is more than displaying several documents. Each context must hold its own arrangement: its own filter, its own ordering, its own position within a set. A comparison is frequently between different views of similar material rather than between the material itself, and a tool that shows several things in a single shared arrangement has not solved the problem so much as spread one view across a screen.

There is also a temporal dimension worth naming. Substantial reviews are not completed in one sitting. Somebody assembles a working context, examines it, is interrupted, and returns. The cost of reassembling that context is what determines whether the review happens in one careful session or in four fragmented ones, and most people underestimate how much of their reviewing time is spent rebuilding a state they had an hour earlier.

What follows is a conversation between two people who conduct entirely different kinds of review, one legal and one financial, and who have each arrived at the same arrangement without having discussed it.


## Two people who compare things for a living

Claire had proposed it after a remark Marcus made in the group.

‘You said something about four properties being open at once,’ she said, ‘and you said it as though it were unremarkable.’

‘Which it is now and was not eighteen months ago.’

‘Say what changed.’

‘That I stopped comparing from notes.’ Marcus was matter of fact. ‘Which sounds like nothing and is the whole of it.’

‘Then we have the same story.’ Claire was precise. ‘Mine is patent applications rather than buildings and the mechanism is identical.’

‘Say what you compare.’

‘Families.’ She counted. ‘A group of applications covering different aspects of one technology, filed at different times, which have to be consistent with each other and frequently are not.’

‘And the inconsistency is the thing you are looking for.’

‘The inconsistency is precisely the thing, and it is invisible sequentially.’ Claire was definite. ‘Because reading application A and then application B, I read the second one expecting the first, and expectation is exactly what makes a discrepancy disappear.’

‘Say that again.’

‘I read what I expect to read.’ She shrugged. ‘Which everybody does and which is fatal in my work, because the discrepancy I am looking for is a small variation in language that I will smooth over without noticing.’

‘Whereas side by side.’

‘Whereas side by side there is nothing to smooth. The two are present and either they say the same thing or they do not.’ Claire was emphatic. ‘Which is not a better version of the same activity. It is a different activity.’

Marcus said his was the same claim about numbers.

‘Mine is not language, it is behaviour,’ he said.

‘Say it.’

‘Whether this building is behaving like the others.’ He counted. ‘Which is almost every question I have about a portfolio, and it is never a question about one building.’

‘So the unit of analysis is the comparison.’

‘The unit of analysis is the comparison and I spent about six years treating each building as a unit and being puzzled that my conclusions felt thin.’ Marcus shrugged. ‘They were thin because I was answering the wrong question carefully.’


## Four buildings on a Tuesday morning

‘Show me,’ Claire said. ‘Because I want to see somebody else's arrangement rather than describing mine into a vacuum.’

Marcus shared.

‘Quarterly review,’ he said. ‘Which happens over about two days and which used to happen over a week badly.’

‘Say what is open.’

‘Four contexts.’ He pointed. ‘One building with maintenance that has been recurring. One approaching a lease decision. One I bought recently. And one that is doing nothing wrong, which is there deliberately.’

‘Say why the last one.’

‘Because I need a baseline.’ Marcus was definite. ‘Three problem buildings compared with each other tells me which is worst. One ordinary building tells me whether any of them is actually unusual.’

‘Which is a control.’

‘Which is exactly a control and I did not include one for about a year, and my conclusions were consistently more alarming than the situation warranted.’ He shrugged. ‘Everything looks like a problem when you only look at problems.’

Claire made a noise of recognition.

‘Say what each one is showing.’

‘Different things, which is the part that matters.’ Marcus went through them. ‘The maintenance building is filtered to anything marked maintenance in the last month, sorted by date.’

‘So a narrow view.’

‘A deliberately narrow view because the question is recent, and everything older is noise for this purpose.’ He counted. ‘The lease building is filtered to documents rather than notes, because what I need is the agreements.’

‘Which is a different filter entirely.’

‘A completely different filter, on a different field, sorted differently.’ Marcus was emphatic. ‘Which is the property that makes this useful. If the four contexts shared one arrangement I would have four windows onto the same view, which is nothing.’

‘Say the third.’

‘The acquisition is everything, unsorted, because I do not yet know what I am looking for.’ He shrugged. ‘Which is the correct arrangement for something I bought four months ago and have not fully read.’

‘And the baseline?’

‘Same filter as the maintenance building, deliberately, so the comparison is like for like.’ Marcus was precise. ‘Which is the only one where matching the arrangement matters, and it matters completely.’

Claire asked what he actually does with them.

‘Say the operation.’

‘I look across.’ He was matter of fact. ‘Which sounds absurd as a description of professional work and is accurate.’

‘Say what you see.’

‘Whether the maintenance building has four entries this month and the baseline has one.’ Marcus counted. ‘Which is a ratio I take in without calculating anything, because both are in front of me.’

‘And previously?’

‘Previously I would have counted one, written it down, counted the other, and compared two numbers.’ He shrugged. ‘Which gives the same answer and takes eleven times as long and I did it perhaps twice a year rather than every quarter.’

‘So the frequency changed.’

‘The frequency changed because the operation became cheap, and the frequency is where the value is.’ Marcus was definite. ‘A comparison I make quarterly beats a better comparison I make annually.’


## Three applications and a discrepancy

‘Now yours,’ Marcus said.

Claire shared.

‘A family,’ she said. ‘Three applications, filed across about eighteen months, covering related aspects of one invention.’

‘Say what has to be true across them.’

‘That the terminology is consistent, that the technical descriptions do not contradict, and that nothing in one undermines a position taken in another.’ Claire counted. ‘Which sounds mechanical and is the entire risk.’

‘Say the risk.’

‘An examiner or an opponent reads all three.’ She was precise. ‘And a variation in how we described a component across two applications is a gift to somebody arguing that we did not know what we were claiming.’

‘Which is a real thing.’

‘Which is an extremely real thing and I have watched it happen to somebody else's family.’ Claire was definite. ‘A single word used differently in two documents, and eighteen months of argument about what was actually intended.’

‘So you check.’

‘I check, and I check by having them present rather than by reading them in sequence.’ She was emphatic. ‘Because the check requires noticing that two things differ, and difference is a relation. You cannot perceive a relation in one thing at a time.’

‘Say the arrangement.’

‘Three contexts, each on one application, each sorted differently because each question is different.’ Claire went through them. ‘The one under examination is sorted by date, because I need the sequence of correspondence.’

‘Which is chronological.’

‘Which has to be chronological because an examination is a conversation and the order is the meaning.’ She counted. ‘The second is sorted by category, because I am looking for document types rather than a sequence.’

‘Which is a different question about similar material.’

‘Which is exactly it, and it is the thing I could not do before.’ Claire was definite. ‘Two applications, one arranged by time and one by kind, both visible, because the questions I am asking of them differ.’

‘And the third?’

‘Filtered to prior art, which for that application is the live issue.’ She shrugged. ‘And the filter stays while I move between the others, which is the property everything depends on.’

Marcus asked about the office action.

‘Say the actual decision you made.’

‘Which of three to prioritise, with a limited number of hours this fortnight.’ Claire was matter of fact. ‘One had received a rejection with a deadline. Two were pending.’

‘Which sounds obvious.’

‘Which sounds obvious and was not, because the rejection turned on a claim construction that also appears in one of the pending applications.’ She was precise. ‘Which I saw because both were open.’

‘So the response affects both.’

‘The response affects both, and drafting it without having looked at the pending one would have produced an argument that damaged an application I was not thinking about.’ Claire was definite. ‘Which is the kind of error that is invisible until somebody points it out three years later.’

‘And you saw it because.’

‘Because the phrase was on my screen twice, in two documents, in two contexts, and my eye went to it.’ She shrugged. ‘Which is not analysis. It is perception, and perception requires simultaneity.’


## Different questions in different contexts

‘I want to be precise about the filtering,’ Marcus said, ‘because I think this is where people misunderstand what the arrangement is for.’

‘Say the misunderstanding.’

‘That it is for looking at several things.’ He was definite. ‘Which it is not. It is for asking several different questions at once.’

‘Say the distinction.’

‘Four windows onto the same view is one view spread across a screen.’ Marcus counted. ‘Four contexts each narrowed differently is four analyses running in parallel.’

‘Give me the concrete version.’

‘One is filtered to a category and a recent period, which answers what has been going wrong lately.’ He went through them. ‘One is filtered to a document type, which answers what agreements exist. One is unfiltered, which answers what is here at all.’

‘Three different questions.’

‘Three completely different questions about three buildings, and the answers are visible together, which is where the comparison happens.’ Marcus shrugged. ‘If they all showed everything I would be reading rather than comparing.’

Claire said hers divided the same way.

‘Mine is category filtering across two and sorting differently across all three.’

‘Say the categories.’

‘One context filtered to examiner correspondence.’ She counted. ‘Another filtered to prior art research. Which are the two halves of an argument and which I need to hold against each other.’

‘Which is the objection and the answer.’

‘Which is what the examiner says and what I have found, and the work is entirely in the space between those two.’ Claire was precise. ‘Reading one and then the other is reading two documents. Holding them together is doing the analysis.’

‘Say what the filtering saves you.’

‘Perhaps forty items reduced to eleven in one and nine in the other.’ She was matter of fact. ‘Which is the difference between a comparison I can hold and one I cannot.’

‘So the filter is not convenience.’

‘The filter is what brings the comparison within the range of what a person can perceive at once.’ Claire was definite. ‘Which is a small number. Everybody overestimates it.’

Marcus made a point about the date ranges.

‘Mine uses periods more than categories and I want to say why.’

‘Say it.’

‘Because a building generates a continuous stream and the question is nearly always about a window.’ He counted. ‘What has happened since the last review. What happened in the quarter before this one.’

‘Which is a comparison across time rather than across buildings.’

‘Which is occasionally a comparison across time in the same building, using two contexts on the same property with different periods.’ Marcus shrugged. ‘Which sounds odd and is the clearest way to see whether something is getting worse.’

‘That is a use I have not thought of.’

‘It is one building compared with its own past, which is a comparison like any other and needs the same simultaneity.’


## What this changes about deciding

‘I want to say what I think is actually going on,’ Claire said, ‘because we have been describing a mechanism and the effect is on judgement.’

‘Say it.’

‘Sequential examination produces impressions and impressions are compressed.’ She was precise. ‘I read a document and what I retain is a summary I constructed while reading.’

‘Which is not the document.’

‘Which is a considerably smaller object than the document, and comparing two summaries is not comparing two documents.’ Claire was definite. ‘It is comparing my compression of one against my compression of another.’

‘And compressions lose different things.’

‘Compressions lose whatever did not seem important at the moment of reading, and the entire point of a comparison is that something unimportant in isolation is significant in contrast.’ She shrugged. ‘Which is precisely the material that does not survive.’

Marcus added the ordering effect.

‘Mine is that the first one frames the others.’

‘Say it.’

‘Whichever building I examine first becomes the standard.’ He was matter of fact. ‘And I read the second against it without deciding to, and the third against my accumulated sense of the first two.’

‘Which drifts.’

‘Which drifts and which I could not detect, because each individual judgement felt reasonable.’ Marcus shrugged. ‘I would reach the end of a review and my conclusions about the last building would be systematically different from my conclusions about the first, for no reason connected to the buildings.’

‘Have you tested that?’

‘Not rigorously and I noticed it retrospectively, which is the only way anybody notices anything about their own judgement.’ He was frank. ‘Reviewing my old quarterly notes, the later entries are consistently harsher.’

‘Which is fatigue as much as framing.’

‘Which is both and the arrangement addresses both, because the load is lower and the order matters less.’ Marcus was definite.

Claire raised the cognitive load.

‘There is a point about capacity that I would emphasise.’

‘Say it.’

‘Comparing three things sequentially requires holding two while forming a third.’ She counted. ‘Which is at the limit of what anybody can do and is why most people quietly reduce a five-way comparison to a two-way one.’

‘Without noticing.’

‘Without noticing that they have, and while believing they considered all five.’ Claire was emphatic. ‘Which is the failure that concerns me most, because the person is confident and the comparison did not happen.’

‘And with several present?’

‘With several present the holding is unnecessary, so the capacity goes into the actual judgement rather than into keeping things in mind.’ She shrugged. ‘Which is the same argument everybody makes about externalising memory, applied to a single afternoon.’


## The review Claire will not do this way

‘There is a kind of work where I close everything down to one,’ Claire said.

‘Say it.’

‘Drafting.’ She was definite. ‘Which is the opposite of everything we have discussed and I want it stated, because somebody will conclude that more contexts is always better.’

‘Say why drafting is different.’

‘Because comparison is a receptive activity and drafting is a generative one.’ Claire was precise. ‘When I compare, I want as much in view as I can perceive. When I draft, I want one thing and nothing else.’

‘Which is the concentration argument.’

‘Which is the concentration argument and it is stronger for me than for most people, because a claim is one sentence that may run to three hundred words and every clause matters.’

‘So the comparison happens before.’

‘The comparison happens before, and produces a set of decisions, and then I close everything and draft against those decisions.’ She counted. ‘Two distinct phases, deliberately separated, and I got that wrong for about six months.’

‘Say what going wrong looked like.’

‘Drafting with three applications open, referring across constantly, producing something that was a patchwork of three documents.’ Claire was frank. ‘Which read as though it had been assembled rather than written, because it had been.’

Marcus made a noise.

‘Mine is the same in a different register.’

‘Say it.’

‘Writing the actual recommendation.’ He counted. ‘I compare across four buildings and then I write what I am going to do, and the writing happens with one thing open.’

‘Which is one building.’

‘Which is generally no building at all. A blank entry and my conclusions.’ Marcus shrugged. ‘Because if a building is in front of me while I am writing a recommendation, I write about that building rather than about the portfolio.’

‘So the material pulls the writing toward itself.’

‘The material pulls the writing toward whatever is visible, which is exactly what you want when comparing and exactly what you do not want when concluding.’ He was definite. ‘Which is a rule I would give anybody. Compare with everything open. Decide with nothing open.’


## Managing the contexts

‘Practical,’ Marcus said. ‘Because somebody reading this will open eleven contexts and find it useless.’

‘Say your number.’

‘Four, and I rarely exceed it.’ He was definite. ‘Which is not a rule I set. It is where things settled.’

‘Say what happens at five.’

‘At five I stop looking across and start switching between, which is sequential examination with extra steps.’ Marcus shrugged. ‘The whole benefit disappears at the point where I can no longer take them in together.’

‘Which is a perceptual limit rather than a technical one.’

‘Which is entirely about what I can see at once, and it is four for me on the screen I have.’

Claire said hers goes higher and for a reason.

‘I sometimes have six.’

‘Say why.’

‘Because a large family is six applications and the comparison genuinely spans all of them.’ She was matter of fact. ‘And I accept that at six I am doing something different.’

‘Say what.’

‘Working in pairs within a larger set.’ Claire counted. ‘I compare two properly, then two others, then across the pairs, which is sequential at the top level and parallel within it.’

‘Which is a compromise.’

‘Which is a compromise and it is considerably better than six sequentially, and I am clear-eyed that it is not the same as four in view.’ She shrugged.

Marcus described his rotation.

‘Mine is a standing set that changes composition.’

‘Say it.’

‘During a review, one context is always the baseline and the others rotate.’ He counted. ‘I finish with a building, close it, open the next, and the baseline stays.’

‘So one is fixed.’

‘One is fixed for the whole review, deliberately, and the other three cycle through thirty-one properties over two days.’ Marcus was matter of fact. ‘Which means every comparison is against the same reference rather than against whatever I looked at previously.’

‘That is considerably more rigorous than mine.’

‘It is one decision that removes an entire category of drift and it took me a year to think of.’

Claire said hers is per matter rather than standing.

‘I open a set for a matter and close it when I move on,’ she said.

‘Say why close them.’

‘Because contexts from a previous matter are worse than nothing.’ She was definite. ‘If I am working on one client and something from another is still open, that is an error waiting to happen.’

‘Which is a professional obligation rather than tidiness.’

‘Which is entirely an obligation, and it is the one piece of my practice here that is not about efficiency at all.’

They discussed reconstruction.

‘The contexts do not survive a session,’ Marcus said. ‘Which took some adjusting.’

‘Say how you handle it.’

‘Starred items and the deadline view, which between them tell me what I was doing.’ He counted. ‘Which is about ninety seconds to reassemble a review, and I do it twice a day during a review period.’

‘Which is not nothing.’

‘Which is ninety seconds against two days of work, and the alternative would be state that persists whether or not it should.’ Marcus shrugged. ‘I would rather rebuild deliberately than inherit something I set up on Tuesday.’

‘Mine is the same and I would put it more strongly,’ Claire said. ‘Reconstructing forces me to decide what I am doing, which is a useful question at the start of a session.’

## What the panel is doing while the contexts are open

‘There is a part of this we have not mentioned and it is running the whole time,’ Marcus said.

‘The side panel.’

‘The side panel, which is doing something completely different from the contexts and which I would not want to lose during a review.’

‘Say the difference.’

‘The contexts are narrow and the panel is wide.’ He counted. ‘Each context is one building filtered to one question. The panel is everything I own.’

‘So one is depth and one is breadth.’

‘Which is exactly the division and they need each other.’ Marcus was definite. ‘Because a review conducted entirely inside four narrow contexts is a review that has lost sight of the portfolio.’

‘Say what you actually look at.’

‘Deadlines, which span everything regardless of what is open.’ He was matter of fact. ‘Which matters during a review because I am concentrating on four buildings and there are twenty-seven others with obligations.’

‘Which the contexts cannot show you.’

‘Which the contexts by construction cannot show me, because I have narrowed them deliberately.’ Marcus shrugged. ‘The narrowing is the point and the narrowing is also a blindness, and the panel is what covers it.’

Claire said hers is the starred set rather than the deadlines.

‘Mine holds what is currently live across every matter,’ she said.

‘Say why that matters mid-review.’

‘Because I am inside one family for two hours and something else has a response deadline on Thursday.’ She was precise. ‘And the family in front of me is absorbing, and absorbing is how deadlines get missed.’

‘So the panel interrupts.’

‘The panel does not interrupt, which is the important part.’ Claire was definite. ‘It is present. I glance at it. Nothing arrives and nothing demands anything.’

‘Which is different from a notification.’

‘Which is entirely different from a notification, and it is the correct behaviour for something that has to coexist with concentration.’ She shrugged. ‘Something that pushed at me while I was comparing claim language would be worse than useless.’

Marcus added the numbers.

‘I look at the collection figures once during a review and not more.’

‘Say what for.’

‘Whether the review itself is producing what it should.’ He counted. ‘Two days of quarterly review should generate perhaps twenty entries. If it has generated four, I have been reading rather than recording.’

‘Which is a check on your own process.’

‘Which is a check on whether I am actually doing the work or merely looking at it, and I have caught myself twice.’


## The comparison Marcus refuses to make

‘There is one comparison I will not do this way and I want to explain why,’ Marcus said.

‘Say it.’

‘Anything involving tenants as people.’

Claire was interested.

‘Say what you mean.’

‘I compare buildings constantly and I do not compare the people in them.’ He was precise. ‘Which sounds like a technical distinction and is not.’

‘Say the difference.’

‘A building generates a maintenance record and an occupancy figure, and those are facts about a structure.’ Marcus counted. ‘A tenant generates a payment history, and a payment history side by side with four others invites a comparison I should not be making.’

‘Which is ranking people.’

‘Which is exactly ranking people, and the arrangement makes it effortless, and effortless is the problem.’ He shrugged. ‘Four payment histories in view and my eye finds the worst one in about a second.’

‘Which may be accurate.’

‘Which may be entirely accurate and is still the wrong operation.’ Marcus was definite. ‘Because a payment history is a record of somebody's circumstances and the reasons are not in the record.’

‘Say what you do instead.’

‘I look at one at a time, deliberately, which is slower and which forces me to think about a person rather than to sort a column.’ He counted. ‘And when somebody is behind, I find out why, which is a conversation rather than a comparison.’

‘So you have chosen the worse method.’

‘I have chosen the slower method for one category, on purpose, because the faster one produces a judgement I do not want to make quickly.’ Marcus shrugged. ‘Which is the only place in my work where I have deliberately declined a capability.’

Claire thought about it.

‘I have an equivalent and it is smaller.’

‘Say it.’

‘Comparing associates' drafting.’ She was matter of fact. ‘Which I could do and which would tell me something and which I do not do side by side.’

‘Say why not.’

‘Because two drafts in view invites a verdict about two people, and what I actually want is to help each of them with their own work.’ Claire was definite. ‘Which is a different activity that happens one at a time.’

‘So the same principle.’

‘The same principle exactly, and I would state it as: compare things, and do not compare people, however easy the arrangement makes it.’


## Simultaneity is the whole of it

Almost every professional decision of consequence is comparative, and almost every tool for holding information presents one thing at a time and expects the person to supply the comparison from memory. That expectation is where the cost sits, and both practitioners here describe having paid it for years without identifying it.

The failure is precise rather than general. Examining several things in sequence produces impressions, and impressions are compressed. What survives is a summary constructed during reading, which necessarily discards whatever seemed unimportant at that moment. The entire purpose of a comparison is that something unremarkable in isolation becomes significant in contrast, which is exactly the material that does not survive compression.

Two further distortions arrived independently from both. Whatever is examined first becomes the frame through which everything after it is read, which one practitioner detected only retrospectively, by noticing that his conclusions about the last building in a review were consistently harsher than his conclusions about the first for reasons unconnected to the buildings. And a person comparing several things sequentially quietly reduces the comparison to two or three, without noticing they have done so and while believing they considered everything.

What resolves it is having several contexts present at once, each holding its own arrangement. That last part is the qualification that matters, and one practitioner states it directly: several windows onto the same view is one view spread across a screen, while several contexts each narrowed differently is several analyses running in parallel. One property filtered to recent maintenance answers what has been going wrong; another filtered to documents answers what agreements exist; a third unfiltered answers what is present at all. Three different questions, visible together.

The same principle produces a legal version. One application ordered chronologically because an examination is a conversation and the sequence carries meaning; another ordered by kind because the question concerns document types rather than a sequence. Both visible, because the questions differ. The filtering also does something less obvious, which is bringing the comparison within the range of what anybody can perceive at once, a number both describe as small and universally overestimated.

Two findings emerged that neither had planned. One practitioner keeps a deliberately ordinary item in his review set as a control, having spent a year comparing problems only with other problems and reaching conclusions consistently more alarming than the situation warranted. And one describes catching a claim construction appearing in two applications at once, which meant a response drafted for one would have damaged the other, an error he describes as the kind that stays invisible until somebody identifies it years later.

The boundary is as important as the technique and both stated it firmly. Comparison is receptive and drafting is generative, and they require opposite arrangements. One describes six months of drafting with three applications open and producing something that read as though it had been assembled, because it had been. The other describes writing recommendations with nothing open at all, since material in view pulls the writing toward itself, which is precisely what somebody wants while comparing and precisely what they do not want while concluding. The rule both offer is short: compare with everything open, decide with nothing open.

Management is a matter of perceptual limits rather than technical ones. Four contexts is where one settled, because beyond that he stops looking across and starts switching between, which is sequential examination with additional steps. The other goes to six for large families and is clear-eyed that she is then working in pairs within a larger set, which is a compromise rather than the same operation. One keeps a fixed reference while the others rotate, which removes an entire category of drift. The other closes everything when moving between clients, which is a professional obligation rather than a preference.

Contexts not persisting between sessions turned out to be welcome rather than otherwise. Reconstruction takes around ninety seconds using starred items and a deadline view, and both describe the deliberate rebuild as preferable to inheriting an arrangement assembled for a different purpose on a different day. One describes it as forcing the question of what she is actually doing, which is useful at the start of any session.

The narrow contexts and the broad panel do different jobs simultaneously, and the practitioners are explicit that the narrowing which makes comparison possible is also a blindness. Four filtered contexts cannot show what is happening across everything else, which is what the panel covers, present and unpressing throughout. Something that pushed at somebody while they were comparing claim language would be worse than useless, and something absent would let a deadline pass unnoticed while its owner was absorbed in four buildings.

What all of it reduces to is that decisions are made by comparison, comparison requires simultaneity, and simultaneity is not a convenience. It determines which differences are perceptible at all, and a difference nobody can perceive is a difference that does not inform anything.


## FAQs

### Why are professional decisions comparative?

Because the questions that matter are rarely about a single item. Practitioners describe asking which of several should receive a resource, which is behaving unlike the others, or whether one document says the same as another. All of those are relations between things, and a relation cannot be perceived in one thing at a time regardless of how carefully somebody examines it. Practitioners in unrelated professions arrive at the same conclusion independently, which suggests it follows from how perception works rather than from any particular kind of material. It is one of the distortions that is invisible while it operates and obvious once somebody names it. Anybody uncertain can test the question against their own last review rather than reasoning about it in the abstract.
