A name looks like a label and behaves like an address. That distinction is the entire subject, and almost nobody notices it because naming happens in a second, at the moment of creating something, when the thing being named is fresh and obvious and the person doing it cannot imagine ever forgetting what it is. The name feels temporary. It is, in fact, the most durable decision made about that item, since the content will be revised, the structure will be rearranged, and the name will sit there unchanged for years, determining whether anybody ever reaches what is underneath it.

The reason this matters more in a searchable system than in a filing cabinet is that titles carry weight. In any ranked retrieval, a match in a title counts for considerably more than a match in a body, which means the words somebody chooses for a name are not merely descriptive: they are the terms most likely to surface the item later. Somebody who names an entry for what it is called will find it when they remember what it is called. Somebody who names it for what they will later be looking for will find it whenever they need it, which is a different and much more useful property.

![The Art of Naming Things in a Knowledge Base](/assets/img/feature-screenshot-placeholder.svg)

The mistake almost everybody makes is naming descriptively rather than for retrieval, and the mistake is invisible for months. A researcher titles a paper entry with its authors and its own title, which is how a reference is conventionally written and which contains none of the terms they will actually search. A property manager names an inspection after the date, which is the one thing they will never remember. A minister names a note after what happened, which is exactly what has faded by the time they need it. In each case the name is accurate and useless.
<!--more-->


There is a second failure, subtler and more expensive, which is inconsistency. A convention that is wrong but applied uniformly can be corrected in an afternoon, because the items sharing it can be found and adjusted together. A collection named inconsistently cannot be corrected at all, because there is nothing to search for: the whole problem is that similar things were named in dissimilar ways, so no query gathers them. Somebody who has titled things three different ways across eighteen months has produced three partial archives inside one system, and no amount of subsequent discipline recovers the first two.

What makes this genuinely difficult is that there is no universally correct convention, and the professionals who insist otherwise are describing their own field rather than a principle. A convention has to encode whatever a person will actually remember at the moment of searching, and what somebody remembers is a fact about their work rather than about naming. A researcher remembers authors. A property manager remembers addresses. A journalist protecting sources cannot put the most memorable thing in the title at all, since the title is searchable and searchable is the opposite of what that material requires.

The considerations pull against each other, which is why the subject produces such strong opinions. A name optimised for search wants the terms somebody will type. A name optimised for scanning wants to be short enough to read at a glance down a list of forty. A name optimised for privacy wants to reveal nothing. A name optimised for evocation, which matters for creative work, wants to be memorable rather than systematic. No single scheme serves all four, and choosing between them requires knowing which one the work actually demands.

What follows is a thread in a small private group where somebody raised the question and everybody answered, which produced twelve incompatible schemes, one genuine argument, and considerably more agreement about the underlying principle than anybody expected.


## The post that started it

Tomas posted on a Tuesday evening and apologised in the first line, which several people told him afterward was becoming a habit.

The post read: I have just spent forty minutes failing to find something I wrote in March and I have worked out why. I have named my entries three different ways since I started, depending on what I was thinking about that week. The search is doing exactly what it should and my titles are giving it nothing to work with. So: what are your naming conventions, and how did you arrive at them?

There were twelve replies over three days and one of them started an argument.


## Tomas Herrera, chemistry teacher, San Antonio

Since I asked, I will go first, and I will describe both the mess and the fix.

The mess was that I had three conventions running at once. Some entries were named after the topic, because that is what I was thinking about. Some were named after the lesson number, because that is what my planning document uses. And some were named after the activity, which is how the students refer to them, and which is why I have an entry called The Balloon Thing that I could not find in March because I had forgotten the balloon.

What I have moved to is a pattern with three parts, in a fixed order. The course code first, then the unit, then the topic. So an entry looks like AP Chemistry, unit three, and then whatever the thing actually is. Stoichiometry lab procedures. Enthalpy diagrams. The specific heat experiment.

The order matters and it took me a fortnight to work out why. Course first means my list sorts into course groupings without any filtering, which is how I think during a planning period. Unit second means everything for a unit is adjacent, which is how I think during a teaching week. Topic last means the distinguishing part is the part I read, and by the time my eye reaches it I have already established the context from the two things before it.

The thing I got wrong initially was putting the topic first because it seemed like the most important information. It is the most important information and it is the wrong thing to lead with, because a list of forty entries all beginning with different topics is a list you have to read rather than scan.

Two other rules I have adopted and would defend.

The first is that a label never duplicates a page name. My pages say where something belongs: which course, which unit. My labels say what kind of thing it is: lab, homework, exam preparation, safety. If I had a label called thermochemistry I would have two ways of expressing the same fact and I would use them inconsistently, which is exactly the problem I have just spent forty minutes paying for.

The second is that I write the terms I search for rather than the terms that are technically correct. My students and I refer to a particular experiment by a name that appears in no textbook. That name goes in the title, because that is what I will type at seven in the morning. The formal name goes in the body, where it belongs and where the search will still find it at a lower weight.

I would add one confession. Retitling my old entries is not something I am going to do comprehensively. I have about six hundred and I am doing the ones I actually reach for, which I identified by looking at what I have searched repeatedly, and it is roughly fifty. That will take me two evenings and it will fix the problem for everything I actually use.

**Priya:** The point about leading with the thing that groups rather than the thing that distinguishes is exactly right and I arrived at it independently for a completely different field.

**Suki:** Six hundred entries and you are fixing fifty. That is the correct ratio and almost nobody has the discipline to stop there.


## Priya Sharma, doctoral candidate, London

Mine has two conventions because I have two kinds of thing and they are not alike.

Papers are named with the first author, then an abbreviation of the journal, then a short form of what the paper actually establishes. Not its own title. Its own title is written to sound significant to a reviewing committee and frequently contains none of the words anybody would search for.

The first author is doing the heaviest work in that pattern and I want to explain why, because it is the single most useful thing I have learned about naming in four years. In my field, the retrieval key is almost always a person. I do not remember what a paper was called. I remember that somebody's group did the thing with the modified delivery mechanism, and I remember their name, because names are what circulate in conversation and at conferences and in every discussion I have ever had about anybody's work.

Since a title match outranks a match anywhere else, putting the author name in the title means the entry surfaces first whenever I search a person, and searching a person is what I actually do.

Experiments are the other kind and they use a different pattern entirely. A sequential identifier first, then a brief description of what was being tested. The identifier is not decorative: my notebooks use it, my analysis scripts use it, and my supervisor refers to it, so it is the one string that appears in every context around that experiment.

Here is what I got wrong for three years and am now fixing. My experiment titles used to be the identifier and a date and nothing else, on the grounds that the identifier was unique and therefore sufficient. It is unique and it is not memorable, and I never search for an identifier because I do not know which one I want. I search for the variable I was investigating, and that word appeared nowhere in the title.

I am adding it to about a hundred and forty of them, which is the subset I go back to, which is the failures rather than the successes. A successful experiment produces a figure that ends up in a paper. A failed one produces knowledge that exists nowhere else, and those are the ones I reach for.

One more thing which is specific to a long project. My chapter labels do work my titles cannot, because a paper may bear on three chapters and its title cannot say so without becoming absurd. So the title is about the paper and the labels are about my argument, and those are genuinely separate axes rather than two ways of saying the same thing.

**Claire:** The observation that a document's own title is written for a different purpose than retrieval is exactly true in my field as well and I had never articulated it.

**Felix:** A paper is titled to impress a committee. That sentence explains about thirty years of my irritation with my own discipline.


## Claire Fontaine, patent attorney, Chicago

Mine is the most rigid scheme in this thread and I make no apology for it, because my titles are read by people other than me and occasionally by people who are not friendly.

Four components in a fixed order. A client code, then a matter number, then the document type, then a brief description of the specific document.

The client code is three or four letters and it is the same code the firm uses in its billing system, which is deliberate. A convention that agrees with the conventions already in use around it requires no translation, and translation is where errors enter. Anybody in the firm reading one of my titles recognises the client immediately without knowing anything about my personal system.

The matter number is sequential within each client rather than globally, which is a decision I made in my first month and which I would defend against anybody. Globally sequential numbers tell you when something started and nothing else. Client-sequential numbers tell you that this is the fourteenth matter for that client, which is information about a relationship, and I frequently want to know how deep a relationship runs before I want to know anything else.

The document type is a small controlled vocabulary. Application. Office action. Response. Prior art. Claim chart. Correspondence. Six or seven words, used exactly, never varied. This is the component that makes a matter scannable: a matter with thirty entries under it is unreadable if the types are described in free text and entirely readable if the same six words appear in the same position every time.

The description is the only free part and it is short. Enough to distinguish this response from the previous response, which usually means naming the claim or the ground it concerns.

Priya asked me about numbering and I will answer it properly because it is a real question. Sequential numbering has one genuine weakness, which is that it encodes the order things arrived and nothing about what they are. That is fine for a matter, because a matter is a proceeding and its order is meaningful. It would be wrong for anything where the sequence is arbitrary.

The other thing I want to say concerns version history and titles, because I see people confuse these.

A title identifies a document. The history identifies its states. Those are different jobs and mixing them produces the file-naming disease everybody in this thread has suffered from: response final, response final revised, response actually final. I have never once put a version marker in a title, because the entry is one document with a history inside it, and there is nothing to distinguish.

I would offer one warning that is specific to regulated work. Whatever appears in a title may be read by somebody who was not the intended audience. A colleague picking up a matter. Somebody in a discovery process. A person glancing at a screen. That is a reason for precision rather than for vagueness, but it is a genuine constraint and it rules out the kind of shorthand I would otherwise use.

**Priya:** Client-sequential rather than globally sequential is the answer to a question I have had for two years and never asked anyone.

**Marcus:** The point about matching the convention already in use around you is the one I would put at the top of this whole thread.


## Marcus Webb, property investor, Denver

Mine is short because everything about my work rewards brevity.

Three components. An abbreviated address, then a document type, then a four-digit date.

The abbreviation is the part that matters and it is the part everybody questions. My properties are pages named with the full address written out, no shortenings, because the page name has to be unambiguous and sorts predictably. My entry titles use a compressed form: the street number and the first few letters of the street name, run together.

The reason is scanning. A unit page may hold sixty entries and I am reading down a list. Full addresses at the front of every title would occupy half the width and would be identical across every entry on that page, which is a considerable amount of screen space spent repeating what the page already told me.

The compressed form is short enough that the document type and the date are visible at the same time, and those are the parts I am actually distinguishing between.

The date is four digits, month and day, no year. The year is implied by ordering and by the property context, and adding it makes every title longer for information I have never once needed to see in a list. If I want the year I look at the dates column, which is right there.

The document type is a fixed vocabulary of about eight words. Inspection. Lease. Invoice. Notice. Certificate. Correspondence. Photos. Report. Never varied, never abbreviated, always the same word for the same kind of thing.

That last rule is the one I would put above everything else, and I learned it the expensive way. For my first year I used inspection, inspection report, and property inspection interchangeably, which are three names for one thing, and a query for any one of them returned a third of what existed.

One further thing, which came up in another conversation and which I have started applying. Anything arriving as a photograph of paper, rather than as a document, gets its key terms written into the title deliberately, because the extraction from a phone photograph depends on how somebody held the phone and I cannot control that. A contractor's invoice photographed badly still gets found if I put the contractor's name in the title myself.

I do not name most files. About one in fifteen, and only the ones that carry weight.

## Suki Tanaka, security consultant, Tokyo and remote

Mine is three components and one of them exists for a reason nobody else in this thread has.

Client code, engagement type, phase.

The client code is short and it is not the client's name. That is the deliberate part. A screen visible in a cafe, a laptop open on a train, somebody glancing over during a call: none of those should reveal which organisations I am currently working for, because the fact of an engagement is frequently more sensitive than anything inside it.

An organisation discovering that a competitor has retained a security consultant learns something valuable. An organisation discovering that I am in the middle of an engagement learns when they are least defended. Neither of those requires anybody to read a single finding.

So the codes are arbitrary, they are consistent, and the mapping lives in one encrypted entry that I open rarely.

The engagement type and the phase are both from small fixed vocabularies. Four types, six phases. Together they mean that any title tells me, at a glance, what kind of work this is and how far along it is, which is the same information the board columns carry and which I want available even when I am not looking at a board.

The thing I would add that is specific to my work concerns what goes inside entries rather than on them. My stored scripts and commands follow their own naming convention, separate from the entry titles, and I am strict about it because a snippet with an unhelpful name is a puzzle I have set for myself. The name of a stored procedure should state what it does and against what, in words, not in whatever I typed while tired.

On the general question Tomas asked, I would make one point that I think is underweighted in this thread so far.

A convention is a decision made once and applied thousands of times. That asymmetry is what makes the initial thinking worth doing properly and what makes changing it later so expensive. It also means the value of a convention is almost entirely in its consistency rather than in its cleverness. I have seen elaborate schemes fail because they required judgment at the moment of naming, and simple ones succeed for twenty years because they required none.

If your convention requires you to think, it will be applied inconsistently, and inconsistently applied is functionally the same as absent.

## Lena Johansson, investigative journalist, Stockholm

Suki has described the milder version of my rule and I want to state the strict one, because for me this is not a preference.

Three components: a source code, a document type, and a summary of what the content concerns.

No real name appears in any searchable field. Ever. Not in a title, not in a label, not in a hashtag, not in the name of an attached file. This applies to sources, to people mentioned by sources, and to organisations where naming them would identify who I am talking to.

The reason is not that somebody might read my screen, although they might. It is that a title is searchable, and searchable means that anybody who ever gains access to the material, by any route, has a fast index into it. A well-named archive is an efficient archive, and efficiency is neutral about who is using it.

So a source is a code. The mapping between codes and people exists in one encrypted entry with its own password, and that entry is the only place in my entire system where the two are connected.

The document type is a small vocabulary: memo, filing, correspondence, transcript, dataset, bundle.

The summary is where I put the retrieval terms, and it is deliberately about subject matter rather than about people. Executive compensation. Subsidiary structure. Regulatory correspondence. Those are the terms I search, they are meaningful to me, and they identify nobody.

There is a cost to this and I want to be honest about it, because everybody else in this thread is optimising for retrieval and I am not.

My titles are worse at retrieval than they could be. If I could put real names in them, my searches would be faster and my results would be more precise, because a person's name is the most distinctive string available and it is exactly what I would type. I have given that up deliberately and I pay for it in small amounts every day.

What I have gained is that no single failure produces a map. Somebody who obtains my folder and cannot open the mapping entry has a set of documents about subjects, attributed to codes, which is considerably less useful than a set of documents attributed to names.

One practical consequence worth stating. Because my titles cannot carry the most memorable thing, I lean harder on everything else. My page structure does more work than most people's, because a source's page is where the context lives. My labels are more specific. And I use the search inside document contents constantly, since the real names are in the documents themselves and the documents are not what I control the naming of.

The general point I would offer to anybody whose material is sensitive: decide what your titles are allowed to contain before you name anything, because a title written once is a title that exists forever, and retroactively removing a name from four hundred entries is not a project anybody completes.

## Raj Malakar, documentary filmmaker, Berlin and Mumbai

Mine is three parts and one of them is a number, which several of you will approve of and one of you will not.

Project abbreviation, scene or segment number, description.

The project abbreviation is three or four letters and exists because I run three projects simultaneously and always have. Without it, my entry list is a mixture of three films and I cannot tell at a glance which is which.

The number is the part that matters and it is not a sequential identifier in the way Priya's is. It is a position. Scene fourteen is the fourteenth thing in the current cut, and when the cut changes, that number is wrong.

Which is exactly the objection somebody is about to raise, so I will raise it myself. Yes, my numbers go stale. Yes, I renumber. It happens perhaps three times per project, at points where the structure genuinely changes, and it takes an evening.

I do it anyway because the alternative is worse. Editing is sequential, my head is sequential during a cut, and a list sorted into the order of the film is the single most useful view I have. A list sorted alphabetically by description is a list I have to think about.

The description is short and it is concrete. Not what the scene is about. What is physically in it. Indigo dyeing process. The market at dawn. The interview in the hospital corridor. Because when I am searching at eleven at night I am remembering an image rather than a theme.

That is the distinction I would offer to anybody doing visual work. Name the thing you saw, not the thing it means. The meaning changes across a production and the image does not.

Hashtags are a separate convention and I keep them entirely apart from titles. Titles say what something is. Hashtags say what stage it is at, and those move constantly. Mixing them would mean editing a title every time something progressed, which is both tedious and a good way to lose the number.

One more thing, which is a confession. My first project used descriptive titles with no numbers and no project code, because I was being creative and thought codes were bureaucratic. That project is the one I cannot navigate, four years later, and it is the one I most frequently need to, because material from it keeps turning out to be relevant to what I am doing now.

Being creative about naming is being creative in the one place where creativity has no benefit and a permanent cost.

**Tomas:** Name the thing you saw rather than the thing it means. I am going to use that with students about their lab notes.

## Captain Nkechi Obi, cargo ship officer, at sea

Mine is not a personal convention. It is a professional one that predates me by about a century, and I have simply applied it.

Vessel abbreviation, category, date.

The vessel comes first, always, without exception, and this is not negotiable in my industry. Every document produced aboard any ship identifies the ship first, because a document without a vessel is a document that cannot be filed by anybody ashore, and because I will serve on several vessels and my records need to remain sortable across all of them.

The category is from a short fixed list: inspection, drill, cargo, maintenance, handover, certificate.

The date is four digits, month and day. Never the year, for the same reason Marcus gave, which I read with some satisfaction because we arrived at it separately.

Raj's comment about renumbering is the thing I want to respond to, because it clarified something for me.

My numbers are dates. A date is assigned by the world rather than by me, it never becomes wrong, and it never needs to be reissued. Which means my ordering is stable in a way his cannot be, and that is not a virtue of my system. It is a property of my work. Operations happen in time and films are assembled in an order somebody chooses.

What I would add for anybody working shifts or watches is a point about when the naming happens.

I capture during a watch in a chat-style log, one line at a time, with no title at all, because I have eleven seconds and titling is a decision. At the end of the watch I turn those lines into proper entries, and that is when they get named, when I am sitting down and can think.

Separating capture from naming is the thing that makes a convention survivable for anybody whose work interrupts them. A scheme requiring a well-formed title at the moment something happens is a scheme that will be abandoned during the first genuinely busy period, and busy periods are when the most important things happen.

One last thing. My certification entries carry the expiry date in the title, which breaks my own pattern, and I do it deliberately. Those are the entries where the date is the most important fact about the item, and a convention that hides the most important fact is a convention worth breaking once, in a documented and consistent way.

## Dr. Felix Brennan, retired professor, Vermont

Mine is unlike everybody else's because my titles have a second job.

Two components: a decade and a category, then a descriptive title that is doing something none of yours are.

The nineteen-nineties, teaching, and then something like the year I almost left. The department, and then the argument about the survey course. Correspondence, and then a name.

The decade and the category are ordinary. They group, they sort, they let me find a branch of my life without filtering.

The last part is where mine diverges. My descriptive titles are evocative rather than descriptive, deliberately, because a substantial number of them will become chapter headings and because I am writing something that has to be read rather than retrieved.

There is a practical argument for this and it is not sentimental.

I begin every working day with the widget that hands me a random entry from anywhere in the vault. It refreshes hourly and I read whatever it offers. And what determines whether that ritual produces anything is entirely the title, because the title is what I see first and what decides whether I open the thing.

An entry called Correspondence, box four, second folder is an entry I will not open. An entry called The letter I never answered is an entry I will open every time, and about one time in twenty opening it produces a chapter.

So my titles are optimised for a mechanism that most of you do not use in the way I do. They are written to make a version of me at seven in the morning curious.

I will grant Priya's point that this costs me retrieval. My titles contain fewer searchable terms than they might and I find things slightly more slowly. I have decided that trade is correct for a book and would be wrong for a thesis, which is a distinction about the work rather than about naming.

One thing I would say to anybody in a long solitary project. Your titles are the only voice you have that reaches your future self, and your future self is the only reader you have for several years. Write them for that person. Nobody else is going to read them, and by the time anybody does, the titles will have already done their work.

## Dr. Meera Iyer, psychiatrist, Austin

Mine is the shortest scheme in this thread and it is short for a reason nobody else has.

Patient initials, then a sequential session number. That is the whole title.

Two characters and a number. Nothing descriptive, nothing about content, nothing about what happened.

The reason is that my entries are encrypted and the title is not. A title appears in a list. A list is visible on a screen. A screen is in a room, and my room has a door that people knock on and occasionally open, and the person opening it may be the next patient.

So the title carries the minimum required to identify the entry to me and nothing that would mean anything to anybody else. All of the clinical content is inside, behind a password.

That is the entire logic and it produces a scheme that would be inadequate for anybody else here.

There is a second benefit which I did not design for and now value. A list of forty entries, each two characters and a number, is scannable in a way that descriptive titles never are. ## Consistency beats sophistication

Twelve people, twelve schemes, and no two alike. Read as a survey, that looks like an absence of any principle. Read carefully, it is the opposite: every one of those schemes is a correct answer to a different question, and the questions differ because the work differs.

Four distinct priorities appear across the thread and each one produces a different scheme. Retrieval optimisation puts the terms somebody will actually search into the highest-weighted field, which is why a researcher leads with an author name and a teacher with a course code. Scanability keeps titles short enough to read down a list of sixty, which is why a property manager compresses addresses and a clinician reduces a title to two characters and a number. Security determines what a title is allowed to contain at all, which for a journalist and a consultant rules out the single most memorable string available. And evocation matters where the title has a second job, as it does for somebody whose entries are candidate chapter headings and whose working day begins with a mechanism that offers a random one.

No scheme serves all four, which is why the argument in that thread did not resolve and should not have. What emerged instead was a structural observation: the first component of a title groups and the last component distinguishes, so the first is always doing scanning work and the last is always doing search work. Every scheme in the thread has that shape, including the two that reject conventions entirely.

The genuinely universal points are smaller and less interesting than any individual scheme, which is characteristic of things that are actually true.

Consistency outperforms sophistication by a wide margin. An elaborate convention applied unevenly produces a collection that cannot be corrected, because the items sharing a mistake cannot be gathered. A crude convention applied without exception can be fixed in an afternoon whenever somebody decides to. Every practitioner in that thread who described a failure described inconsistency rather than a wrong choice.

A convention that requires judgment at the moment of naming will not survive. This is why the most rigid schemes in the thread are also the ones with the smallest controlled vocabularies: six document types, four engagement types, eight categories, never varied. Judgment is what fails at the end of a long day, and a scheme designed for a well-rested user is a scheme designed for the wrong person.

Naming should match the conventions already in use around the work rather than inventing new ones. A client code that matches the billing system, a vessel abbreviation that matches a century of maritime practice, and a term that students actually use all require no translation, and translation is where errors enter.

And separating capture from naming is what makes any convention survivable for anybody whose work interrupts them. A scheme demanding a well-formed title at the moment something happens will be abandoned during the first genuinely busy week, which is precisely when the most important material is generated.

The two dissenters are worth taking seriously rather than treating as charming exceptions. One argued that his memory is verbal rather than systematic and that a scheme requiring something artificial, however easy, is a scheme he will bypass and then abandon. The other argued that his collection is small enough that consistent labels do the work titles would otherwise have to, and conceded frankly that this would fail at ten times the size. Both are describing a genuine fit between a practice and a context, and both had a convention exactly where they needed one and nowhere else.

Which is the actual conclusion. The right scheme is the one somebody will apply without deciding to, on a bad day, for years. Any convention meeting that standard outperforms a better one that does not, and the sophistication of the scheme turns out to matter considerably less than whether the person naming things has to think about it at all.


## FAQs

### Why do entry titles matter more than they appear to?

Because a title is an address rather than a description. In a ranked retrieval system a match in a title carries substantially more weight than a match anywhere else, so the words chosen at the moment of creation are the terms most likely to surface the item years later. Titles are also the most durable decision made about an item: content gets revised and structure gets rearranged, while the name sits unchanged, determining whether anybody reaches what is underneath it. Most people name in a second and pay for it for years. Practitioners across unrelated professions arrive at this independently, which is a reasonable sign that it reflects the problem rather than any particular field.

### What is the most common naming mistake?

Naming descriptively rather than for retrieval, which is invisible for months. A researcher titles a paper with its own title, which was written to sound significant to a committee and contains none of the words anybody searches. A property manager names an inspection after the date, which is the one thing nobody remembers. A minister names a note after what happened, which is exactly what has faded by the time it is needed. In each case the name is accurate and useless. The point is worth settling early, since naming decisions are cheap to make and expensive to revise across an accumulated collection. Practitioners who have made this change describe the improvement as arriving immediately for everything named afterward, which is unusual among organisational adjustments and is why it is worth doing early rather than deferring it.

### Why is inconsistency worse than a wrong convention?

Because a wrong convention applied uniformly is correctable and inconsistency is not. Items sharing a mistake can be found and adjusted together in an afternoon. Items named three different ways cannot be gathered at all, since the whole problem is that similar things were named dissimilarly and no query collects them. Practitioners describing naming failures almost always describe drift across months rather than a single bad decision, and drift is the version that cannot be undone. It helps considerably, provided the convention matches vocabulary already in use around the work. A colleague recognises a client code taken from the billing system, a vessel abbreviation from industry practice, or a document type from the profession's own terminology, with no explanation required. A private scheme, however elegant, requires a mapping that exists only in one person's head, which fails the moment somebody else needs to work with the material. Anybody weighing this against other priorities should note that it costs nothing to apply and cannot be retrofitted cheaply, which places it firmly among the decisions worth settling before a collection grows.

### What role do labels play if titles are well designed?

They carry the dimension titles cannot, which is anything cutting across the hierarchy. A title identifies one item and a page places it; a label states a property true of items in several different places. Where labels are consistent and few, titles can be looser, which is the explicit argument made by practitioners using natural-language names. Where labels are weak or absent, titles have to carry more, which is why disciplined labelling and relaxed titling frequently appear together. ### Should a title say what something is about or what it contains?

For visual and observational work, what it contains, because that is what somebody remembers. A scene named for its meaning becomes wrong as the meaning shifts across a production, while a scene named for what is physically in it remains accurate permanently. The same applies to photographs, field notes, and inspection records. For analytical work the reverse frequently holds, since what a document establishes is more memorable than the material it happens to discuss. ### How does naming affect a random-surfacing mechanism?

Decisively, since the title is the only thing visible before somebody decides whether to open an entry. ### What is the cost of an overly rigid convention?

Being bypassed. A scheme requiring judgment, translation, or a lookup before anything can be written will be skipped at the end of a demanding day, and a convention skipped twice is finished. This is why the most durable rigid schemes pair strict structure with tiny controlled vocabularies: the structure is fixed and the choices within it are trivial. Rigidity in the shape is sustainable; rigidity requiring thought is not. ### What is the cost of no convention at all?

It depends almost entirely on scale, which is why practitioners disagree about it. At several hundred entries with consistent labels and full content search, informal naming genuinely works and costs very little. At several thousand, with many similar items, it produces a collection that can only be searched and never scanned, and where similar things cannot be gathered because nothing groups them. The honest position is that informality is a defensible choice with a known breaking point. ### Should the convention be written down?

### How often should a naming convention be reviewed?

Rarely, and only when something concrete prompts it. A convention that keeps changing produces exactly the inconsistency it exists to prevent, so stability is itself a virtue. ### What is the single most useful naming change most people could make?

Putting the term they actually search into the title rather than the term that is technically correct. Most people name accurately and unhelpfully, using the formal designation, the document's own title, or the date, none of which is what they type when looking for something. Identifying the one word they would reach for, and placing it in the highest-weighted field, changes retrieval immediately for everything named afterward. It takes no additional time and requires only noticing what one actually searches for. Practitioners describe this as the adjustment they would make first if starting again, and several identify it as the one thing they wish somebody had told them in their first week rather than discovering after a year of accumulated material.
