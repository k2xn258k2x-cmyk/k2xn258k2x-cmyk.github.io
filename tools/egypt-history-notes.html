A timeline you assemble yourself is worth more than one you read, because the act of deciding what belongs on it is the analysis. If an event does not earn a line, ask why it appears in the guide at all, and if a period fills up while its neighbours stay empty, that imbalance is telling you something about the sources rather than about Egypt.

Periods

The conventional division of Egyptian history, with what defines each period and what ended it. The bar under each entry shows its share of the roughly three thousand years from unification to the Roman annexation, which is the fastest way to see how unevenly the familiar names are distributed.

Every date on this page is conventional and approximate. Egyptian chronology is reconstructed rather than recorded, the margin of error widens the further back you go, and reputable scholars differ by decades in the New Kingdom and by considerably more before it. Treat these figures as a working framework and check the specific date any argument rests on.

Dynasties

All thirty one dynasties in the conventional scheme, plus the Ptolemaic line. The numbering comes from a single ancient source and it is a convenience rather than a natural division, which is why several dynasties overlap and one or two are largely a matter of convention.

Why dates disagree

Eight reasons two reputable books will give you different years for the same pharaoh. Understanding these turns a frustrating inconsistency into something you can write about, and it is the single most useful thing to know before quoting any Egyptian date.

The practical rule for an essay is simple. State which chronology you are following, keep to it, and when a date matters to your argument, say how firm it is. A confident date with no attribution is weaker evidence than an approximate one that names its basis.
