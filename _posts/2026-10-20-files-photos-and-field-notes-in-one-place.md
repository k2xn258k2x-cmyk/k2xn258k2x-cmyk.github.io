Nearly every knowledge management tool ever built has treated text as the real content and everything else as luggage. The note is the thing. The note can be searched, linked, tagged, structured, and reasoned about. Attached to the note, in a smaller font and usually behind a paperclip, sits a list of filenames representing photographs, recordings, scans, spreadsheets, and reports, and those filenames are essentially opaque to the system holding them. It knows they exist. It knows how large they are. It has no idea what is inside them, and it will happily let a person write a paragraph referring to an image it cannot see and could not find.

This arrangement made sense when it was designed, since text was cheap to index and everything else was not. It has aged badly, because the professions that most need a working memory are frequently the ones whose primary material is not text at all. A property manager's record of a building is a sequence of photographs of conditions, each one evidence about a moment. A documentary maker's research is footage logs, reference images, recorded interviews, and stills pulled from a location scout. A field scientist's notebook is microscopy captures. A journalist's leaked material is scanned pages. In every one of those cases the written note is commentary, and the attachment is the record. Inverting that priority in software produces a tool that is confidently organized around the least important half of the material.

![Files, Photos, and Field Notes in One Place](/assets/img/feature-screenshot-placeholder.svg)

The symptom is familiar to anybody who has worked this way for a few years, and it is always the same sentence. A note reads see attached photo, and the photograph is somewhere else entirely: in a folder named by date, in an email thread, on a drive in a drawer, in a messaging thread with a contractor who has since stopped replying. The note and the evidence were separated at the moment of filing, usually because the tool holding one had no ability to hold the other, and reuniting them costs twenty minutes each time. Multiply that by a career and the loss is substantial, and almost none of it appears anywhere as a cost.
<!--more-->


What fixes this is not a better attachment panel. It is a change in status. Attachments have to sit where the context sits, so that the photograph of a damaged fitting lives inside the record of that incident rather than in a folder that only remembers dates. Their contents have to be readable by the system, so that a name appearing inside a scanned report is findable without anybody having retyped it. Images pasted into the flow of a note have to be treated as part of the note rather than as decoration, including the words visible inside them. And there has to be a way to look at a large quantity of visual material as visual material, since recognition works differently from retrieval and no amount of filename discipline substitutes for being able to see four hundred photographs at once.

That last point is the one most often missed. Search answers a question you can phrase. A person scanning a grid of images is not asking a question; they are waiting to recognize something, and recognition operates on entirely different equipment. Someone who has photographed a property four times a year for six years cannot describe the image they are looking for, but they will know it in a fraction of a second when it appears. A tool that offers only search to somebody in that position has offered them the wrong instrument, politely.

Put together, the requirement is that a system treat every kind of content as first-class: text that is written, files that are attached, images that are embedded, data that is tabular, and the words that happen to be visible inside a photograph. When that holds, something changes about the working relationship. The archive stops being a set of notes with references to material stored elsewhere, and becomes a single body of evidence with commentary attached to it, which is what these professions were producing all along.

Two people who accumulate that kind of material at industrial rates, in fields with nothing else in common, compared their systems and each came away intending to change something.


## Two very different piles of the same problem

The call connected at half past six in Berlin, which was half past ten in the morning in Denver, and it opened with Raj Malakar apologising for a background that was visibly a hotel desk.

‘I am between things,’ he said. ‘I am always between things. This is a permanent condition rather than a temporary one.’

‘I have a home office and a filing cabinet and I have never been between things in my life,’ Marcus Webb said. ‘I suspect that is why somebody thought we should talk.’

They had been put in touch through the group, which by then functioned as a small informal exchange among people using the same tools for wildly different work. Somebody had noted that both of them dealt in enormous quantities of attachments and had solved the problem from opposite temperamental directions.

‘Give me your numbers,’ Marcus said, ‘because that is how I understand a problem.’

‘I do not know my numbers.’

‘Estimate.’

Raj thought about it, visibly, and it was clear the estimating was itself an unfamiliar exercise.

‘A feature documentary produces, from my side alone, perhaps four thousand reference photographs, three hundred hours of footage which I do not store but do log, sixty to ninety recorded interviews, several hundred scanned documents, location scouting stills, permits, releases, and a quantity of screenshots I refuse to quantify.’ He shrugged. ‘And I have three projects running, always, because that is how the funding works.’

‘So call it fifteen thousand items.’

‘Call it that. It sounds worse when you say it.’

‘Mine is smaller and it never stops,’ Marcus said. ‘Thirty-one properties, about a hundred and forty doors. Every unit generates a move-in inspection and a move-out inspection with photographs, which is between forty and ninety images per inspection because my inspector is thorough. Every maintenance incident generates an invoice, usually a photograph of the problem, sometimes a photograph of the repair. Every tenancy generates a lease as a document. Every property generates insurance certificates, tax assessments, and a roof contract I would rather not discuss.’

‘Per year?’

‘Several thousand new items a year, forever, with no natural end, and every single one of them may need to be produced in a dispute five years later.’ Marcus said this flatly. ‘Which is the difference between us, I think. Your material has a project that ends. Mine accumulates until I sell the building.’

‘My material also does not end,’ Raj said, ‘because I keep everything from every film in case something becomes relevant, which it does, roughly once a year, and that once justifies the whole archive.’

Marcus asked the question he had actually joined the call for.

‘What did it look like before?’

Raj laughed, and it was not entirely a happy sound.

‘Folders,’ he said. ‘Beautiful, nested, dated folders. Project, then year, then month, then shoot day, then a subfolder per camera or per source. Genuinely disciplined. I want that understood, because people assume creative people are disorganized and my folder structure was immaculate.’

‘And the problem was.’

‘The problem was that my notes were somewhere else entirely.’ He spread his hands. ‘I wrote about the material constantly. What I thought of a location. Why a particular interview did not work. Which reference image I wanted to show the cinematographer. And the notes lived in a writing application and the images lived in folders, and the connection between them existed exclusively in my head.’

‘See attached photo,’ Marcus said.

‘See attached photo,’ Raj agreed. ‘Except nothing was attached to anything. I wrote the sentence, I meant a specific image, and I had a name for it that only made sense that week.’

‘Mine was worse and less elegant,’ Marcus said. ‘My inspection photographs arrived by email from my inspector. My contractor invoices came into a shared cloud folder that three people wrote to and nobody organized. My leases were signed electronically and lived in whatever the signing service put them in, and I also had a copy on a drive because I did not trust the signing service. And my actual notes about any of it were on a legal pad.’

‘So four systems and a pad.’

‘Four systems, a pad, and my memory doing the work of a database,’ Marcus said. ‘And it functioned, in the sense that I could always find things eventually. What it cost was that every question took twenty minutes, so I stopped asking questions I did not have to ask, and I did not notice that I had stopped.’

Raj went quiet for a moment.

‘That is the thing nobody says,’ he said. ‘You do not know what you stopped doing.’


## Attaching a thing to the place where it means something

‘Show me how yours is arranged now,’ Marcus said, ‘because I want to see whether the creative version differs from mine or is the same thing with different words.’

Raj shared his screen. Beneath a project page sat sub-pages by phase, and beneath those, entries.

‘One entry per shooting day,’ he said. ‘That is the unit. Day fourteen, the market. Day fifteen, the hospital corridor. Under each of those, sections.’

He opened one. Six section bars.

‘Location and access. Interviews. Coverage. Problems. Reference. And what I would do differently, which nobody asks for and which is the most useful section I write.’

‘And the files sit where?’

‘Inside the sections.’ He expanded two of them to show it. ‘The location photographs go on Location. The audio recordings go on Interviews, one per interview, with the subject's name. The reference images I brought in from elsewhere go on Reference. The invoice from the fixer goes on Problems, because that is where the problem was.’

Marcus leaned toward his camera slightly.

‘Say why that matters rather than putting them all on the entry.’

‘Because when I open a shooting day I am usually looking for one thing, and I know what kind of thing it is before I know its name.’ Raj was definite about this. ‘I want the audio from the second interview. I do not remember what the file is called, because it is called something the recorder invented. But I know it is an interview, so I open Interviews, and there are four things in it, and one of them has a name on it.’

‘Whereas on the entry.’

‘Whereas on the entry there would be thirty-one files in a list, named by a camera, a recorder, a phone, and a fixer with an idiosyncratic system.’ He shook his head. ‘The section is doing the work the filename cannot do. It says what kind of thing this is, and that is nearly always the first thing I know and the last thing a filename tells me.’

Marcus was nodding before he finished.

‘Mine is identical and I arrived at it from paperwork rather than photographs,’ he said, and shared his own.

Property page, unit page beneath it, entries at the leaves. He opened a maintenance entry.

‘Kitchen leak, unit 2B. Sections for the initial report from the tenant, the diagnosis when the plumber arrived, the invoice, the follow-up two weeks later, and the correspondence with the insurer.’ He expanded them one at a time. ‘Photographs the tenant sent are on the report. The plumber's invoice is on the invoice section. The insurer's letter is on the insurer section.’

‘So when a dispute arrives.’

‘When a dispute arrives I open one entry and read it in order, and every document is sitting at the point in the story where it belongs.’ Marcus said it with some satisfaction. ‘I have done that twice in the last year and both times the conversation ended within a few minutes, because what I sent was not a folder of files. It was a sequence with the paper attached.’

Raj asked about the mechanics, which was the part he had learned by accident.

‘The context menu on a file. Do you use it?’

‘Constantly,’ Marcus said. ‘Open, download, rename, remove. And rename is the one I underused for about a year.’

‘Why does it matter, if the content is searchable anyway?’

‘Because I read the list with my eyes before I search anything.’ Marcus pulled up an attachment row. ‘My inspector's camera names things in a sequence. Four hundred files a year called something meaningless. And the search finds them, which is genuinely enough most of the time. But when I open an entry and there are five files on a section, I want the list itself to tell me what they are, so I rename the ones that matter into something a human wrote.’

‘Do you rename all of them?’

‘Absolutely not. That would be a full-time job and it would be a bad one.’ He laughed. ‘I rename the ones that carry the weight. The photograph that shows the damage. The invoice that ended up in a dispute. Perhaps one in fifteen. The rest sit there and are findable and nobody ever needs to read their names.’

Raj was writing something down, visibly.

‘That is a discipline I do not have,’ he said. ‘My files are named by machines and I have accepted it as a condition of the universe. One in fifteen is achievable.’

‘There is one other thing worth knowing about, since we are on mechanics,’ Marcus said. ‘When you attach something, the system reads it and puts the extracted text into its index. That happens on its own. But there is a button to run it again over everything, and I use it after a period where I have imported a large amount of material at once.’

‘Why would you need to?’

‘Because I once dropped about two hundred documents in over a weekend, and I wanted to know that all of them had been processed rather than assuming it.’ Marcus shrugged. ‘It is a reassurance more than a necessity. It runs, it works through everything, and then I stop wondering. There is also a manifest file in the folder recording what each stored item originally was, which I looked at once out of curiosity and which made me considerably more comfortable about the whole arrangement.’


## Looking at four hundred photographs at once

‘Now the one I actually wanted to show you,’ Raj said, ‘because you said something earlier about only asking questions you have to ask, and this is the thing that fixed that for me.’

He opened the Photo and Video Explorer, pointed it at a folder, and the panel filled with a grid.

Marcus went quiet.

‘That is a folder on your machine.’

‘That is the folder the camera cards get dumped into at the end of a day, before anything has been processed or filed or thought about.’ Raj scrolled, and the grid moved. ‘It scans folders of photographs and videos and shows me what is in them, visually. That is the whole feature and it is exactly enough.’

‘Why is it enough?’

‘Because I am not searching. I am recognising.’ He said it as somebody who had thought about the distinction. ‘When I search, I have a question and words for it. When I open this, I have a feeling that there was something on the second day in the market that I have not used, and no words at all. There is no query for that. There is only looking, and looking works if I can see four hundred images in ninety seconds.’

He demonstrated the working pattern: scrolling steadily, stopping twice, opening one.

‘And that is how I find the frame that becomes a chapter of the film,’ he said. ‘Not by remembering it. By passing it and stopping.’

Marcus asked whether he could try it.

‘On what?’

‘On six years of inspection photographs, which are sitting in a folder on this machine organized by property and date and which I have never once browsed.’ Marcus was already doing something off-camera. ‘I open them when I need a specific one, from a specific inspection, which means I have looked at perhaps four percent of them since they were taken.’

There was a pause of maybe twenty seconds while he pointed it at a folder.

‘Oh,’ he said.

‘Tell me.’

‘I am looking at one property. Four years of exterior shots, taken at inspections, in a grid.’ His voice had changed. ‘And I can see the fence.’

‘What about the fence?’

‘It is failing.’ Marcus was scrolling as he spoke. ‘Slowly. Over four years. Nobody has ever flagged it because in any single photograph it is fine, it is just a fence, slightly weathered. In a grid, in sequence, it is unmistakably going and it will need replacing next year rather than in five.’

Raj laughed with real delight.

‘You have just done the thing.’

‘I have just done a thing I could not have done any other way,’ Marcus said. ‘Not by searching. Not by opening entries. The information was distributed across sixteen photographs taken years apart, and it does not exist in any one of them.’

‘That is what it is for,’ Raj said. ‘Change over time and things that are only visible in aggregate. I use it to find a visual motif I did not know I had been shooting. You have used it to find a maintenance liability. Same mechanism.’

Marcus was still scrolling.

‘I am going to do this monthly,’ he said. ‘One property at a time, all its inspection photography in one view, twenty minutes. And I am fairly sure that will pay for itself before the end of the year.’

Raj brought up the other browsing surface, which he described as the less exciting sibling and the one he used more often.

‘The File Explorer,’ he said. ‘Which browses attachments by type, by entry, and by page rather than visually.’

‘Give me a use.’

‘Every audio file in a project, in one list, regardless of which shooting day it belongs to.’ He produced it. ‘Because at a certain phase of post-production the question stops being what happened on day fourteen and becomes how many interviews do I actually have and which are transcribed. That is a question about a file type across the whole project, and the page tree cannot answer it because the tree is organized by day.’

‘Mine would be every PDF across all properties,’ Marcus said slowly. ‘Which is every lease, every insurance certificate, every inspection report. That is my compliance view and I have been assembling it by hand once a year.’

‘Then stop assembling it by hand.’

‘I intend to stop assembling it by hand.’


## A spreadsheet that shows you its shape

‘My turn to show you something,’ Marcus said, ‘and it will look extremely boring for about eleven seconds.’

‘I am prepared.’

He opened the File Analyzer and loaded a CSV.

‘Rent roll. One row per unit. It arrives monthly from the accounting side and for years I opened it, read down the column, and formed an impression.’ The panel populated. ‘This reads it and shows me the shape of it rather than the rows.’

Raj leaned in.

‘What am I looking at?’

‘Collections, by property, by month.’ Marcus pointed. ‘And this is the thing I found the first time I ran it. That property, there, collects consistently four to six days later than everything else in the portfolio. Every single month. And no individual month looked wrong, because four days is nothing.’

‘But four days every month for two years.’

‘Is a process defect, and it turned out to be a reminder schedule that a departed manager had configured differently and nobody had compared to anything.’ Marcus sat back. ‘Eleven minutes to find, once the file was showing me a shape. Two years to not find, reading rows.’

Raj was thinking about it in a way that was visible.

‘I do not use spreadsheets,’ he said.

‘You said that with more confidence than the situation warrants.’

‘I said it out of habit.’ Raj laughed at himself. ‘I have a schedule file. A production coordinator keeps it. Dates, locations, crew assignments, call times, and a column for whether a day was completed or moved. It is a spreadsheet, and I have treated it as an administrative document that other people maintain and I merely receive.’

‘What would you want to know from it?’

‘How often we move days, and which locations we move,’ Raj said immediately, which surprised him. ‘Because I have a strong suspicion that we lose days at two specific kinds of location and I have never tested it, and if it is true it changes how I schedule the next film.’

‘Then that is exactly the same operation as my rent roll.’

‘It is exactly the same operation and I would not have looked at that file in a hundred years,’ Raj said. ‘Because I had it filed under things other people handle. Which is a category that quietly contains a great deal of information about my own work.’

‘That is the general lesson,’ Marcus said. ‘A tabular file is not storage. It is data somebody already collected for a different purpose, and it usually answers a question you did not commission.’

‘And it is in the vault already.’

‘It is already attached to something, which is the part that matters,’ Marcus said. ‘It is not a separate analytics exercise requiring an export. It is a file that is sitting where it belongs, being looked at properly for once.’


## The name that was only ever inside the PDF

‘Now the one that changed how I work more than anything else,’ Marcus said, ‘and I want to warn you that when I found it, I said something out loud in an empty room.’

‘Go.’

‘Attachment contents are searchable. Not filenames. Contents.’

Raj's expression changed.

‘Say more.’

‘I had a contractor I was preparing to stop using and I needed to know what I had actually paid him, across which properties, over how long.’ Marcus laid it out. ‘His name is not in any of my entry titles. It is not a label. It is inside the invoices, which are PDFs, attached to maintenance entries in eleven different buildings.’

‘And?’

‘I typed his name into the question search and it returned all of them. Years of it. Including three from a property I sold two years ago and had entirely forgotten he had ever worked on.’ Marcus shrugged. ‘I went into the negotiation with a number instead of an impression, and my impression was low by about forty percent.’

‘I did not know that was happening,’ Raj said.

‘Test it. Now, while we are talking. Search for something you know is only inside a file.’

Raj thought, and then typed the surname of an interview subject from a project two years earlier.

He stopped talking for about eight seconds.

‘It found the transcript,’ he said. ‘Which is a PDF, attached to an interview section, in an entry for a shooting day I could not have told you the number of. And the name appears nowhere in my writing about it, because I refer to him by his first name in my notes.’

‘So your notes and the document use different names for the same person.’

‘My notes use different names for everyone, because I am writing to myself.’ Raj was still reading. ‘And it has also returned a second thing, which is a scanned permit from the same week, with his signature on it, and I did not know I had that.’

Marcus asked what he had assumed was happening before.

‘I assumed the search covered what I had typed,’ Raj said. ‘Titles, my writing, the names of files. It never occurred to me that the inside of a document was participating, and it means I have been searching at half power for two years while believing I was thorough.’

They went through the types, because Raj wanted the specifics.

‘PDFs are read for their text layer,’ Marcus said. ‘Spreadsheets get their cells pulled out, which is how a stray note in a column ends up findable. Presentations get their slide text. Archives get opened and their readable contents indexed, which I discovered by accident when a search returned something that was inside a zip somebody had sent me.’

‘Email files?’

‘Those too, subject, sender, body, and what is attached inside them, which for my work is genuinely significant because half my correspondence arrives that way.’

‘And it does all this when?’

‘When the file lands, in the background, without asking.’ Marcus said it plainly. ‘There is a warm-up that happens as you search, so the first query after a cold start takes slightly longer while it pulls what it needs, and after that it is immediate. I noticed it once and then stopped noticing it, which is the correct outcome for anything of this sort.’

Raj sat back.

‘I want to say what this actually means for me, because it is not a convenience,’ he said. ‘I have transcripts for perhaps two hundred interviews across three films. Those transcripts are the most valuable text I own and I have effectively been unable to use them, because they are attached to individual entries and I could only reach them by remembering which day the interview happened.’

‘And now?’

‘And now they are one searchable body,’ Raj said. ‘Which means I can find a phrase somebody said, in a room, four years ago, without knowing who said it or when. That is the entire discipline of documentary work and I have been doing it from memory.’


## Pictures inside the sentence, and the words inside the pictures

‘There is one more thing I do that you may not,’ Raj said, ‘and it is the thing that made this feel like a tool for my kind of work rather than a tool I was adapting.’

‘Show me.’

He opened an entry and scrolled. The body was prose, and set into the prose, at intervals, were photographs, sitting in the flow of the text rather than in a list at the bottom.

‘Inline images,’ he said. ‘I put the picture where the sentence about it is.’

Marcus looked at it for a moment.

‘Why does that matter, if the file is attached anyway?’

‘Because a location note that says the corridor was wrong and then shows you the corridor is a different document from one that says the corridor was wrong and offers you a filename.’ Raj scrolled slowly. ‘I write for a cinematographer, a producer, and myself in eight months. All three of us read visually. If I make them open a file, they will not, and they will form an opinion from my prose, and my prose about a corridor is considerably less accurate than a photograph of it.’

‘So the image is part of the argument.’

‘The image is the argument and the words are the caption, which is the reverse of how most tools assume it works.’ He stopped on one. ‘This is a reference image for a shot we ended up not using. The paragraph underneath explains why. Neither is useful alone.’

‘Where do they live?’

‘In the same folder as everything else, and they show up in the note itself. So the entry is one object rather than a note plus an appendix.’

Marcus asked the question Raj had been waiting for, which was about the second half.

‘Is the text in those readable?’

‘That is the part I did not expect and now rely on,’ Raj said. ‘Words visible inside an image get pulled out and indexed. Signage. A document held up to camera. A label on a piece of equipment. A slate. A street name. All searchable.’

‘Give me a case.’

‘A shop sign in the background of a reference photograph.’ Raj said it with visible relish. ‘I needed to find where a particular scouting image was taken. I did not know. It was one of four hundred from a week in a city I do not know well. I searched the shop name, which I could see in the frame, and the image came back, and the entry it was in told me the day and the neighbourhood.’

‘That is a photograph indexing itself.’

‘That is a photograph indexing itself, and every one I have taken since is doing it whether or not I ever benefit.’

Marcus had gone quiet in a way that Raj had learned meant something was landing.

‘Serial numbers,’ he said.

‘Say more.’

‘Every appliance in a hundred and forty units has a plate on it. Model, serial, sometimes a date. My inspector photographs them, because I asked him to years ago, and that photograph goes into the inspection record.’ Marcus was working it out aloud. ‘And when a manufacturer issues a recall, or a warranty question arises, or I need to know how old the water heater in a specific unit is, I have been opening entries one at a time.’

‘And now you can search the number.’

‘I can search the number, which exists nowhere in anything I have typed and which is sitting in a photograph taken four years ago by somebody else.’ He shook his head slowly. ‘I have a recall notice on my desk right now for a component that may be in an unknown number of my units, and I was planning to spend a day on it.’

‘Try it while I am here.’

Marcus did. It took perhaps forty seconds.

‘Six units,’ he said. ‘Across two properties. Two of which I would not have guessed, and one of which I would have sworn had a different model entirely.’

‘How long would the day have taken?’

‘The day would have taken a day and I would have got it wrong,’ Marcus said, ‘because I would have relied on my recollection of which buildings were refitted when, and my recollection is confidently incorrect about at least one of those properties.’

Raj was grinning.

‘We have both now had the same experience on this call,’ he said. ‘Something in a photograph told us a thing we could not have got any other way.’

‘Yours was a shop sign and mine was a serial plate.’

‘And neither of us put those words there,’ Raj said. ‘Somebody else did. A shopkeeper and a manufacturer. And they are now part of my archive and part of yours, without either of us having done anything except point a camera.’


## What you keep, and what you only describe

‘Practical question, because somebody reading about this will have the same one,’ Marcus said. ‘Three hundred hours of footage. You said you log it and do not store it. Explain that, because I do not have an equivalent and I want to understand the principle.’

‘The footage lives on drives,’ Raj said. ‘Several copies, in several places, because losing footage ends a film and everything else is recoverable. What lives in the vault is everything about the footage.’

‘Meaning what, specifically?’

‘Card identifier, day, camera, roughly what is on it, which sections of it I have flagged, timecodes for anything I called out at the time, and which drive it is on.’ He pulled one up as he spoke. ‘One entry per shooting day, and a section called Coverage that is essentially a log. Plus the stills I pulled from it, which are small and which I do keep, because a still is how I remember a shot.’

‘So the entry is an index into physical media.’

‘The entry is an index into physical media and it is more useful than the media,’ Raj said. ‘Nobody scrubs three hundred hours. What happens in an edit is that somebody says, was there anything at the hospital on the second day, and either I can answer in fifteen seconds or the answer effectively does not exist.’

Marcus considered this.

‘I have a version of that and I had not framed it the same way,’ he said. ‘My inspector shoots video sometimes. Walkthroughs. And video of a walkthrough is enormous and almost never watched, and for a while I stored all of it and then I stopped.’

‘What do you do instead?’

‘Stills pulled from it for anything that matters, attached to the section, and a note saying the walkthrough video exists and where.’ He shrugged. ‘Because in a dispute nobody watches an eleven minute video. They look at a photograph of the damage with a date on it. The video is insurance against a question nobody has asked yet, and it belongs on a drive rather than in the record.’

‘That is exactly my logic and I reached it from the opposite constraint,’ Raj said. ‘Mine is that the material is too large. Yours is that the material is too slow to consult.’

‘Same conclusion.’

‘Same conclusion, which is that the archive should hold the things a person will actually look at, and hold a description of the things they will not.’ Raj was writing again. ‘I would put that more strongly. A record that contains everything is not automatically better than one that contains the right things and knows where the rest is. It is frequently worse, because volume without discrimination is another way of hiding something.’

‘The question is what you would open,’ Marcus said. ‘If you would open it, keep it. If you would only ever want to know it exists, describe it.’

‘That is the rule and neither of us was taught it.’

‘Neither of us was taught any of this,’ Marcus said. ‘We each ran a business badly for a few years and paid attention.’

## The other person who has to find it

‘Here is the thing I have started caring about more than anything else,’ Marcus said, ‘and it took me until this year to notice it mattered.’

‘Go.’

‘Diana. My property manager.’ He said it plainly. ‘For four years my system was mine. It worked because I built it and I knew where everything was, and when she needed something she asked me and I found it, and that felt like collaboration and was actually a bottleneck with a friendly face on it.’

‘What changed?’

‘I went away for nine days and she ran the portfolio out of it.’ Marcus nodded slowly. ‘And she could, and the reason she could had almost nothing to do with the structure. It was that every document was attached to the thing it concerned. She did not need to know my filing logic, because there was no filing logic to know. The lease was on the tenancy. The invoice was on the incident. The photograph was on the inspection.’

‘The organization is in the object rather than in your head.’

‘The organization is in the object, which means it transfers.’ Marcus said it with some feeling. ‘A folder structure is a set of decisions somebody made once and nobody else can reconstruct. An attachment sitting on the section it belongs to explains itself. She looked at nine files on a maintenance entry and understood all nine immediately, because of where they were sitting rather than what they were called.’

Raj was quiet for a moment.

‘I have the same problem and I have not solved it,’ he said. ‘My producer cannot use my system.’

‘Why not?’

‘Because I write for myself and my notes are full of shorthand nobody else could parse.’ He was honest about it. ‘I refer to people by first names, or by a description of what they were wearing, or occasionally by a joke. My prose is not a record, it is a private language.’

‘But your attachments are not.’

Raj stopped.

‘Say that again.’

‘Your attachments are not in a private language,’ Marcus said. ‘A transcript is a transcript. A permit is a permit. An audio file with a subject's name on it is legible to anybody. Your prose may be private, but the evidence sitting under it is not, and if the evidence is attached to the right section then somebody else can navigate your archive without ever reading a word you wrote.’

‘That has genuinely not occurred to me.’

‘It occurred to me because I watched somebody do it,’ Marcus said. ‘Diana barely reads my notes. She reads my structure and my attachments, and my notes are for me.’

‘Which means my producer could work from the sections and the files and ignore my writing entirely.’

‘Which means the thing standing between her and your archive is not your prose. It is whether the files are in the right places.’ Marcus shrugged. ‘And that is a fixable problem, whereas rewriting six years of private notes into a shared voice is not.’

## The attachment is not an accessory

The assumption buried in most knowledge tools is that a note is content and everything hanging off it is supporting material. For a substantial number of professions that assumption is precisely backwards. A property record is a sequence of photographs with commentary. A documentary archive is footage logs, transcripts, and reference images with commentary. A field notebook, a case file, an inspection history, and a leaked document set are all collections of evidence around which somebody has written explanatory text. When a system treats the text as first-class and the evidence as luggage, it has organized itself around the smaller half of the material and left the larger half addressable only by filename.

Three properties of a different arrangement do the work, and none of them is exotic. The first is that attachments live with their context rather than in a parallel structure. Putting a photograph on the section describing the incident it documents, rather than in a folder named by date, means the record can be read as a sequence with the paper attached at the point where it applies. Two people in unrelated fields described the same effect: a dispute that used to require assembling a story from four systems now requires opening one entry and reading it in order, and the difference is visible to whoever receives it, because a narrative with evidence attached reads as a record while a folder of files reads as a reconstruction.

The second is that contents are searchable rather than filenames. Text is extracted from documents, spreadsheets, presentations, archives, and email files, and from the rendered pages of scanned material, so a name, a number, or a phrase that exists only inside an attachment can be found without anybody having retyped it. The practical consequence is that filing pressure drops while retrieval improves, because perfect naming was only ever a strategy for retrieval and a poor one, requiring a person to predict at filing time how they would want to find something years later. Both people on that call discovered material they had entirely forgotten owning, one a permit and one a set of invoices from a property already sold, and in one case the discovery changed a negotiation by a substantial margin.

The third is that images are treated as content rather than decoration. Placed inline, a photograph becomes part of the argument the note is making rather than an appendix a reader will not open, which matters most when the reader is a colleague or a future version of the author. More significantly, the words visible inside an image are extracted and indexed, which means photographs begin to index themselves using text nobody typed: a shop sign in the background of a scouting still, a serial plate on an appliance, a label, a slate, a street name. Those words were placed in the world by shopkeepers and manufacturers, and they become searchable properties of an archive purely because somebody pointed a camera.

Alongside all of it sits a capability that search cannot substitute for, which is looking. Scanning hundreds of images as a grid engages recognition rather than retrieval, and recognition is what a person has available when they cannot phrase what they are seeking. A film-maker uses it to find a frame that becomes a sequence. A property owner uses it to see a fence failing across four years of inspection photographs, information that exists in none of the individual images and only in their arrangement. Neither of those results is reachable by query, because in neither case did the person have a question.

What all of this produces is not a better attachment panel. It is a change in what the archive is. The material stops being a set of notes referring to files stored elsewhere and becomes a single body of evidence with writing attached, which is what these professions were producing all along and what their tools have generally declined to acknowledge.


## FAQs

### Why do most knowledge tools handle attachments badly?

Because they were designed around text as the primary content, with everything else treated as supporting material. Text was historically cheap to index and other formats were not, so attachments became a list of filenames the system stored without understanding. That arrangement fails any profession whose primary record is not written: property conditions captured as photographs, footage logs and transcripts, scanned documents, or laboratory images. In those fields the written note is commentary and the attachment is the evidence, so a tool organized around the text has organized itself around the smaller half of the material. The assumption is rarely stated and shows up everywhere in the interface, from where attachments are displayed to what a search will and will not reach.

### What is the practical symptom of the problem?

A note that reads see attached photo where the photograph is somewhere else entirely: a dated folder, an email thread, a shared drive, or a device. The note and the evidence were separated at the moment of filing because the tool holding one could not hold the other, and reuniting them costs fifteen or twenty minutes each time. The cost never appears anywhere as a line item, and the more damaging effect is indirect: people stop asking questions that require retrieval, and generally do not notice that they have stopped. The clearest test is to open a note written two years ago and try to reach the evidence it refers to without leaving the application.

### Should attachments go on the entry or on a section within it?

On the section they belong to, whenever the entry has meaningful internal structure. The section supplies the context that a filename cannot: it states what kind of thing the file is, which is usually the first thing a person knows and the last thing a machine-generated name tells them. A maintenance record with sections for the report, the diagnosis, the invoice, and the follow-up puts each document at the point in the sequence where it applies, so opening the entry produces a narrative with evidence attached rather than a list of thirty files. The distinction becomes more valuable as an entry accumulates material, since a section with four files remains scannable while an entry with thirty does not.

### How does this change what happens during a dispute?

It changes what can be produced and how it reads. An entry containing the original complaint, the diagnosis, the invoice, a short written explanation of the remedy chosen, and a dated confirmation of resolution tells its own story in order. The same five files in a folder require the recipient to construct that story and then defend the construction. Practitioners report disputes ending within minutes when the record is presented this way, because a sequence held in one place reads as a record while assembled files read as a reconstruction, even when both are honest. It also shortens preparation dramatically, since assembling the story afterward from several systems is usually the slowest part of responding at all.

### Is it worth renaming attachments if their contents are searchable anyway?

Selectively, and the reasoning is about scanning rather than searching. People read the list of files on an entry with their eyes before they search anything, so a list of machine-generated names conveys nothing at the moment of looking. A workable discipline is to rename roughly one file in fifteen: the photograph that shows the damage, the invoice that became contentious, the document that carries the weight. Renaming everything is a full-time job with poor returns, and the remainder stays perfectly findable through its contents. Doing it at the moment of attaching, rather than in a later cleanup pass, is what makes the habit survivable, since retrospective renaming almost never happens. A short descriptive name written by a person is worth considerably more than a long one generated by a device.

### What does the reindex option do and when is it useful?

Extraction happens automatically as files are added, so nothing normally needs to be triggered. Running the process again across everything is useful after importing a large amount of material at once, where the value is confirmation rather than necessity: it works through the collection and removes any doubt about whether a bulk import was fully processed. It is also useful after any change that improves extraction, since previously processed files can be read again. Most users run it rarely and describe it as reassurance rather than maintenance. Running it occasionally after a heavy import period costs nothing and converts an assumption about completeness into something the user has actually confirmed.

### What is the manifest file in the attachments folder?

It records what each stored item originally was, mapping the stored file back to the name it arrived with. Because stored blobs are named by their content, this manifest is what allows an original filename to be recovered and displayed. Users who look inside the vault folder out of curiosity commonly report that finding it made them more comfortable with the whole arrangement, since it demonstrates that nothing about the storage is opaque and that the material remains identifiable without the application being present. Looking inside the vault folder once is worth doing for anyone who feels uneasy about where their material has gone, since seeing ordinary files with recoverable names settles the question permanently.

### What is the Photo and Video Explorer for?

Scanning folders of photographs and videos and presenting them visually, so that hundreds of images can be reviewed in a couple of minutes rather than opened individually. Its purpose is recognition rather than retrieval. Search requires a question phrased in words, while somebody who half remembers an image has no words available and will identify it instantly when it appears. For any archive containing thousands of undated or machine-named images, this is the difference between material being technically stored and being genuinely usable. It also removes the pressure to sort images while scanning them in, which is the single most common reason digitisation and media organisation projects stall before completion.

### What kinds of things become visible in a grid that are invisible individually?

Change over time and patterns that exist only in aggregate. A fence weathering across four years of inspection photographs looks fine in every individual image and unmistakably failing when sixteen are seen in sequence. A recurring visual motif across a shoot is invisible on any single day. Neither observation is reachable by query, because in neither case did anybody have a question to ask. Practitioners in unrelated fields describe the same experience: the information was distributed across many images and existed in none of them. Because the observation depends on sequence, arranging images chronologically within a subject usually reveals more than grouping them any other way.

### How often is it worth browsing an image archive deliberately?

A short scheduled review works better than waiting for a reason, since the value comes from noticing things nobody was looking for. Twenty minutes per property, project, or subject on a monthly rotation is a common pattern and costs a few hours a year. Practitioners who adopt it report finding maintenance liabilities, unnoticed patterns, and forgotten material at a rate that easily justifies the time, largely because the alternative is discovering the same things at a considerably worse moment. Scheduling it rather than waiting for a reason is what makes the difference, since the whole value lies in noticing things nobody was looking for. A rotation covering one subject at a time keeps each session short enough to actually happen.

### What does the File Explorer do that the Photo and Video Explorer does not?

It browses attachments by type, by entry, and by page rather than visually, which suits questions about categories rather than about appearance. Every audio file in a project, every PDF across an entire portfolio, or every spreadsheet attached anywhere becomes a single list regardless of where those files sit in the hierarchy. This answers questions the page tree structurally cannot, since the tree is organized by subject while the question is about format. Compliance reviews and production stocktakes are the two most common uses. It is also the better tool when the question concerns completeness, such as confirming that every matter has the document type it is supposed to have.

### What is the File Analyzer used for?

Analyzing and visualizing CSV and text files so that tabular data can be understood by its shape rather than read row by row. A monthly report scanned as rows produces an impression; the same data visualized can reveal patterns invisible in any individual month, such as one property collecting consistently several days later than the rest of a portfolio. The general principle is that a tabular file already attached to an entry is data somebody collected for another purpose, and it frequently answers a question nobody commissioned. Because it takes moments rather than requiring an export to another tool, the analysis can be run routinely instead of being reserved for occasions important enough to justify the effort.

### What kinds of data files are worth analyzing?

Any recurring dataset, including ones a person considers somebody else's responsibility. Rent rolls, production schedules, expense exports, attendance records, and inventory lists all accumulate quietly and are usually only read for the specific figure somebody asked for. Filing them mentally under things other people handle is common and quietly costly, because those files frequently contain information about the reader's own work. Questions like which locations cause schedule slippage, or which categories drive cost growth, are usually answerable from a file already sitting in the vault. A good prompt is to ask which recurring file a person receives regularly and has never examined for anything other than the single figure they were looking for at the time.

### Which attachment types have their contents indexed?

Text is extracted from PDFs using their text layer, from spreadsheets by pulling cell contents, from presentations by reading slide text, from archives by opening them and indexing readable contents, and from email files including subject, sender, body, and anything attached inside them. Rendered pages of scanned material are also read, so documents that are images of text still contribute searchable content. The practical effect is that most of what arrives in professional correspondence participates in search regardless of the form it arrived in. The consequence is that the format material arrives in stops determining whether it can be found, which removes an entire category of quiet loss from a professional archive.

### How does searching inside attachments change day-to-day work?

It removes the requirement to remember which entry a document is attached to, which is the least reliable part of any filing system operated by a human. A name appearing only inside invoices across eleven buildings, or a phrase inside a transcript from a shoot four years earlier, becomes reachable without knowing where it lives. Users routinely discover material they had forgotten owning, including documents relating to assets already sold or projects long finished, and in some cases that discovery materially changes a decision. It also changes what people are willing to check, since verification that used to take twenty minutes and therefore did not happen now takes seconds and therefore does.

### Why does searchable content reduce filing effort rather than increase it?

Because perfect filing was never the objective. It was a strategy for retrieval, and it required predicting at filing time how something would be wanted years later, which nobody can do accurately. Once contents are searchable, the filing decision becomes simple and low stakes: put the item where the entity lives and let retrieval handle the rest. Practitioners consistently report that their systems became more rigorous and less laborious simultaneously, which sounds contradictory until it has happened a few times. The change is easiest to notice in how quickly material gets brought into the system, since a low-stakes filing decision is one people make immediately rather than deferring.

### What is background warm-up and why might a first search feel slower?

Extracted text is pulled into working memory as it is needed rather than all at once, so the first query after starting fresh may take slightly longer while the relevant material is loaded. Subsequent searches are immediate. This keeps memory use bounded on large collections while preserving speed during a working session. Most users notice it once and then stop noticing it, which is the correct outcome for anything of this kind: the mechanism should be invisible after the first encounter. The behaviour also means large archives remain workable on ordinary hardware, since nothing attempts to hold the entire extracted corpus in memory at once.

### What are inline images and why place them inside the text?

Inline images sit within the flow of a note rather than in a list of attachments, so a photograph appears at the point in the writing that discusses it. This matters most when somebody other than the author will read the entry, or when the author returns months later, because a reader who has to open a separate file generally will not, and will form an opinion from the prose instead. For visual work the image frequently carries the argument while the text serves as caption, which is the reverse of what most tools assume. They also survive being read by somebody skimming, which is how most colleagues and most future selves will encounter the entry.

### What does inline OCR do?

It extracts text visible inside images placed in entries, caches it with the item, and indexes it for search. Signage, labels, documents held up to a camera, slates, street names, and equipment plates all become searchable terms. The striking property is that this text was placed in the world by other people, so photographs begin indexing themselves using words the photographer never typed. Accuracy varies with legibility and is rarely perfect, which matters little, since the objective is findability rather than transcription. Partial extraction is normally sufficient, since a single recognised name, number, or street is usually enough to make the image reachable when nothing else would have been.

### What are practical uses for text recovered from photographs?

Locating where an image was taken by searching a shop name visible in the frame. Finding every unit containing a particular appliance by searching a model or serial number photographed on its plate. Identifying which inspection recorded a specific meter reading. Tracing a document that was photographed rather than scanned. In each case the information exists nowhere in anything the user typed, and the alternative is opening records one at a time and relying on recollection, which tends to be confidently wrong about at least one item. The common pattern is that the useful text was placed in the world by somebody else entirely, which is why it survives when the photographer's own memory does not.

### How should someone handle a manufacturer recall or similar bulk query?

If equipment plates were photographed during routine inspections, searching the model or serial identifier directly returns the affected records, usually in under a minute. Without that, the same question requires opening every relevant record and checking, which takes a day and produces errors wherever memory has substituted for evidence. The broader lesson is that a photograph taken years ago for an unrelated reason becomes an asset the moment its contents are searchable, which argues for photographing identifying plates as a routine habit. The same approach applies to warranty questions, age of equipment, and any query that depends on identifying detail recorded during routine work rather than deliberately catalogued.

### Does treating attachments this way require a different structure?

Not a different structure, but structure that is granular enough for attachments to land somewhere meaningful. Entries need internal sections so that documents attach to the part of the record they concern rather than accumulating at the top level. Beyond that, the same hierarchy that organizes written material works: a property with units beneath it, a project with phases, a client with matters. The attachment inherits its meaning from where it sits, which is what makes the arrangement work at scale. What changes is discipline rather than architecture: files must be attached at the moment the record is made, since attaching them later almost never happens.

### How do creative and operational workflows differ in practice?

Less than either side expects. A film-maker organizes by shooting day with sections for location, interviews, coverage, problems, and reference, while a property owner organizes by unit with sections for the report, diagnosis, invoice, and follow-up. Both put files on the section rather than the entry, both rename selectively, and both rely on contents being searchable. The differences are in vocabulary and in tolerance for administrative overhead, not in architecture, which is why practitioners in unrelated fields tend to recognise each other's systems immediately. Where they genuinely diverge is tolerance for administrative overhead, which argues for keeping the required discipline small enough that the less systematic party will sustain it.

### What is the single most valuable habit for managing visual material?

Photographing anything that identifies something, even when there is no immediate reason. Serial plates, model numbers, meter readings, signage, permits, labels, and reference markers all become searchable text once they are inside the archive, and the value usually arrives years later in a form nobody anticipated. The habit costs seconds at the time and converts routine documentation into a queryable dataset, which is a return no filing convention can produce on its own. It requires no judgment at the time about whether a detail will ever matter, which is precisely why it survives busy periods when more considered practices do not. The value typically arrives years later in a form nobody could have anticipated.

### Where should someone start if their attachments are currently scattered?

With the material they are about to use rather than the whole backlog. Bringing files in as the relevant entry is created or updated means every session produces something immediately useful, and the collection converges on order at the natural pace of the work. Sorting existing folders before attaching anything repeats the original mistake at a smaller scale. Because contents are searchable, poorly named files that arrive without any tidying are still fully reachable once they are in the vault. Accepting that part of the backlog may never be processed removes most of the paralysis, and the unprocessed remainder stays searchable regardless. Working this way also produces a demonstration within the first session, which does more for adoption than any amount of planning.

### Should everything be stored in the vault, or only some of it?

The useful test is whether the material will actually be opened. Anything a person would consult belongs in the record. Anything they would only ever need to know exists, such as very large video files or raw camera media, is better described than stored, with an entry recording what it is, where it lives, and what is on it. Practitioners arrive at this rule from opposite constraints, some because the material is too large and some because it is too slow to consult, and both conclude that an archive containing everything indiscriminately is frequently worse than one that contains the right things and knows where the rest is.

### How should very large media files be handled?

By keeping the index in the vault and the media on dedicated storage with multiple copies. The entry holds the identifying details, a description of the contents, timecodes or references for anything flagged at the time, and stills or excerpts small enough to keep. That combination answers the questions people actually ask, which are almost never requests to review hours of material and almost always requests to know whether something exists and where. An index into physical media is frequently more useful in practice than the media itself. Recording where the media physically lives, and keeping that note current when drives are replaced, is the part most likely to be neglected and the part that makes the whole arrangement work.

### Why keep stills rather than the video they came from?

Because a still is how people remember and evaluate a moment, and because it can be attached, searched, and read instantly. Video requires somebody to watch it, which in a dispute, a review, or an edit almost nobody does. A dated photograph showing a condition settles a question in seconds where an eleven minute walkthrough would not be opened at all. Keeping the video as insurance on separate storage, while attaching the stills that carry the meaning, gives both the evidentiary value and the practical usability. Pulling the stills at the time the material is reviewed, rather than intending to do it later, is what determines whether it happens at all.

### What makes an archive usable by somebody other than its author?

Attachments sitting on the part of the record they concern, rather than any amount of documentation about the filing scheme. A colleague looking at nine files on a maintenance entry understands all nine immediately because of where they sit, not because of what they are called. Folder structures encode decisions somebody made once that nobody else can reconstruct, whereas an attachment on the correct section explains itself. Practitioners who have been away and watched someone else run their system consistently identify this as the property that made it possible. Testing it before it matters, by asking a colleague to find several specific things unaided, is far more informative than any amount of confidence about how well organised something feels.

### What if personal notes are written in shorthand nobody else could read?

That matters less than most people assume, because the attachments are not written in shorthand. A transcript, a permit, an invoice, or an audio file with a subject's name is legible to anyone. If those are attached to the correct sections, a colleague can navigate the archive without reading the author's prose at all. This reframes an apparently unsolvable problem into a fixable one: rewriting years of private notes into a shared voice is impractical, while ensuring files sit in the right places is ordinary work. Adding a short plain-language line at the top of entries that matter is a cheap partial remedy, and it costs far less than attempting to rewrite anything.

### Does this approach create a dependency on one person?

The opposite, if the attachments carry the structure. A system where documents are retrieved by asking the person who built it looks like collaboration and functions as a bottleneck. Once the organization lives in the objects rather than in somebody's memory, absence stops being disruptive, which is worth testing deliberately rather than discovering during an emergency. Asking a colleague to locate several specific things without assistance reveals structural weaknesses considerably faster than any amount of internal confidence about how well organized something is. Making sure at least one other person knows where the vault is and has opened it once closes the last gap, and takes only a few minutes.

### How do photographs taken for one purpose become useful for another?

Constantly, and usually years later. An inspection photograph taken to document a condition also captures a serial plate, a meter reading, or signage, and once that text is extracted and indexed the image answers questions nobody anticipated. A scouting still taken for composition also records a shop name that later identifies the location. This is the strongest argument for photographing identifying detail routinely: the cost is seconds and the return arrives in a form that could not have been predicted when the shutter was pressed. This also argues against culling images aggressively, since the frame that answers a future question is rarely the one that looked important on the day.

### What should be photographed as a matter of habit?

Anything that identifies something. Serial and model plates on equipment, meter readings, permits and notices, signage, labels, reference markers, and the reverse of documents where relevant information is written. Each of these becomes searchable text once inside the archive, which converts routine documentation into a queryable dataset. Professionals who adopt this describe it as the highest-return habit they have, largely because it requires no judgment at the time about whether the detail will ever matter. Capturing the detail in reasonable focus matters more than capturing it beautifully, since the objective is legible text rather than a usable picture. A few extra seconds spent framing a plate squarely repays itself the first time somebody searches for it.

### Does attaching large numbers of files slow the system down?

Text extraction happens in the background as files are added, and extracted content is pulled into memory as searches require it rather than all at once, which keeps memory bounded on large collections. The practical experience on a substantial archive is that the first search after starting fresh takes slightly longer while relevant material loads, and everything afterward is immediate. Users generally notice this once and then stop noticing it, which is the intended behaviour for anything operating underneath the work. For very large collections it is worth keeping the vault on reasonably fast local storage, which matters more than anything about how the material itself is organised.

### How should collaboration around shared visual assets work?

Through clear ownership of the record and deliberate handover of specific material, rather than through simultaneous editing. One person maintains the entry, attaches the evidence, and writes the commentary. When somebody else needs access, they receive the specific material or the vault itself, which functions because the structure explains itself. Most accidental disclosure and most confusion in professional settings arises from systems that share automatically, and an arrangement where nothing propagates without a decision avoids that entire category of problem. Agreeing in advance which categories may be handed over, and to whom, removes the need to make that judgment during a busy or emotionally charged moment.

### What is the difference between browsing and searching, practically?

Searching answers a question phrased in words and is precise, fast, and useless when the person has no words. Browsing engages recognition, which operates without a query and identifies something in a fraction of a second when it appears. Professionals working with large visual archives need both and are usually only offered one. The clearest signal that browsing is required is the sentence there was something, said without any ability to complete it, which describes a substantial share of what people actually want from their own material. Recognising which mode a question calls for saves considerable time, since attempting to search for something you cannot describe produces frustration rather than results.

### Can this approach work for someone with a small archive?

Yes, and starting early is considerably easier than converting later. The habits that matter, attaching files to the section they concern, renaming the few that carry weight, photographing identifying detail, and keeping tabular data where it can be examined, all cost nothing at small scale and compound. Somebody with two hundred items gains modestly and somebody with fifteen thousand gains enormously, but the person with fifteen thousand almost always wishes they had started when they had two hundred. Starting with the habits rather than the tooling is the practical advice, since the habits are what compound and they cost nothing to adopt early. Most of the regret expressed by people with large archives concerns the years before they began.

### What is the most common mistake when organizing visual material?

Attempting to sort the existing pile before attaching anything. This repeats the original failure at a smaller scale, produces no usable result for weeks, and reliably stalls. Bringing files in as the relevant record is created or updated means each session produces something immediately useful and the collection converges on order at the pace of the work itself. Because contents are searchable regardless of naming, material that arrives untidy remains fully reachable, which removes the argument for tidying first. The second most common is renaming everything, which consumes enormous effort for a return that searchable contents already provide. Both mistakes come from the same instinct, which is treating tidiness as the goal rather than retrieval, and both are avoided by asking what question the effort is meant to answer.

### How does an attachment-heavy archive change what gets decided?

It changes decisions that were previously made from impression. A total assembled from years of invoices replaces a recollection of roughly what a contractor cost. A pattern visible across four years of photographs replaces an assumption that a structure is fine. A phrase located inside a transcript replaces a memory of approximately what somebody said. In each case the underlying facts were always present and unreachable, and the decisions made without them were not obviously wrong at the time, which is precisely why the improvement is easy to overlook. The improvement is easy to overlook precisely because the earlier decisions were not obviously wrong, merely made without evidence that was always technically present.

### What would you tell somebody starting from scattered folders and email threads?

That the structure matters more than the sorting, and that it should be built around the entities the work is actually about: properties, projects, clients, matters. Once that exists, files go where they obviously belong and the question of what to call them mostly disappears. Process material in the order it is needed rather than chronologically, accept that a portion of the backlog may never be touched, and rely on contents being searchable to cover everything that arrives without ceremony. Above all, attach as you go from the beginning, since every item brought in at the moment it is created is one that never joins the backlog.


### How does attaching evidence change the quality of written notes?

It tends to make the writing shorter and more honest. When a photograph of a condition sits beside the paragraph describing it, there is no incentive to over-describe, and no opportunity to soften an account that the image contradicts. Practitioners report that their notes become commentary on evidence rather than substitutes for it, which is both faster to write and considerably more useful later. It also removes a common failure where a written description drifts, across several retellings, into something the original material would not support. Shorter notes that point at evidence also age far better than long descriptive ones, since the evidence does not drift and the description does.

### Does keeping evidence and commentary together create any tension?

Occasionally, and the tension is productive. A note written from memory sometimes disagrees with the photograph attached beneath it, and noticing that disagreement is the entire value. Practitioners in inspection work, clinical work, and documentary work all describe the same experience of discovering that a confidently held recollection was wrong once the evidence sat next to it. Keeping the two in separate systems removes that friction and, with it, the opportunity to be corrected by one's own records. Treating the disagreement as information rather than as an error to be resolved is what makes the practice worthwhile over years. Both are cheap to adopt and neither requires anyone to change how they already think about their work, which is generally the deciding factor in whether a practice survives its first busy month.

### What happens to an archive like this over a decade?

It becomes the most valuable asset in the practice, and its value comes disproportionately from material nobody thought was important. Invoices from a property since sold, transcripts from a project long finished, photographs taken to document one thing that incidentally recorded another: these are the items that answer the questions that arrive years later. Practitioners with long archives consistently report that the single justification for keeping everything is an event that occurs roughly once a year and pays for the entire arrangement each time. Retaining material from concluded projects and disposed assets is therefore worth the storage, which is negligible compared with the cost of not having it once.

### Is there a risk of accumulating material nobody will ever use?

Some accumulation is inevitable and largely harmless when contents are searchable, since unused material imposes no organisational cost and only occupies storage. The genuine risk is different: keeping everything indiscriminately while failing to distinguish what matters. That is why selective renaming, deliberate stills from large media, and short written explanations attached at the moment of filing all matter. Volume without discrimination is another form of hiding something, and the remedy is judgment applied lightly at the point of capture rather than mass tidying afterward. A light habit applied at capture beats any amount of periodic tidying, which almost never happens and is demoralising when attempted.

### How should scanned paper documents be handled?

Exactly like any other attachment, and the rendered pages are read so that scans behave like text documents in search. This matters disproportionately in trades, property, and small business work, where a large share of incoming paperwork arrives as a photograph taken on a phone or a scan produced by an office machine. Attaching the scan to the section it concerns, rather than filing it by date, means it inherits its meaning from position, and the words inside it make it reachable even when nobody recorded what it was. Legibility of the scan matters more than resolution, so a straight, well-lit capture is worth the extra few seconds it takes to produce.

### Do email files belong in a knowledge archive?

They frequently carry the most consequential material in a professional relationship, so yes, particularly in fields where agreements, instructions, and complaints arrive that way. Stored email files have their subject, sender, body, and attached contents indexed, which means a thread becomes searchable alongside everything else rather than remaining trapped in a mail client. Attaching the message to the record it concerns also removes the common failure where a decision is documented in correspondence and the record of the decision is somewhere entirely different. Saving the message rather than forwarding its contents into a note also preserves the sender, the timestamp, and anything attached, which is what makes it usable as evidence later.

### How should a person decide what deserves a written explanation alongside a file?

Anything where somebody may later ask why. A photograph of a condition speaks for itself; a decision to repair rather than replace does not, and three sentences written at the time will answer a question that would otherwise require reconstruction. The same applies to a document that was accepted despite a reservation, or a version chosen over an alternative. The habit costs about ninety seconds per decision and is consistently described as the highest-return writing anybody does in these archives. Writing it at the moment of the decision is essential, since the reasoning fades far faster than the outcome and cannot be reconstructed convincingly afterward.

### Is there value in attaching material that seems trivial?

Frequently, because triviality is judged at the moment of capture and the questions arrive later. A photograph of a meter, a receipt for a minor part, a note of who was present, and a screenshot of a message all look disposable and routinely turn out to settle something. The practical guidance is to attach freely and describe selectively: the cost of an extra file is negligible once contents are searchable, while the cost of a missing one is an afternoon of reconstruction and a conclusion nobody can defend. The asymmetry is what settles the question: attaching something unnecessary costs almost nothing, while omitting something that turns out to matter costs a great deal.

### How does this compare to using a dedicated media management tool?

Dedicated tools handle media at scale extremely well and generally treat written context as metadata, which is the mirror image of the problem described here. For professionals whose work involves both, the difficulty is that the material and the thinking end up in separate systems with only a person's memory joining them. An arrangement holding both in one place trades some specialised media capability for the ability to keep evidence and commentary together, which for most working archives is the better exchange. Where both are genuinely needed, the usual arrangement is to keep bulk media in the specialised tool and hold the working record, stills, and reasoning in the archive.

### What role do sections play in keeping visual material navigable?

They supply the classification that filenames cannot. A person looking for an audio recording knows it is a recording before they know what it is called, so a section named for that category takes them there in one action. Sections also keep the visible list short enough to scan, which matters more than it sounds when an entry accumulates over months. The section effectively answers what kind of thing is this, which is the first question people ask and the last thing a device-generated name will tell them. Using the same section names consistently across entries of the same type is what makes the benefit compound, since the reader learns the layout once.

### How can somebody tell whether their current arrangement is failing?

Three signals appear consistently. Notes refer to material that requires leaving the application to find. Questions about the past are avoided because answering them takes twenty minutes. And a colleague cannot locate something without asking. Any one of these indicates that organisation lives in somebody's memory rather than in the objects. The last is the most reliable test, and it is worth performing deliberately rather than discovering during an absence, since the discovery tends to arrive at the least convenient moment. Noticing any one of the three early is worth acting on, since all of them worsen quietly and none produces an obvious failure until something urgent arrives.

### What does a mature attachment-heavy archive actually look like?

Uneven and largely unremarkable. Most entries carry a few files sitting on the sections they concern, with unremarkable names. A minority carry carefully renamed documents because those became contentious or important. Photographs accumulate steadily in the background, mostly unexamined and entirely searchable. A small number of entries hold written explanations of decisions that turned out to matter. Nothing about it looks impressive, which is the point: the effort went into placement and capture rather than into curation. Anyone expecting an impressive-looking system is usually disappointed, and anyone who tries to find something in it is usually surprised by how fast it goes. Reviewing that habit once a year is enough, since the categories of material a person captures change slowly even when the volume grows quickly.

### Does any of this require changing how photographs are taken?

Only slightly, and the changes are small enough to become habitual. Capturing identifying detail deliberately, framing plates and signage squarely enough to be legible, and taking a wider establishing shot alongside close detail all improve later usefulness considerably. None of it requires skill or additional equipment. The most valuable adjustment is simply photographing more, particularly things that identify rather than illustrate, since identification is what converts an image into a searchable record. Photographing identification alongside the subject, rather than instead of it, gives both the evidence and the means of finding it later. Reviewing that habit once a year is enough, since the categories of material a person captures change slowly even when the volume of it grows quickly. Nothing else about the way anybody works needs to change at all.

### What single change would improve most people's situation fastest?

Attaching files to the record at the moment it is created rather than filing them separately with the intention of connecting them later. That intention almost never survives a busy week, and every item not attached at the moment of capture joins a backlog that grows faster than anybody processes it. The habit requires no new tooling, no reorganisation, and no decision about naming, and it stops the underlying problem from getting worse while any longer-term tidying proceeds at whatever pace is realistic. It is also reversible and costs nothing to try, which makes it the natural first step for anyone unsure whether a different approach would suit them.
