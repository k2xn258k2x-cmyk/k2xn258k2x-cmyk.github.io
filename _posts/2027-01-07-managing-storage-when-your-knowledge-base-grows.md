Any collection that only grows will eventually collide with something physical. The material a professional accumulates does not shrink on its own: documents arrive, photographs are taken, records are written, and nothing about ordinary work produces a countervailing force. Meanwhile the machine holding it has a fixed capacity that somebody chose years ago, for reasons that had nothing to do with what the collection would become.

Most people meet this at an inconvenient moment. A machine reports it is nearly full, or a backup takes an unreasonable time, or a copy to an external drive fails partway through, and somebody discovers that a thing they have been building steadily for years has a size they have never once looked at. The reaction is usually to delete something, chosen hastily, which is the least considered response available and frequently removes the wrong material.

![Managing Storage When Your Knowledge Base Grows](/assets/img/feature-screenshot-placeholder.svg)

What makes the situation avoidable rather than inevitable is that storage in an archive of this kind is almost never evenly distributed. The intuition that a large collection is large because it contains many things is usually wrong. A collection is large because a small number of items are enormous, and the enormous ones are almost always a specific and identifiable category rather than a random assortment.
<!--more-->


The reason for that concentration is worth understanding, because it determines the whole approach. Text occupies essentially nothing. Somebody who has written several thousand entries across a decade has produced a quantity of writing that is negligible against a single modern photograph, and the entire revision history of that writing is smaller again. What occupies space is anything derived from a physical or optical process: pages that were scanned, images that were captured, and audio that was recorded.

Those categories have a further property that makes them tractable. They were produced at a quality somebody else chose, for purposes that were not the eventual owner's. An archive scans at a resolution appropriate for preservation. A camera captures at whatever its default is. A publisher produces documents sized for print rather than for reading. In every case the file is considerably larger than the use requires, which means reduction is available without any loss of what the owner actually needs.

That distinction between the file and its usefulness is the whole of the subject. A scanned document exists to be read and searched; the additional resolution beyond legibility serves nobody. A photograph documenting a condition needs to show the condition; the remaining data is a fact about the camera. Reducing those files removes something real and removes nothing anybody was using, which is a considerably better position than choosing what to delete.

The complementary decision concerns what should be present rather than how large it is. Not everything an archive contains needs to be immediately available on a working machine, particularly for anybody with a genuinely constrained device. Material from concluded work can live elsewhere, retrievable when needed, without occupying capacity every day. That is archiving rather than deletion, and it preserves everything while changing what is carried.

Underlying all of this is visibility, which is what people lack. Somebody who cannot see how their storage is distributed will guess, and the guess is reliably wrong: they will assume the many small things are the problem and will spend an evening deleting text that occupies less space than one photograph. Measurement first, then a reduction that loses nothing, then a decision about what needs to be present. In that order, the whole exercise takes an evening and does not require deleting anything at all.

What follows is a conversation between two people whose constraints could hardly be more different, one with a large collection on a machine with room to spare and one with a modest collection on a machine with almost none.


## Busan, and a laptop that is nearly full

Nkechi had suggested it from a hotel during a port stop, which she said was the only reliable way to arrange anything.

‘Four days alongside and I have a connection that works,’ she said. ‘Which is the entire reason this call is happening today rather than in March.’

‘Say your problem,’ Marcus said. ‘Because I have one and I suspect they are opposite.’

‘My laptop is nearly full and it is not a large laptop.’ She was matter of fact. ‘It is the machine I take to sea, which means it is the machine I chose for durability rather than for capacity, and replacing it during a contract is not possible.’

‘How full?’

‘About eleven percent free, which is the number that made me start thinking about this.’ Nkechi shrugged. ‘And my vault is not the whole of it, obviously. But it is a growing part of it and it is the part I control.’

‘Mine is the opposite in every respect,’ Marcus said. ‘I have a desktop machine with a great deal of room and about two thousand entries and eleven thousand files, and until recently I had genuinely never thought about the size of any of it.’

‘Then why are you interested in this?’

‘Because my backup started taking forty minutes.’ He was blunt. ‘Which is the first time the size of the thing produced any consequence at all, and it made me realise I had no idea what was in there.’

‘So your constraint is time and mine is space.’

‘My constraint is time and yours is space and I suspect the diagnosis is identical,’ Marcus said, ‘which is what I want to find out.’


## Looking at it for the first time

‘Run the analysis,’ Marcus said. ‘Because I did this last week and the result was not what I expected at all.’

‘Say what you expected first.’

‘I expected it to be spread out.’ He was frank. ‘Eleven thousand files, thirty-one properties, six years. I assumed the size was the accumulation of a great many things, which is what large collections feel like from inside.’

‘And?’

‘And about eighty percent of it is in one directory, and within that directory the great majority is two kinds of file.’ Marcus shared. ‘Scanned inspection reports and property photographs. That is my archive by weight, and everything else I own is a rounding error against it.’

‘What proportion is your actual writing?’

‘Six years of entries, several hundred thousand words, and it is under one percent.’ He said it slowly, because he had clearly said it to himself several times. ‘Which I found genuinely startling. The thing I have spent six years producing is invisible in the measurement.’

‘Text is nothing.’

‘Text is nothing and I did not know that in any practical sense.’ He shrugged. ‘I knew it abstractly. I had never seen it as a proportion.’

Nkechi ran hers.

‘Mine is smaller and considerably more concentrated,’ she said. ‘Sixty percent of my attachments are documents, which are maritime regulations and certification material, and twenty-five percent is audio.’

‘Audio?’

‘Recorded study material. Lectures for the master mariner examinations, which I listen to on watch when the bridge is quiet.’ She was matter of fact. ‘Which I had not thought of as part of my vault at all, because I think of them as something I listen to rather than something I keep.’

‘And they are the second largest thing you own.’

‘They are about a quarter of everything, and there are perhaps forty of them, which is a considerable amount of space for forty items.’ Nkechi paused. ‘That is the finding. Not that I have too much. That forty files out of several hundred are a quarter of the weight.’

‘Which is mine at a different scale.’

‘Which is yours at a different scale and I would not have found it by thinking about it,’ she said. ‘I would have gone looking through my entries, which are the thing I interact with, and which turn out to be irrelevant.’

Marcus made the point he said he had arrived at afterward.

‘The thing you look at all day is not the thing occupying the space,’ he said. ‘And that is the reason people delete the wrong material.’

‘Say more.’

‘Somebody whose machine is full goes looking for things to remove, and what they can see is their entries, because that is the interface.’ He counted. ‘So they spend an evening deleting notes, which recovers essentially nothing, and they feel they have done something, and the machine is still full.’

‘Whereas the answer is four files.’

‘The answer is usually a small number of very large files and they are almost never the ones you are looking at.’


## The documents that are mostly picture

‘Then let us do the largest category,’ Nkechi said. ‘Because mine is documents and I suspect yours is too.’

‘Scanned inspection reports,’ Marcus said. ‘And they are the single worst thing I own, by a considerable margin.’

‘Say the numbers.’

‘Between twenty and fifty for each one, in the units the analysis reports.’ He pulled one up. ‘Which is an enormous amount for a thirty-page document about a boiler.’

‘Why so large?’

‘Because it is not a document, it is thirty photographs of pages.’ He was precise. ‘An inspector produces a report on paper, somebody scans it at whatever resolution their equipment defaults to, and I receive a file that is essentially an album.’

‘And you have how many?’

‘Several hundred, across thirty-one properties and six years, and they are the reason my backup takes forty minutes.’

He demonstrated the reduction on one, and Nkechi asked the obvious question.

‘Is it still readable?’

‘That is the only question that matters and the answer is yes, and I check every time.’ Marcus was emphatic. ‘Which takes about four seconds and which I would not skip, because the entire purpose of a scanned report is that somebody can read it.’

‘What proportion have you done?’

‘About four hundred, in two evenings, in batches.’ He shrugged. ‘And the total came down enough that the backup is now under fifteen minutes, which was the whole objective.’

‘Did you lose anything?’

‘Two of them I kept at the original size, because they were poor scans to begin with and the reduction made a reference number ambiguous.’ He was matter of fact. ‘Which is the correct outcome. The operation did what I asked. The source was already bad.’

Nkechi asked about the searchable content.

‘Does the text survive?’

‘The text was extracted when the document arrived and it is held separately, so reducing the file does not touch it.’ Marcus was definite. ‘Which I confirmed rather than assumed, by searching a phrase from inside one after compressing it.’

‘That is the check I would not have thought to do.’

‘It is the check that would have been extremely annoying to discover I needed six months later.’

Her own case was different.

‘Mine are published documents rather than scans,’ Nkechi said. ‘Regulations, downloaded from official sources, and they are large for a different reason.’

‘Which is?’

‘Diagrams and photographs embedded in them, produced for print.’ She shrugged. ‘Which is entirely reasonable of the publisher and completely unnecessary for a person reading them on a small screen at sea.’

‘Do they reduce?’

‘Substantially, and the text is unaffected because it was text to begin with.’ She was pleased about it. ‘Which is the easier case, because there is nothing to make illegible. The words are words.’

‘How much did you recover?’

‘Enough to matter on a machine with eleven percent free, which is the only measure I care about.’ Nkechi paused. ‘And I want to say something about that, because it changed how I think about downloads.’

‘Go on.’

‘I now reduce anything large at the moment I bring it in, rather than as a periodic exercise.’ She counted. ‘Which takes ten seconds per document and means the problem does not accumulate. Marcus had to do four hundred in two evenings. I would rather never have four hundred.’

‘That is better than what I do.’

‘It is available to me because my inflow is small,’ she said. ‘Yours is hundreds of reports a year and you cannot do it individually. So you batch and I do it at intake, and both are correct for the volume.’


## The photographs nobody will ever look at

‘I want to raise the category I have not touched,’ Marcus said, ‘because it is the second largest thing I own and I have done nothing about it.’

‘Photographs.’

‘Thirty percent of my files and a considerable share of the weight, and unlike the scanned reports I have not compressed a single one.’

‘Say why.’

‘Because I am not confident about it and I would rather be honest than pretend I have a system.’ He was frank. ‘A scanned report is a picture of words and I know what legible means. A photograph of a crack in a wall is evidence, and I do not know how much detail I might need.’

‘What is the case you are worried about?’

‘Somebody disputing what a condition looked like four years ago.’ Marcus was precise. ‘And me producing a photograph that has been reduced, and somebody asking whether the reduction removed something.’

‘Which is a fair question.’

‘Which is a fair question that I would rather not have to answer, and the cost of never answering it is some storage.’ He shrugged. ‘So I keep them as they came off the camera.’

Nkechi thought about it.

‘Is that all of them?’

‘That is the thing I have been circling and you have just put your finger on it.’ Marcus was slightly rueful. ‘No. Perhaps one in eight is evidence. The rest are photographs of a boiler that has since been replaced, taken during a routine inspection, on a property I no longer own.’

‘So the reason applies to a minority.’

‘The reason applies to a minority and I have been applying the policy to all of them, because separating them requires a judgment about each one and there are several thousand.’

‘That is the same problem as archiving.’

‘It is exactly the archiving problem and I have solved it there and not here, which is inconsistent and I am aware of it.’ He laughed. ‘My honest position is that I have not found a mechanical rule and until I do I am keeping everything.’

‘Is there one available?’

‘Possibly. Anything attached to a property I no longer own, or anything more than five years old on a matter that closed.’ Marcus thought aloud. ‘Which would be a mechanical rule and I would have to accept that it occasionally catches something evidential.’

‘And you will not.’

‘And I have not, so far, and I want that recorded as an unresolved thing rather than presented as a considered decision.’ He shrugged. ‘Somebody reading this should know that one of us has a category he has not sorted out.’


## What a collection is made of

‘There is a smaller view I want to mention,’ Marcus said, ‘because it is what actually started this for me.’

‘The composition.’

‘The type distribution in the analytics, which shows what proportion of my attachments are each kind of thing.’ He shared it. ‘Mine is roughly forty-five percent documents, thirty percent images, fifteen percent workbooks, and the rest is a mixture.’

‘Which is different from the size question.’

‘Which is a completely different question and I confused the two for about a week.’ Marcus was frank. ‘That distribution is counts. It tells me what kinds of thing I have. The size analysis tells me what is heavy. And a category can be numerous and light or few and enormous.’

‘Say the case where they diverge.’

‘Workbooks. Fifteen percent of my files and almost none of my storage, because a spreadsheet is text with structure.’ He counted. ‘Whereas images are thirty percent of my files and a considerably larger share of the weight.’

‘So you need both.’

‘I need both and they answer different questions.’ He was definite. ‘The counts tell me what my practice actually consists of. The sizes tell me where the problem is. And knowing that thirty percent of my files are photographs told me something about my working habits before it told me anything about storage.’

‘Say what it told you.’

‘That I document visually far more than I would have said.’ Marcus shrugged. ‘I think of myself as somebody who writes things down. A third of what I produce is photographs of conditions, which is a different professional self-image and an accurate one.’

Nkechi's produced a similar surprise.

‘Mine says sixty percent documents and twenty-five percent audio,’ she said. ‘And the audio proportion is the one I would have got wrong.’

‘Because you do not think of it as material.’

‘Because I think of it as something I consume rather than something I hold, and a quarter of my collection is something I have never once considered part of my collection.’ She paused. ‘Which is a strange thing to discover about your own archive.’

‘Has it changed anything?’

‘It has changed what I do with them after the examination.’ Nkechi was matter of fact. ‘Which we will come to, because it is the archiving question rather than the compression one.’


## The directory nobody thinks about

‘There is a third directory and neither of us has mentioned it,’ Marcus said.

‘The version snapshots.’

‘Which I looked at expecting to find a problem, because a history of everything sounds like it would accumulate.’ He shrugged. ‘And it is small enough that I stopped worrying within about ninety seconds.’

‘Say why.’

‘Two reasons. It is text, which we have established is nothing. And old snapshots retire automatically after a period rather than accumulating indefinitely.’

‘Which is a design decision rather than something you configured.’

‘Which is a decision somebody made on my behalf and which I would have made the same way.’ Marcus was definite. ‘Because the useful window for a previous version is short. I want yesterday's version of something I have just broken. I do not want the version from three years ago.’

‘Is that true for everybody?’

‘It is true for me because I rarely revise entries after writing them.’ He was careful. ‘A property record accumulates rather than being rewritten. Somebody producing drafts would generate considerably more history than I do.’

‘I generate almost none,’ Nkechi said. ‘My entries are written once, from processed observations, and are essentially never edited. So my snapshot directory is negligible.’

‘Which means neither of us is a good test of it.’

‘Neither of us is a good test and I would say that plainly rather than reassuring anybody.’ She was matter of fact. ‘Somebody writing a book, or a thesis, or anything revised repeatedly would generate substantially more, and the automatic retirement is what stops that becoming a problem.’

Marcus made the point he said mattered.

‘The reason I raise it at all is that people assume it is the problem,’ he said. ‘History sounds expensive. It is text, it retires, and it is not where anybody's storage has gone.’

‘Whereas the thing nobody suspects.’

‘Whereas the thing nobody suspects is forty photographs from an inspection four years ago that nobody will ever look at and which occupy more than my entire six years of writing.’


## Keeping the index honest

‘One thing I learned the hard way,’ Marcus said, ‘and I want to describe it because it took me some time to diagnose.’

‘Say it.’

‘After I compressed four hundred documents, the reduction produces new files replacing the originals.’ He was precise. ‘Which is what you want and which means the folder has changed underneath the record of what is in it.’

‘And?’

‘And I ran the rebuild afterward, out of caution rather than knowledge, and I am glad I did.’ He shrugged. ‘Because the alternative is a manifest describing a state that no longer exists, and I would not have found out until something failed to appear in a search.’

‘Which is the worst kind of failure.’

‘Which is silent, and would have looked like a search problem rather than a maintenance problem, and I would have spent a considerable time looking in the wrong place.’

Nkechi described her own version.

‘Mine was worse and entirely my fault,’ she said. ‘I moved files manually.’

‘Say what you did.’

‘I was trying to free space in a hurry, before a departure, and I went into the folder and moved a set of documents onto a drive.’ She was matter of fact. ‘Directly, in the file manager, without doing anything else.’

‘Which left the record pointing at things that were gone.’

‘Which left the record describing a folder that no longer matched, and the entries still listed documents that were not there.’ Nkechi shrugged. ‘And I discovered it at sea, three days later, when I opened an entry and the document did not open.’

‘What fixed it?’

‘Rebuilding, which reconciled the record with what is actually present, and which took an evening and no thought.’ She paused. ‘And the lesson is that I should not have done it that way. If I want material out of the folder, I should do it through the application rather than around it.’

‘I would say something slightly different,’ Marcus said.

‘Go on.’

‘Do it however you like and rebuild afterward.’ He was definite. ‘Because sometimes moving a hundred files in a file manager is genuinely the fastest way, and the arrangement tolerates it provided you reconcile afterward. What it does not tolerate is doing it and not reconciling.’

‘That is more practical than my rule.’

‘It is more practical because your rule requires discipline in a hurry and mine requires an evening afterward, and I have more confidence in the evening.’


## The size of the copy

‘This all connects to backup and that is where it started for me,’ Marcus said.

‘Say the connection.’

‘Every one of these decisions changes how long a copy takes and how much space it needs somewhere else.’ He counted. ‘A folder of a certain size means a copy of that size, on a drive, weekly, plus another somewhere else monthly.’

‘So the working size multiplies.’

‘The working size multiplies by however many copies you keep, which for me is three including the one on the machine.’ Marcus shrugged. ‘Which is the actual argument for compression, and it is not that my machine is full. My machine is fine. It is that every unnecessary unit is being stored three times and copied weekly.’

‘Which is a considerably better framing than tidiness.’

‘It is the only framing that made me act, and I have thought about why.’ He was reflective. ‘A full machine is a problem you solve when it arrives. A backup taking forty minutes is a problem you experience every single week, and weekly friction is what actually produces behaviour.’

Nkechi described the constraint that shapes hers.

‘Mine is different and it is the reason I started archiving rather than only compressing,’ she said.

‘Say it.’

‘I go to sea for periods of several months with a machine I cannot replace and cannot add to.’ She was matter of fact. ‘And what I need on that machine is the current voyage, my certification material, and enough history to answer questions about the recent past.’

‘Not everything.’

‘Not everything, and everything is what I had for the first two years, because it never occurred to me to arrange it otherwise.’

‘What do you do now?’

‘At the end of a contract, when I am ashore, I copy the completed voyage material to a drive and take it out of the working folder.’ Nkechi described it. ‘Which is not deletion. It is two copies on two drives and nothing on the laptop.’

‘And you can get it back.’

‘I can get it back by copying it in, which requires being somewhere with the drive, which is a real limitation and which I have accepted.’ She shrugged. ‘In four contracts I have needed archived material twice, both times ashore, both times it took ten minutes.’

Marcus asked the question he said he had been building to.

‘How do you decide what leaves?’

‘A completed voyage on a vessel I am no longer serving on.’ She was precise. ‘That is the whole rule and it is deliberately mechanical, because a judgment about what might be needed is a judgment I will make badly.’

‘Which means some of it will be needed.’

‘Some of it will be needed and the cost is ten minutes ashore, and the alternative is carrying four years of voyages on a machine with eleven percent free.’ Nkechi was definite. ‘That is a trade I would make again.’

‘Mine is simpler because I have no constraint,’ Marcus said.

‘You archive nothing.’

‘I archive nothing and I keep everything, because a sold property is still a property somebody may ask me about and my machine has room.’ He shrugged. ‘Which is only defensible because I compressed. If I had not, six years of uncompressed scans would have forced me into your position eventually.’

‘So compression bought you the ability not to archive.’

‘Compression bought me the ability not to make a decision, which is what I actually wanted,’ he said. ‘Every deletion or archive is a judgment about the future and I would rather make as few of those as possible.’


## The thing Nkechi does that Marcus cannot

‘There is one part of my arrangement that is not available to you,’ Nkechi said, ‘and I want to describe it because somebody in a similar position would benefit.’

‘Go on.’

‘I have two folders rather than one.’ She described it. ‘A working folder for the current contract, and a reference folder that contains my certification material and anything I consult regardless of which vessel I am on.’

‘Why separate them?’

‘Because they have completely different lifecycles.’ Nkechi was precise. ‘The working folder empties at the end of a contract. The reference folder never empties and grows very slowly and is the same on every voyage.’

‘And they back up differently.’

‘They back up at completely different intervals, which is the practical benefit.’ She counted. ‘The working folder is copied weekly because it changes constantly. The reference folder is copied perhaps twice a year because it barely moves, and the copy of it that sits on a drive at home is essentially always current.’

‘That is a genuine saving.’

‘It is a saving in time rather than in space and it is the reason I did it.’ She shrugged. ‘A weekly copy of everything, on a ship, over a connection that does not exist, means copying to a physical drive, and the smaller that copy is the more likely I am to actually do it.’

Marcus considered whether it applied to him.

‘I could split by portfolio,’ he said. ‘Residential and commercial.’

‘Would you search across them?’

‘Constantly, and that is the answer.’ He shook his head. ‘Somebody told me not to split for tidiness because a query only reaches the folder you are in, and my questions are comparative across everything.’

‘Whereas mine are not.’

‘Whereas your reference material is genuinely a different kind of thing from a voyage record, and you would never search both at once.’ Marcus was definite. ‘Which is the actual test and it is not about size at all.’

‘Say the test.’

‘Would you ever want a single query to reach both.’ He counted. ‘If yes, one folder regardless of what it costs you. If no, two folders and take the benefits.’

‘That is better than my reasoning, which was simply that they felt different.’

‘Your reasoning arrived at the correct answer, which is what matters, and mine is a way of explaining it to somebody else.’


## What each of them does now

‘Say your practice, in order,’ Nkechi said. ‘Because somebody reading this wants a sequence rather than an argument.’

‘Four things and I do them at different intervals.’ Marcus counted. ‘I look at the size distribution twice a year, which takes five minutes and which I put in the calendar because I would otherwise never do it.’

‘Twice a year is enough?’

‘Twice a year is enough because the composition of a collection changes slowly.’ He was definite. ‘I am not monitoring anything. I am checking whether the shape has changed, and it has not in two years except in the way I expected.’

‘And then?’

‘Then compression, in batches, on whatever category the distribution says is heavy.’ He shrugged. ‘Which for me is always the same category and always will be, because scanned reports are what my business produces.’

‘Third?’

‘Rebuild after any batch operation, without exception, because I have already described why.’

‘And fourth?’

‘Verify. Search for something inside a compressed document and confirm it still comes back.’ Marcus was emphatic. ‘Which takes ten seconds and which is the only way I know the maintenance did not break anything.’

Nkechi gave hers.

‘Mine has five and the first two are yours.’ She counted. ‘Reduce at intake rather than in batches, because my inflow is small enough. Look at the distribution before each contract rather than twice a year, because a contract is my natural period.’

‘Which is the event-based version.’

‘Which is the event-based version of the same habit and it is the only kind that survives my schedule.’ She continued. ‘Third, archive completed voyages when I am ashore. Fourth, rebuild after anything that touches the folder directly. Fifth, and this is mine rather than yours, check the free space on the machine itself.’

‘Which you do how often?’

‘Before every departure, alongside checking that I have the charts and the certificates.’ Nkechi shrugged. ‘Because arriving at sea with a nearly full machine is a problem I cannot solve for three months, and it belongs in a departure checklist rather than in a maintenance routine.’

‘That is a considerably better place for it than mine.’

‘It is in a different place because my failure is different.’ She was matter of fact. ‘Your worst outcome is a slow backup. Mine is a machine that stops working correctly in the middle of the South China Sea, and things with that shape belong on the list you check before you leave.’

Marcus was quiet for a second.

‘I want to say the thing I have taken from this,’ he said.

‘Go on.’

‘That I spent six years never looking, and then twenty minutes looking, and the twenty minutes told me something I could not have guessed and would have got wrong.’ He shrugged. ‘Which is the whole content of what I would tell anybody. Not compress, not archive. Look first, because you do not know what you have.’


## Maintenance, and why it is worth an evening

Storage management is the least interesting subject a professional archive presents and one of the few with a hard edge, because a machine reporting itself full does not negotiate. What makes it tractable rather than alarming is a property of these collections that almost nobody knows without measuring: the weight is concentrated, and it is concentrated in a category that can be identified in a few minutes.

Both practitioners here describe the same discovery from opposite scales. Text is nothing. Six years of daily writing, several hundred thousand words, was under one percent of one practitioner's archive, which he described as startling because the thing he had spent six years producing was invisible in the measurement. What occupies space is whatever came from a physical or optical process: scanned pages, captured photographs, and recorded audio, in every case produced at a quality somebody else chose for purposes that were not the owner's.

The practical consequence is that people delete the wrong material. Somebody whose machine is full goes looking through the interface they use, which shows them entries, and spends an evening removing writing that occupies less space than a single photograph. The thing being looked at all day is not the thing occupying the space, and the remedy is measurement before action rather than any particular discipline.

Reduction of oversized files is what makes the problem disappear without any decision about what to lose. Documents that are photographs of pages reduce enormously; documents that were produced electronically reduce only where they carry embedded images. In both cases the extracted searchable text is held separately and is unaffected, which one practitioner confirmed rather than assumed by searching inside a compressed document afterward. The one discipline both insist on is checking that a reduced scan is still legible, since a saving that renders a reference number ambiguous has destroyed a document rather than shrunk it.

Two rhythms emerged and both are correct for their volumes. High inflow means batches: several hundred documents in two evenings, once, on the category the measurement identified. Low inflow means reduction at intake, ten seconds per item, so that four hundred never accumulate. Neither practitioner recommends the other's approach for their own situation and both recognise why the difference exists.

The composition view answers a different question from the size view, and conflating them costs a week. Counts describe what a practice consists of; sizes describe where the weight is. A category can be numerous and light or few and enormous, and both practitioners found something about their own working habits in the counts before they found anything about storage: one documents visually far more than he would have claimed, and the other holds a quarter of her collection in a category she had never considered part of it.

Two directories turn out not to be the problem despite sounding as if they should be. Version history is text, retires automatically after a window, and is negligible for anybody who does not revise heavily. Neither practitioner is a good test of it and both said so rather than reassuring anybody, since somebody producing repeated drafts would generate substantially more.

Rebuilding the index after any bulk operation is the step both learned the hard way and would now describe as non-negotiable. Compression replaces files, manual moves change a folder underneath the record of it, and in both cases the failure is silent and looks like a search problem months later. The practical rule that emerged is permissive rather than restrictive: do whatever is fastest, including moving things directly, and reconcile afterward.

Archiving is the separate decision and it belongs to anybody with a genuinely constrained machine. Removing completed work to external storage is not deletion, keeps two copies elsewhere, and costs ten minutes on the rare occasions the material is wanted. The rule should be mechanical rather than a judgment about future need, since that judgment is made badly. And for anybody whose failure mode is not inconvenience but being unable to fix anything for three months, free capacity belongs on a departure checklist rather than in a maintenance routine.

What connects all of it to backup is the multiplier that nobody counts. A working folder is copied several times and refreshed weekly, so every unnecessary unit is stored three times and moved regularly. That framing is what produced action in one case, and the reasoning is worth extracting: a full machine is a problem solved when it arrives, and a backup taking forty minutes is friction experienced every week, and weekly friction is what actually changes behaviour.

The advice both would give reduces to one instruction, and it is the least dramatic thing in the conversation. Look first. Twenty minutes of measurement tells somebody something they could not have guessed and would have got wrong, and everything sensible follows from it. Six years of never looking cost nothing until it did, and the looking cost an evening.


## FAQs

### Why does a knowledge base eventually strain storage?

Because it only grows. Documents arrive, photographs accumulate, records are written, and nothing in ordinary professional work produces a countervailing force. Meanwhile the machine holding it has a capacity somebody chose years earlier for reasons unrelated to what the collection would become. Most people meet this at an inconvenient moment: a full disk, a failed copy, or a backup that has quietly become slow enough to notice. Anybody who has never looked at the size of their own archive should expect the number to be larger than their estimate, which is consistent enough across practitioners to be worth predicting. Practitioners at very different scales describe the same conclusion independently, which suggests it follows from how these collections accumulate rather than from any particular kind of work.

### Where does the space actually go?

Almost never where people assume. Collections are large because a small number of items are enormous, not because there are many items. Text occupies essentially nothing: practitioners with six years of daily writing find it accounts for under one percent of their archive. What consumes space is anything produced by a physical or optical process, meaning scanned pages, captured photographs, and recorded audio, in every case at a quality chosen by somebody else. Establishing this for one's own collection takes a few minutes and reliably redirects any subsequent effort toward the material that actually matters.
