Analytics in a knowledge tool are usually understood as inventory. How many items exist, how much space they occupy, how many files are attached. Those are operational questions with operational answers, and they are the reason most people open such a panel once, register that the numbers are unremarkable, and never return. Nothing about a storage figure changes what anybody does on a Tuesday.

There is a different question the same data answers, and it is the one nobody thinks to ask: what kind of worker am I, actually, rather than in my own account of myself. That question is genuinely difficult to answer by introspection, because memory is an unreliable narrator about effort. It records failures with precision and ordinary days not at all, which produces a systematically unfair picture in which somebody remembers the fortnight they lost and not the eleven months they did not.

![What Your Analytics Are Actually Telling You](/assets/img/feature-screenshot-placeholder.svg)

A record of activity generated as a side effect of working is immune to that distortion. It was not curated, it was not assembled to make a case, and nobody was performing for it. Which is precisely why it is credible in a way that no self-assessment is: the person reading it cannot dispute it, because they produced every point on it themselves without any awareness that it was being recorded.
<!--more-->


This is the property that distinguishes it from the entire category of tools built to encourage behaviour. Streaks, badges, completion rings, and habit counters all work by manufacturing a signal that stands in for progress, and they suffer a common failure: everybody eventually notices the signal is artificial, at which point it stops motivating and starts feeling faintly manipulative. A number somebody generated by doing their own work carries no such implication. Nobody feels congratulated by a count of what they actually produced, and that is exactly why the count remains believable indefinitely.

The distributional views do something else and arguably more useful. A breakdown of where content sits, how categories are applied, or what kinds of files have accumulated is not a measure of effort at all. It is a description of attention, and attention is something almost nobody can accurately report on. People believe they are spending their energy where they intend to spend it, and a distribution frequently disagrees, which produces the specific and uncomfortable experience of learning something about yourself from a chart you did not commission.

What makes those observations actionable rather than merely interesting is that they identify things that are absent. A category created with intent and applied three times is a plan that quietly stopped. A branch of a structure that has barely grown is an area somebody meant to develop and did not. Absence is invisible during ordinary work, because nothing prompts anybody to notice what is not accumulating, and a distribution makes it visible in a second.

None of which is about optimisation. Nothing here suggests anybody should write more, produce faster, or improve a metric. The value is entirely in awareness: knowing what your working rhythm actually looks like, where your attention has genuinely gone, and which of your intentions have quietly lapsed. What somebody does with that is their own business, and most of them do something small.

What follows is a conversation between two people at opposite ends of familiarity with the subject, one of whom has been using it deliberately for years and one of whom opened it once by accident and closed it again.


## A bakery after closing and a flat in London

Amir answered on the third attempt, with the sound of a dishwasher behind him and Yasmin visible over his shoulder for a moment before she moved out of frame.

‘I am upright and I have flour on me and this is the best I can offer,’ he said.

‘That is more than I expected on a Tuesday evening,’ said Priya. ‘Do you want to postpone?’

‘Absolutely not. I have been curious about this for a month and if I postpone I will postpone again.’ He wiped something off the table. ‘I should say at the outset that I have opened this panel exactly twice. The first time by accident and the second time to show somebody a number.’

‘What number?’

‘My entry count, which I was showing off about.’ He was cheerfully unembarrassed. ‘And I did not understand anything else on the screen and I closed it, and I have been meaning to ask somebody ever since.’

‘Then you are the ideal person to do this with.’

‘Why?’

‘Because I have been using it for three years and I no longer see it,’ Priya said. ‘Everything I would tell you is a thing I worked out slowly and have stopped noticing. Explaining it to somebody who is genuinely looking at it for the first time is how I find out which parts are useful and which are habit.’

Yasmin's voice came from somewhere off camera.

‘He is going to ask you what the point of it is within four minutes.’

‘I am going to ask her what the point of it is right now,’ Amir said, ‘because that is a fair question and I would rather ask it at the beginning than pretend for half an hour.’

Priya laughed.

‘The point is that you are going to learn something about yourself that you did not know and could not have found out any other way,’ she said. ‘I am aware that sounds grand for a panel of numbers.’

‘It sounds enormously grand.’

‘Give me twenty minutes.’


## The numbers, and one of them is a shock

‘Open the panel,’ Priya said. ‘It is in the sidebar, and it expands.’

Amir did, and there was a pause.

‘Say what you see,’ she said.

‘Nine hundred and eleven entries.’

Another pause.

‘Is that a lot?’ he asked.

‘It is more than most people I know have.’ Priya was matter of fact. ‘It is roughly what I have of one particular kind of item and I have been doing this for four years as a full-time occupation.’

‘I run a bakery.’

‘You run a bakery and you have nine hundred and eleven entries, and I want you to sit with that for a second rather than moving on.’

He did, visibly.

‘I thought it was a few hundred,’ he said. ‘I genuinely did. If you had asked me before this call I would have said three hundred and something, and I would have said it confidently.’

‘Everybody says a number about a third of the real one.’

‘Why?’

‘Because you remember the days you did not do it.’ Priya said it plainly. ‘Memory is extremely good at recording the fortnight you were too busy and completely silent about the eleven months where you added two things a day without thinking about it. So your estimate is built from the exceptions.’

Yasmin's voice again, closer.

‘I told him he would not stick with it.’

‘She did tell me that.’

‘I told him that eighteen months ago and I have been reminded of it approximately weekly since.’

‘And I am about to be reminded of it again,’ Amir said, with obvious satisfaction, ‘because there is now a number.’

Priya walked him through the rest of the top section.

‘Entries with files attached to them,’ she said. ‘That is a subset of your total.’

‘Two hundred and eleven.’

‘Which tells you what proportion of your material has evidence attached, and yours is high.’ She was interested. ‘Roughly a quarter of your entries carry something. Mine is lower and it should probably not be.’

‘What is yours?’

‘About a fifth, which sounds similar and is not, because my work is entirely built on documents.’ She frowned slightly. ‘I have four hundred papers attached to entries and about seven hundred entries that are my own thinking with nothing attached, which is correct, and then a category of experiment logs where the data files should be attached and frequently are not.’

‘So the number told you something.’

‘The number told me something I have half known for a year and have never had to look at.’

The file count and the storage figure came next, which Amir was less interested in and Priya explained anyway.

‘Total files, and total space occupied.’

‘Three hundred and eighty files.’ Amir read it out. ‘And the space is a number I have no feeling about at all.’

‘You should not have a feeling about it and I would not spend any time on it,’ Priya said. ‘Storage is the one genuinely operational figure here and it matters only if you are near a limit, which on a modern machine you are not.’

‘Then why is it there?’

‘Because somebody, somewhere, is running this on a laptop that is nearly full and needs to know.’ She shrugged. ‘It is not for you. Most panels contain something that is not for you, and the skill is working out which parts are.’

She showed him the strength indicators last, which sat inline as small pills with a fuller view available beneath them.

‘These summarise how well built out your material is,’ she said. ‘And they expand if you want the detail behind them.’

‘Mine says something moderately encouraging and I do not entirely understand it.’

‘Then leave it,’ Priya said. ‘I looked at these obsessively for a month and now glance at them monthly. They are a summary, and a summary is only useful once you understand what it is summarising, which is what the charts are for.’


## Two shapes, and neither is what its owner expected

‘Now the part I actually wanted to show you,’ Priya said. ‘Scroll down.’

The charts appeared: a line of daily activity across the last fortnight, a longer view by month, and two circular breakdowns beneath them.

‘Read yours to me,’ she said.

‘It is a flat line.’ Amir sounded almost disappointed. ‘Not entirely flat. It goes up and down by one or two. But it is the same every day.’

‘Say that again and listen to yourself.’

‘It is the same every day.’ He stopped. ‘Ah.’

‘Yes.’

‘I add things at roughly the same time and it is roughly the same amount, and I have never once thought about it as a habit,’ he said slowly. ‘It is after closing. Between half past six and seven, most days, because that is when the last tray is out and before I do the ordering.’

‘Which is a routine you constructed without deciding to.’

‘Which is a routine I did not know I had.’ He was looking at it. ‘There is one gap. Four days, about ten days ago.’

‘What happened?’

‘The oven.’ He laughed. ‘Four days of an engineer and a great deal of swearing and no time for anything. And I would have told you that period lasted a fortnight.’

‘It lasted four days and the chart is not being kind to you, it is simply correct,’ Priya said. ‘That is the thing about it. It has no opinion.’

Hers was entirely different and she said so.

‘Mine is spikes,’ she said, sharing. ‘Look at it. Three or four large days, then almost nothing, then another cluster.’

‘Why?’

‘Because my week is not uniform. I have two days that are laboratory days, which generate a great deal of material because I am recording observations as they happen. I have a teaching day, which generates almost nothing because I am on my feet for six hours. And I have reading days, which produce one or two long entries rather than eleven short ones.’

‘So the shape is your timetable.’

‘The shape is my timetable, which I knew, and the useful part is the exception.’ She pointed at something. ‘That is a Thursday with fourteen entries on it, which should be a teaching day, and I have no idea what happened.’

‘Can you find out?’

‘I can look at what I wrote, which I am going to do after this call, and it will be something,’ Priya said. ‘Which is a small example of the general use. The chart does not tell you anything. It tells you where to look.’

The monthly view was where both of them found something larger.

‘Longer patterns,’ Priya said. ‘Mine is embarrassing and completely consistent.’

‘Go on.’

‘Every submission I have, and there are perhaps four a year, produces a surge in the three weeks before and a trough of about ten days afterward.’ She was matter of fact. ‘Every single time. Four years of it. The shape is so regular you could set a clock by it.’

‘Is that bad?’

‘The surge is not bad. That is a deadline doing what deadlines do.’ She paused. ‘The trough is the part I had never seen, and it is ten days of almost nothing every single time, and I had always experienced those as a bad fortnight rather than as a pattern.’

‘And now?’

‘And now I know it is coming, which changes it from a failure into a phase.’ She was definite. ‘I have started deliberately putting low-demand work into that window rather than planning something ambitious and then feeling terrible about it. Which is a scheduling change produced entirely by a chart.’

Amir's monthly view was seasonal.

‘Mine goes up in the autumn and stays up until January,’ he said. ‘Which is obvious and which I have never seen drawn.’

‘Holiday baking.’

‘Holiday baking, which is about forty percent of my year's business compressed into eleven weeks.’ He was studying it. ‘And there is a second bump in the spring which I did not expect.’

‘What is in it?’

‘Weddings and communions.’ He said it slowly. ‘Which I have always thought of as a quiet time of year because there is no rush in the shop. But apparently I am writing a great deal during it, which means I am doing a great deal of thinking about it.’

‘That is exactly the kind of thing this is for,’ Priya said. ‘You had a belief about a season and the record disagrees.’

Yasmin, from off camera: ‘I have been telling him spring is busy for three years.’

‘She has been telling me spring is busy for three years,’ Amir agreed, ‘and I have been telling her the shop is quiet, and it turns out we were both right about different things.’


## The chart that finds the thing you stopped doing

‘Now the one I would put above all the others,’ Priya said, ‘and I want to show you mine first because mine is more uncomfortable.’

She shared the circular breakdown of how her labels were distributed.

‘Two labels account for about sixty percent of everything I have,’ she said. ‘Which is not surprising. Those are my two main techniques and my whole project is built on them.’

‘And the rest?’

‘The rest is a long tail, and three of them have fewer than five entries each.’ She paused. ‘And I want to tell you what those three are, because it took me about a minute to work out and then it was not a pleasant minute.’

‘Go on.’

‘They are three research directions I created labels for at the start of my second year, because I intended to explore them.’ Priya was very even about it. ‘Not vaguely. I wrote a document about them. I discussed two of them with my supervisor. And then I did not do any of it, and I would not have said I had abandoned them. I would have said they were upcoming.’

‘For how long?’

‘Two years,’ she said. ‘Two years of a thing being upcoming, and the only reason I know is that I made a label and the chart shows me it has four entries on it.’

Amir was quiet for a second.

‘That is a considerably more serious use than I was expecting from a pie chart.’

‘It is the only tool I have ever used that finds absences,’ Priya said. ‘Everything else in my working life shows me what I have done. A list of tasks. A draft. A record of experiments. All of it is a picture of activity. Nothing anywhere shows me what I intended and stopped.’

‘Because nothing accumulates.’

‘Because nothing accumulates, and an absence produces no evidence.’ She was definite. ‘Which is why nobody notices a plan quietly ending. It does not end. It just fails to grow, and failing to grow is invisible unless something draws it.’

‘What did you do about the three?’

‘I abandoned one deliberately, which I should have done two years ago and which was a relief.’ She counted. ‘One I have scheduled for after submission, with a date on it, which converts it from a guilt into an item. And one I started three weeks ago, and it has eleven entries now, and it is going to be a chapter.’

‘From a chart.’

‘From a chart that told me a number was four,’ Priya said. ‘Now do yours.’

Amir opened it, and read it out.

‘Recipes is nearly half. Suppliers is about a quarter. Then daily things, then business, then a few smaller ones.’

‘Is that what you expected?’

‘That is exactly what I expected and it is slightly boring,’ he said. ‘Which I suppose is a good sign.’

Yasmin's voice: ‘What about staff?’

There was a pause.

‘Three,’ Amir said.

‘Three what?’

‘Three entries. Out of nine hundred and eleven.’ He sounded genuinely taken aback. ‘I have five people working for me and two of them have been with me for more than a decade.’

Yasmin came into frame properly, which she had been avoiding.

‘Can I say the thing I have been trying to say for a year?’

‘You are going to say it whether I agree or not.’

‘You know everything about your suppliers and your recipes and nothing about your people is written down anywhere.’ She said it directly. ‘Who is trained on what. Who covers what. What you agreed with Marta about her hours. What happened with the thing in March that you and I both remember differently.’

Amir did not say anything immediately.

‘She is right,’ he said eventually, to Priya rather than to Yasmin.

‘It is the same shape as mine,’ Priya said. ‘You made a category because you knew it mattered and then you did not fill it, and there was no mechanism anywhere that would have told you.’

‘Why did I not fill it?’

‘Do you want my guess or Yasmin's?’

‘Yasmin's,’ said Amir, ‘because she has clearly been waiting.’

‘Because writing about people feels different from writing about flour,’ Yasmin said. ‘And because you would have to write down things you have decided not to think about.’

‘That is entirely correct and I am going to need a moment.’

‘Take the moment,’ Priya said. ‘And then write three entries this week, which is what I did, and see what the number is in a month.’


## Where the work has actually gone

‘There is a second one of those and it is about structure rather than categories,’ Priya said.

She shared the breakdown by page.

‘This shows how my entries distribute across the tree.’

‘And yours is uneven.’

‘Mine is dramatically uneven and it took me a while to decide whether that was a problem.’ She pointed. ‘One chapter has more entries under it than all the others combined. Not slightly more. More than the rest together.’

‘Is that where the work is?’

‘That is where the work has been, which is not the same as where I thought it was.’ She sat back. ‘If you had asked me to describe my thesis I would have described four chapters of roughly equal weight, because that is the shape of the document. The material says one of them is the thesis and the other three are supporting it.’

‘Is that bad?’

‘I decided it is not, after about a week of worrying about it,’ Priya said. ‘Because it turned out to be true rather than a failure of balance. That chapter is the contribution. The others are context, method, and implication, and they do not require the same volume of underlying material.’

‘So the chart did not change anything.’

‘The chart changed how I talk about my own work, which turns out to matter enormously in a viva and in every conversation with my supervisor.’ She was definite. ‘I had been describing a four-part project. I am now describing one central argument with three supporting sections, which is both more accurate and considerably more persuasive.’

Amir's was less dramatic and produced one surprise.

‘Recipes is enormous, which is correct,’ he said. ‘Suppliers is second. And then there is daily, which is bigger than I expected.’

‘How much bigger?’

‘I would have said it was the smallest thing I do.’ He was scrolling. ‘Four lines at the end of the day about what sold and what did not and anything anybody said. It takes ninety seconds and it feels like nothing.’

‘And it is a fifth of your vault.’

‘It is a fifth of my vault, which is about a hundred and eighty entries, which is nearly two years of days.’ He stopped. ‘Yasmin made me start that.’

‘I made you start that,’ Yasmin confirmed.

‘And I have complained about it approximately every month since,’ Amir said, ‘and it turns out to be the second largest thing I have written and I have never once gone back and read it as a series.’

‘Then that is your homework,’ Priya said. ‘Read a year of it in one sitting.’

‘Why?’

‘Because a hundred and eighty entries about what sold, read consecutively, is the shape of your business and you have never seen it drawn.’


## What kind of files a person turns out to keep

‘One more breakdown and then I want to talk about something else entirely,’ Priya said.

She showed the small chips listing file types with counts beside them.

‘This is what you have actually attached, by kind.’

‘Mine is going to be photographs,’ Amir said, before looking.

He looked.

‘Mine is overwhelmingly photographs.’

‘How overwhelmingly?’

‘About three quarters.’ He read the rest. ‘Then documents, then a few spreadsheets that Yasmin made, then some things I do not recognise.’

‘What are the photographs of?’

‘Two things.’ He counted them. ‘Pastries, which is deliberate, because I photograph anything that comes out right so I can remember what right looked like. And invoices, which is because a supplier hands me a piece of paper and I photograph it with the phone before I lose it.’

Priya made a face he could see.

‘Say what that face is.’

‘The invoices should not be photographs,’ she said. ‘The pastries obviously should. But a photograph of a document is a picture of words, and a document is words, and those behave completely differently when you search.’

‘Do they?’

‘A supplier contract that exists as a proper document is searchable for every term in it,’ Priya said. ‘A photograph of the same contract depends entirely on how you held the phone, whether the light was even, and whether the page was flat.’

‘Mine are none of those things. They are taken in about four seconds on a counter with somebody waiting.’

‘Then that is the change I would make, and only for the things that matter.’ She was careful about it. ‘Not every invoice. The contracts, the agreements, the annual terms. The ones somebody might argue with you about.’

‘I have a scanner in the office that I use twice a year.’

‘Then it is a scanner you use eleven times a year,’ said Yasmin, from off camera.

‘She has been waiting to say that as well.’

Priya described her own, which had produced a mirror-image realisation.

‘Mine is mostly page documents, which is correct, because I work in papers.’ She read it. ‘Then images, which is microscopy, which is also correct. And almost no spreadsheets at all, which is wrong.’

‘Why wrong?’

‘Because every experiment I run produces a data file and those files live in a directory on my machine rather than attached to the entry describing the experiment.’ She was frank about it. ‘Which means the entry says what I did and the numbers are somewhere else, and connecting them requires me to remember a folder name.’

‘Why did you never attach them?’

‘Because at the beginning they felt like working files rather than records, and by the time they were records the habit was set.’ She shrugged. ‘And nothing ever told me. The chart told me, by having a number next to a format that was almost zero.’

‘So we have both learned the same thing in opposite directions.’

‘We have both learned that our attachment habits were formed by convenience rather than by a decision,’ Priya said, ‘which is true of almost everybody and which nothing else would have surfaced.’


## The part that is not analysis

‘Right,’ Priya said. ‘I want to talk about something that is not diagnostic at all, and I want to say first that I am slightly self-conscious about it.’

‘Then it is probably the important part,’ Amir said.

‘A doctorate is four years and almost nobody watches you do it.’ She said it carefully. ‘My supervisor sees me for an hour a fortnight. My family thinks I am doing something they cannot picture. There is no manager, no colleague at the next desk, and no external marker of any kind between one milestone and the next, and the milestones are about nine months apart.’

‘That is a long time to work without anybody noticing.’

‘It is a very long time, and the specific difficulty is not loneliness.’ Priya paused. ‘It is that on any given Wednesday I have no way of knowing whether I am doing well. There is nothing to compare against. So the default assumption becomes that I am behind, because that assumption is always available and never contradicted.’

‘And the chart contradicts it.’

‘The chart contradicts it, in a form I cannot argue with, because I made every point on it.’ She was definite. ‘Three years of near daily activity. Two thousand entries and something. Sixteen hundred papers processed. And I cannot dismiss any of that as self-deception, because I did not produce it in order to feel better. I produced it by working.’

Amir was nodding slowly.

‘Say your version,’ Priya said. ‘Because I suspect you have one.’

He took a moment.

‘Nobody told me to do this,’ he said. ‘That is the whole of it, really. My daughter set it up and then left me alone, and every entry in there exists because I decided, on that particular evening, that a thing was worth writing down.’

‘And nobody checked.’

‘Nobody has ever checked. There is no manager. There is no report. My accountant does not care.’ He turned a hand over. ‘I am fifty-one years old and I run a bakery my father ran, and the honest truth is that I have spent most of my working life feeling that I am maintaining something rather than building anything.’

Nobody said anything for a second.

‘And the number says otherwise,’ Priya said.

‘The number says nine hundred and eleven, and a chart says I have done it nearly every day for eighteen months, and that is a thing I built.’ He said it plainly. ‘Not the bakery. My father built the bakery. That.’

Yasmin had come back into frame and did not say anything at all.

‘I want to make a distinction,’ Priya said after a moment, ‘because I think it matters and because I have been on the other side of it.’

‘Go.’

‘I have used applications that try to produce exactly this feeling with streaks and badges and a ring that closes.’ She was dismissive. ‘And they work for a fortnight and then you notice that the thing congratulating you is a mechanism, and it turns manipulative, and I have deleted three of them in anger.’

‘Why is this different?’

‘Because nothing here is congratulating anybody.’ Priya was emphatic. ‘There is no message. There is no encouragement. It is a count of what you did, drawn as a line, with no opinion attached, and you have to go and look at it deliberately.’

‘So the absence of praise is why it works.’

‘The absence of praise is exactly why it works, because praise from a machine is worth nothing and a number you generated yourself is evidence.’ She shrugged. ‘One of those can be dismissed and the other cannot.’

Amir laughed suddenly.

‘I am going to show Yasmin the chart.’

‘I am here,’ said Yasmin.

‘I am going to show it to you again, in about a month, when the staff label has more than three things on it.’


## What a chart cannot see

‘I want to say the limiting thing before we finish,’ Priya said, ‘because I have watched people go slightly wrong with this and I would rather you did not.’

‘Go on.’

‘None of this measures quality and it is not trying to.’ She was precise. ‘A day with fourteen entries on it is not a better day than a day with one. Some of my most important work happened on days that produced a single paragraph, and one of my biggest entry counts is a day I spent processing a reading list, which was necessary and was not thinking.’

‘So a high line is not a good line.’

‘A high line is a busy line, and busy and valuable are different things that occasionally coincide.’ She shrugged. ‘I know somebody who started splitting entries in order to raise a number, which is absurd and which happened within a fortnight of them discovering the chart.’

Amir laughed.

‘I can see myself doing that.’

‘Everybody can see themselves doing that, which is why I raise it.’ Priya was definite. ‘The moment a record becomes a target it stops being a record. It becomes a performance, and a performance about yourself, for yourself, which is the least useful document anybody can produce.’

‘Then how do you use it without doing that?’

‘I look at it monthly rather than daily, and I never look at it while I am working.’ She counted. ‘And I have a rule that I do not act on the temporal charts at all. I read them, I notice what they say, and I do nothing. Only the distributions produce actions.’

‘Why the distinction?’

‘Because the distributions are about coverage and the lines are about effort, and effort is the thing I should not be managing by number.’ She paused. ‘My effort is governed by what needs doing. If I start managing it by a line on a chart I have inverted the relationship between the work and the record of it.’

Amir was thinking.

‘There is a second thing it cannot see,’ he said. ‘Which occurred to me while you were talking.’

‘Say it.’

‘Everything I actually know about baking is not in there.’ He said it plainly. ‘Nine hundred and eleven entries and not one of them contains the thing my hands know about dough at half past five in the morning. That is thirty years of it and it is not written anywhere and it never will be.’

‘Does that trouble you?’

‘It used to trouble me a great deal and it stopped about a year ago.’ He shrugged. ‘Because I worked out that the notes are not the knowledge. They are the part of the knowledge that can be handed to somebody else, and that is a smaller thing and still worth having.’

Priya was quiet for a second.

‘That is a better statement of it than anything I would have said.’

‘It is a thing you learn from a trade rather than from a university,’ Amir said. ‘My father could not have written any of it down either. What I have that he did not is a record of everything around it, which is what somebody would need if they had to run this place without me for a month.’


## What each of them decided to do

‘Before we finish,’ Priya said, ‘tell me what you are actually changing, because otherwise this was an entertaining evening and nothing else.’

‘Three things and one of them is Yasmin's,’ Amir said.

‘Say all three.’

‘Staff, obviously. Three entries this week, and I am going to start with the two long-serving people because those are the ones where the knowledge is entirely in my head and I am the only copy.’ He counted. ‘Contracts get scanned rather than photographed. Not everything. The dozen documents that would matter in an argument.’

‘And the third?’

‘I am going to read a year of the daily entries in one sitting, on a Sunday, because you told me to and because I have never done it and it costs me an afternoon.’

‘That is the one I would be most interested in hearing about,’ Priya said.

‘Yours?’

‘Two, and one of them I have been avoiding for two years.’ She was frank. ‘My experiment data files get attached to their entries from now on, and I am doing the last six months retrospectively because that is the period I still work with.’

‘And the avoided one?’

‘The three research directions.’ She took a breath. ‘One abandoned, formally, in writing, with a sentence saying why. One scheduled with an actual date. One started, which I have already done.’

‘Why does the abandoned one need writing down?’

‘Because otherwise it is not abandoned, it is dormant, and dormant things generate a small amount of guilt indefinitely,’ Priya said. ‘Writing the sentence is what ends it. And in three years when somebody asks why I did not pursue that direction I will have an answer that is better than having forgotten.’

Amir was quiet for a second.

‘My father had about four of those,’ he said. ‘Things he meant to do with the shop. I know about them because he mentioned them, and none of them are written anywhere, and I have spent twenty years half wondering whether I ought to be doing them.’

‘That is exactly the cost.’

‘That is exactly the cost, and it is paid by somebody who was not there when the decision was made,’ he said. ‘I am going to write mine down. Not tonight. But I am going to.’


## The Sunday afternoon

He did the reading eleven days later and reported back in the group rather than to Priya directly, which she said afterward was the correct instinct because everybody should have seen it.

He wrote: I have read a year of the daily entries. It took four hours. I want to say three things.

The first is that I have never done this before and I have been keeping them for nearly two years, which is a considerable amount of writing that I have produced and never read.

The second is that there is a pattern in it that nobody could have told me and that I did not know. Three times in the year, I have written some version of the sentence I need to talk to somebody about the mornings. Three separate times, months apart, in different words, and each time I did nothing about it. I did not remember writing it the first two times.

The third is that the mornings are the actual problem in my business and have been for about four years and I have been treating them as a season.

Priya replied within the hour: that is the single best argument for keeping a daily log I have ever read and I would like your permission to describe it to people.

He wrote: you may describe it if you also describe the part where I ignored myself twice.

Suki wrote: that is the part that makes it worth describing.

Felix wrote: a man who reads a year of his own notes and finds that he has been telling himself something is a man who has just acquired a colleague, and the colleague is himself.

## A mirror rather than a dashboard

What that conversation demonstrates is that the same panel answers two entirely different classes of question, and that almost everybody opens it for the first and stops before reaching the second.

The operational figures are genuinely uninteresting for most people. Storage occupied matters only near a limit. A file count is inventory. Even the headline number of items is trivial except in one specific respect, which turned out to be the beginning of the whole conversation: people underestimate it, consistently and by a wide margin, because memory records the exceptional periods and stays silent about the ordinary ones. Somebody who would confidently say three hundred and finds nine hundred has just learned something about the reliability of their own self-assessment rather than about their archive.

The activity views are where that correction becomes useful. A daily line reveals a working rhythm nobody constructed deliberately, and both practitioners recognised their own patterns immediately once drawn: one a flat and unvarying routine attached to the end of a working day, the other a spiked shape mapping directly onto a weekly timetable. The longer monthly view finds cycles instead, and produces the more valuable observations, since a pattern recurring four times a year is invisible while it is happening and obvious when drawn. A predictable trough after every deadline, previously experienced as a personal failure, becomes a phase somebody can plan around. A season believed to be quiet turns out to generate substantial thinking.

The distributional views do something categorically different, and it is the part worth understanding properly. They do not measure effort at all; they describe attention, and their unique property is that they make absences visible. Nothing else in ordinary working life does. Every other record shows what somebody produced, so an intention that quietly stopped produces no evidence anywhere and can remain nominally alive for years. A category created deliberately and applied four times is a plan that ended without anybody noticing, and in both cases described here the discovery was uncomfortable and immediately actionable: three abandoned research directions in one archive, and a category covering five employees with three entries in it.

The structural breakdown answers a related question about where work has actually accumulated rather than where somebody believes it has. That produced no crisis in either case and changed how one of them describes her own project, which turns out to matter considerably in conversations where the description is the thing being assessed.

The file type breakdown exposes habits formed by convenience rather than decision, which is nearly universal and nearly invisible. One practitioner discovered three quarters of their attachments were photographs, including documents that would behave far better as documents. The other discovered an almost total absence of the data files their own work produces, sitting in a directory instead of attached to the records describing them. Neither habit was ever chosen; both were established in the first weeks and never revisited.

Underneath all of it sits something that is not analysis. Both of these people work without observation: a doctoral candidate with a supervisor seen for an hour a fortnight and milestones nine months apart, and a small business owner whom nobody has ever checked on. In both cases the default assumption during any ordinary week is that they are behind, because that assumption is always available and nothing contradicts it. A record of activity contradicts it in the one form that cannot be dismissed, which is a count somebody generated themselves without knowing it was being kept.

That distinction is why this differs from every mechanism designed to produce the same feeling. Streaks and badges manufacture a signal, and the manufacture eventually becomes visible, at which point encouragement reads as manipulation. Nothing here congratulates anybody, offers a message, or asks to be looked at. It is a count with no opinion attached, which is precisely what makes it credible, and credibility is the only property that matters in evidence about oneself.


## FAQs

### What is the analytics panel actually for?

Two different things, and most people only ever encounter the first. The operational layer reports inventory: how many entries exist, how many carry attachments, how many files there are, and how much space they occupy. The second layer is behavioural, showing activity over time and how content distributes across categories, structure, and file types. The first answers housekeeping questions and is uninteresting for most people. The second describes working patterns and attention, which is where the value sits. Most people open it once, register that the inventory figures are unremarkable, and never return, which means the informative half goes unread indefinitely. ### Why do people underestimate how much they have written?

Because memory records exceptions and stays silent about routine. The fortnight lost to a crisis is vivid; the eleven months of adding two items most days is invisible, because nothing about an ordinary day is memorable. Estimates are therefore constructed from the exceptions, which produces a figure typically around a third of the real one. Practitioners consistently report being confident about a number and then finding it substantially wrong, which says more about self-assessment than about any archive. Anybody can test this in ten seconds by writing down their estimate before opening the panel, which is more instructive than simply reading the number. It is one of the observations that costs nothing to act on and produces a return the next time somebody has a difficult week.

### Should storage figures be paid attention to?

Rarely, and only near a genuine limit. Storage is the one purely operational figure in most such panels, and on a modern machine a personal archive of text and documents occupies a negligible fraction of available space. It exists because somebody somewhere is running close to capacity and needs to know. Recognising which parts of a panel are not intended for you is a useful skill in itself, since most panels contain at least one figure that is somebody else's concern. Anybody near a genuine capacity limit will know it already, and everybody else can safely ignore the figure entirely. Anybody uncertain about this can settle it by looking once and noticing what they expected beforehand.

### What does the proportion of entries with attachments reveal?

How much of somebody's material carries evidence rather than only interpretation. A high proportion suggests a practice of attaching source documents, photographs, or data alongside written notes. A low proportion is not automatically a problem, since a great deal of valuable material is purely somebody's own thinking. It becomes informative when it contradicts what the work actually requires: a document-driven profession with few attachments has a gap somewhere, usually in one specific category rather than across the board. Comparing the figure against what the work demands, rather than against anybody else's, is the only way to interpret it usefully. The effect is small in any single instance and accumulates over a year of occasional checks, which is the general shape of everything worth doing here.

### What does a daily activity chart show?

The rhythm of somebody's working week, which almost nobody has constructed deliberately and almost everybody recognises immediately when it is drawn. A flat and consistent line indicates a habit attached to a fixed point in the day. A spiked line usually maps onto a timetable, with material generated heavily on some kinds of day and barely at all on others. Neither shape is better; what matters is whether the shape matches what somebody believes about their own week. The recognition tends to be immediate, which is a reasonable sign that the shape reflects something real rather than an artefact of how the chart is drawn.

### What should somebody look for in a daily chart?

The exceptions rather than the pattern. The overall shape is usually explicable within seconds, since it reflects a known timetable or routine. The useful observations are the days that do not fit: an unusually productive day that should have been quiet, or a gap in a period somebody remembers as ordinary. Those points identify something worth investigating, and the chart's role is to say where to look rather than to explain anything itself. Following up one anomaly is usually enough to establish whether the habit of checking is worth maintaining at all. ### What does a monthly activity view reveal that a daily one does not?

Cycles, which are invisible while they are happening. A pattern recurring three or four times a year cannot be perceived from inside any single instance, because each instance feels like circumstance. Practitioners describe discovering consistent surges before deadlines and equally consistent troughs afterward, occurring every single time across several years. Seeing the regularity converts what felt like a repeated personal failure into a predictable phase, which is a substantially different thing to plan around. The longer the record, the more informative this becomes, which is a reason to keep looking occasionally rather than concluding after the first month that it says nothing. It is one of the observations that costs nothing to act on and produces a return the next time somebody has a difficult week.

### How can somebody act on a recurring pattern once they see it?

By scheduling for it rather than against it. A predictable low-productivity period following every deadline can be filled with genuinely low-demand work instead of an ambitious plan that will not survive contact with it. A known seasonal peak can be prepared for in the preceding quiet period. The change is small and the effect is largely psychological: knowing a trough is coming removes the interpretation that something has gone wrong, which is frequently the more expensive part. The intervention is generally small and the reinterpretation is the larger part, since removing a false explanation frees whatever energy was being spent on it. ### What should somebody take away from all of this?

That a record generated as a side effect of working is the only disinterested account of their habits they will ever have, and that it answers questions introspection cannot. ### Does any of this apply to somebody who does not consider themselves organised?

Particularly to them. The people who benefit most from seeing a record of their own activity are those who assume they are inconsistent, since that assumption is almost always wrong and is held most firmly by people who never check. ### Is there a version of this that works for people who dislike being measured?

The distinction worth drawing is between being measured and having a record. Measurement implies a standard, an assessor, and a judgment, all of which are absent here. What exists is a count of what somebody did, visible only to them, with nothing attached to it. People with a strong aversion to workplace metrics frequently find this acceptable for exactly that reason, and several describe it as the only form of tracking they have tolerated for more than a few weeks. The aversion is generally well founded, since workplace metrics create incentives to influence them and consequently stop describing anything. What removes that here is simply that nobody else sees the figures and nothing is being assessed.

### What would you say to somebody who thinks this sounds self-indulgent?

That the alternative is not modesty but inaccuracy. Somebody who declines to look continues operating on a picture of themselves built from memory, which systematically exaggerates disruption and erases routine effort. That inaccuracy has costs: unnecessary discouragement during ordinary weeks, plans quietly abandoned without anybody noticing, and habits nobody chose. Spending a few minutes with an uncurated record is less an exercise in self-regard than a correction to a distorted account. Practitioners who resisted for exactly this reason describe the eventual look as unglamorous and useful, which is a reasonable expectation. Nothing about it involves admiring anything; it is closer to reading a meter.
