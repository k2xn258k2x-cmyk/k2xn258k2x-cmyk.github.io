Moving accumulated knowledge from one system to another is the point at which most people discover what they actually built, and it is rarely what they thought. The content transfers, because content is the easy part: text is text, files are files, and any competent tool can absorb both. What frequently does not transfer is the organisation, which is the part that took years and the part nobody itemised because it lived in the arrangement rather than in any of the material.

The failure has a recognisable shape. Categories that meant something in the old system arrive as strings attached to nothing. A hierarchy that expressed how somebody thought about their work flattens into a list. Documents that sat beside the notes explaining them end up in a directory of their own, connected to nothing. Every individual item survives and the relationships between them do not, which produces an archive that is technically complete and practically unusable.

![Migrating Into VaultBook From Anywhere](/assets/img/feature-screenshot-placeholder.svg)

That outcome is worse than it sounds, because the person who ran the migration cannot see it. They know what everything is, so a flattened structure remains navigable for them through memory, and the loss only becomes visible months later when memory has faded or somebody else needs to use the material. By then the original arrangement is gone and reconstructing it means recalling decisions made across several years.
<!--more-->


There is a second problem, which is temporal rather than structural. Migration asks somebody to commit to a new arrangement while their existing work continues, which means running two systems simultaneously during a period when neither is complete. Most people respond by attempting to transfer everything at once so the ambiguity ends quickly, and that instinct produces the largest possible project at the moment somebody understands the new system least.

The alternative is unfamiliar enough to feel wrong. Bring across what is currently in use, work in the new arrangement for a period, and let the historical material follow once somebody has learned what shape it should take. This means living with a partial archive for some weeks, which is uncomfortable and considerably less damaging than committing to a structure designed in advance by somebody who has not yet used the thing they are designing for.

What makes any of this bearable is a property of the destination rather than of the process. If material can be enriched after it arrives, meaning categories added later, documents attached later, structure adjusted later, and everything remaining searchable throughout, then a migration does not have to be correct on the day it happens. It only has to be complete enough to work in, and the refinement can happen against real use rather than against a plan.

There is also an opportunity that people miss because they are focused on preservation. A migration is the only occasion on which somebody looks at all of their material at once, and looking at all of it reveals things that daily use never does: duplication, abandoned categories, structures that made sense years ago and describe nothing current. Treating the move as a chance to decide rather than as an exercise in faithful copying produces something better than what existed, which is a strange outcome to aim for and the right one.

What follows is a conversation between two people whose starting positions could hardly have been less alike: one with a well-organised archive in a competing tool, and one with six years of material scattered across everything.


## Two very different starting points

Tomas had suggested it during a school holiday, which he said was the only period in which he could think about anything structural.

‘I want to compare migrations,’ he said, ‘and I suspect ours have nothing in common.’

‘Say why you think that,’ said Claire.

‘Because you came from something. I came from nothing arranged in a heap.’

Claire laughed.

‘Say the heap.’

‘Lesson plans in an online document service. Laboratory safety material on a memory stick. Student project files in electronic mail attachments, some of which I had never downloaded.’ He counted. ‘Physical notebooks. And a folder on a school machine that I could not access from home.’

‘How many years?’

‘Six, and no organising principle at any point.’ Tomas was cheerful about it. ‘Which sounds like a disaster and turned out to be an advantage, and I want to come back to that.’

‘Mine was the opposite.’ Claire shared something. ‘Over two thousand text files in a well-regarded tool, organised in folders, categorised with tags, cross-referenced with links between them, maintained carefully for about four years.’

‘Which sounds like a considerably easier migration.’

‘It sounds easier and it was harder in one specific respect, which took me a month to understand.’

‘Say it.’

‘Because I had something worth preserving, I spent the first fortnight trying to preserve it exactly,’ Claire said. ‘And exact preservation was the wrong objective and I could not see that at the time.’


## Two thousand files and a mistaken assumption

‘Walk me through it,’ Tomas said.

‘The mechanical part was straightforward and I want to say that first, because people expect the mechanical part to be the difficulty.’ Claire was precise. ‘There is a tool for exactly this source. The files went in, the content came across, and the formatting survived.’

‘How long?’

‘The import itself was minutes. The reorganisation was six weeks, and that ratio is the honest description of what a migration actually is.’

‘Say what did not map.’

‘The shape.’ She thought about how to describe it. ‘My previous arrangement was essentially flat with a dense web of links between items, which is how that tool wants you to work and which I had done properly.’

‘And here the structure is a hierarchy.’

‘Here the structure is a hierarchy, and my first instinct was to reproduce the flatness and rely on connections, which produced something genuinely poor.’ Claire was blunt. ‘Two thousand entries under almost no structure, findable only by search, with links that had lost most of their meaning.’

‘Why had the links lost meaning?’

‘Because a link between two items is a statement that they are related, and in a flat system that is the only structural information available, so it carries an enormous amount.’ She counted. ‘In a hierarchy, most of that relatedness is expressed by position. Two documents about the same matter are related because they are both under the matter. The link becomes redundant and the redundancy is not obvious until you look at four hundred of them.’

‘So you rebuilt.’

‘I rebuilt, and I want to be honest that it was three evenings a week for about six weeks, and that I complained about it constantly.’ Claire laughed. ‘And that the result is better than what I had, which I would not have believed at the outset.’

‘Say what the structure became.’

‘Clients at the top, matters beneath them, and entries under matters.’ She was definite. ‘Which is how my professional life is actually shaped, and which my previous arrangement had not expressed at all, because that tool does not encourage you to think in hierarchies.’

‘And the categories?’

‘My old tags became labels, mostly, and this is where I made my most useful decision.’ Claire paused. ‘I had about sixty tags. I brought across nine.’

Tomas made a noise.

‘Sixty to nine.’

‘Sixty to nine, and the fifty-one I dropped were almost all things that the structure now expresses.’ She counted. ‘I had a tag for each client. I now have a page for each client, so the tag says nothing. I had tags for years, which are dates on entries. I had tags for document types, which I kept, because those genuinely cut across everything.’

‘That is the rule, is it not.’

‘That is precisely the rule and I arrived at it painfully.’ She was emphatic. ‘A category that duplicates the structure is a decision you have to make twice and will make inconsistently. Categories should express only what the structure cannot.’

‘How did you decide which fifty-one to drop?’

‘I listed them with counts and looked at the small ones first, which is a technique I learned from somebody else in the group.’ Claire shrugged. ‘Eleven of them had fewer than four items on them, and every one of those was an idea I had once had and never sustained. They came across as nothing.’


## Six years in a heap

‘Now yours,’ Claire said, ‘because I have been curious since you described it.’

‘Mine had no import tool because there was nothing to import from.’ Tomas was matter of fact. ‘There is no mechanism for a memory stick and a folder of electronic mail, and there should not be, because those are not systems.’

‘So what did you do?’

‘I built the structure first, before moving anything, which is the opposite of what you did and which I only did because I had no choice.’ He described it. ‘A weekend. Pages for each course, sections of each course beneath them, and about six labels.’

‘Designed against what?’

‘Against how I teach, which is the only thing I actually knew.’ He shrugged. ‘I could not design against my existing arrangement because I did not have one.’

‘Which is the advantage you mentioned.’

‘Which is the advantage, and I did not appreciate it until I heard you describe six weeks of undoing something.’ Tomas was thoughtful. ‘I had nothing to undo. I had a heap and a blank structure, and the migration was the first time in six years that I had actually organised anything.’

‘Say how you populated it.’

‘Source by source, and each source needed a different method.’ He counted them. ‘The online documents were the easiest. Open one, select everything, paste it into an entry. The formatting survives, which surprised me, and I had assumed I would be reconstructing headings by hand.’

‘How many?’

‘About ninety lesson plans, and it took two evenings, and it is genuinely tedious and requires no thought at all.’ He laughed. ‘Which made it the ideal thing to do while watching something.’

‘And the documents on the memory stick?’

‘Attached to whichever entry they belong to, which meant deciding, which was slower.’ Tomas was precise. ‘Forty safety documents across about thirty entries, and I had to open several of them to remember what they were.’

‘Because the names were unhelpful.’

‘Because the names were assigned by a publisher and meant nothing, and that is a general problem with anything you did not write.’

‘And the notebooks?’

‘The notebooks are the part I am proudest of and it took an afternoon.’ He was animated. ‘Four physical notebooks of laboratory observations and modifications I have made to procedures over six years.’

‘Which you photographed.’

‘Which I photographed, page by page, flat on a table with good light, because a colleague warned me about doing it casually.’ Tomas shrugged. ‘About two hundred pages, and they went in as images inside the entries they belong to.’

‘And the handwriting.’

‘Is read and becomes searchable, which is the part I did not expect and which changed what those notebooks are.’ He paused. ‘They were a shelf. They are now part of the searchable material, and I have found things in them that I had entirely forgotten writing.’

‘Give me one.’

‘A modification to a titration procedure that I worked out four years ago because the school glassware is a particular size.’ He was rueful. ‘Which I rediscovered by accident two years later and worked out again, and both versions are now in there, in my own handwriting, dated.’


## The decisions nobody warns you about

‘I want to talk about the choices rather than the mechanics,’ Claire said, ‘because the mechanics took an afternoon and the choices took two months.’

‘Say the first one.’

‘How deep to nest.’ She was definite. ‘Which sounds trivial and determines whether anything is findable.’

‘What did you settle on?’

‘Three levels and almost never four.’ Claire counted. ‘Client, matter, and then entries. I experimented with a fourth level for document types within a matter and abandoned it within a fortnight.’

‘Why?’

‘Because at four levels I could no longer see a whole matter at once, and a matter is the unit I actually think about.’ She shrugged. ‘Every level of depth is a thing you have to open, and opening is a decision, and decisions accumulate.’

‘Mine is two,’ Tomas said. ‘Course, then unit, then entries beneath.’

‘That is three.’

‘That is three and I count from the wrong end, which tells you something about how I think.’ He laughed. ‘And I tried four as well, for individual lessons under units, and abandoned it for exactly your reason.’

‘Then that is a finding.’

‘Three levels, arrived at independently, in two professions with nothing in common.’ Tomas was interested. ‘Which either means three is correct or means we are both limited in the same way.’

‘I would say three is where a hierarchy stops helping and starts costing,’ Claire said. ‘Beyond that you are filing rather than organising.’

They discussed the second decision, which both said was harder.

‘When something is a section inside an entry and when it is its own entry,’ Tomas said.

‘Which I got wrong initially in both directions.’

‘Say the wrong versions.’

‘I started by making everything separate, because my old arrangement was flat and separate was familiar.’ Claire was blunt. ‘Which produced thirty items under a matter, in no order, none of which made sense alone.’

‘And then?’

‘And then I overcorrected and put everything into sections, and had entries containing eleven sections that should have been four entries.’ She shrugged. ‘The rule I eventually got from somebody else is that it is a section if it is about the same subject and would be looked at together, and an entry if it needs its own date, categories, or protection.’

‘I had the same overcorrection,’ Tomas said. ‘My assessments started as sections inside unit entries and became separate entries, because an assessment has a date and the date is the point.’

The third decision was one Claire said nobody warns anybody about.

‘Duplicates,’ she said.

‘Say the problem.’

‘Six years of accumulated material contains the same thing several times, in different states, and you cannot tell which is current.’ She was frank. ‘I had four versions of one document, all with slightly different names, and I had no memory of which had been filed.’

‘What did you do?’

‘Brought all four across and marked three of them, and then resolved it over the following month as I encountered each matter.’ Claire shrugged. ‘Which is unsatisfying and is the only workable approach, because resolving it at migration time means opening four hundred documents in one week.’

‘Mine was worse and simpler,’ Tomas said. ‘I had the same lesson plan in three places, and the three were genuinely different because I had improved it separately in each.’

‘Which is the awful case.’

‘Which is the awful case and the resolution was to bring all three in as one entry with the three versions as sections, and then write a fourth version that combined them.’ He was matter of fact. ‘Which took an hour and produced a better lesson than any of the three.’

‘That is a migration producing an improvement.’

‘That is a migration forcing me to confront something I had been avoiding for four years by never looking at all three at once.’


## The weeks after

‘Say what happened after the content was in,’ Claire said, ‘because that period is the one nobody describes.’

‘Mine was about labelling.’ Tomas pulled something up. ‘I had created six labels during the weekend and applied them to about a third of what I imported, because applying labels while pasting ninety documents is not something anybody sustains.’

‘So two thirds were bare.’

‘Two thirds were bare and I had a slightly sick feeling about it.’ He shrugged. ‘And then I noticed the suggestions.’

‘Say what they do.’

‘When I open an entry with no labels, it offers ones from my existing vocabulary that appear to fit the content.’ Tomas was precise. ‘Which is not automatic labelling and I would not want it to be. It is a proposal I accept or ignore.’

‘Was it accurate?’

‘Accurate enough that I labelled the remaining six hundred over about three weeks, at a rate of a few a day, by accepting a suggestion and moving on.’ He was definite. ‘Which is completely different from a project. It never felt like work.’

‘Mine was attachments,’ Claire said. ‘Because my previous arrangement referenced documents that lived somewhere else entirely.’

‘Say how many.’

‘Several hundred, sitting in a directory structure that mirrored my notes and was connected to them only by convention.’ She was blunt. ‘Which had worked for four years and which I had stopped noticing was fragile.’

‘And you attached them.’

‘Over about a month, matter by matter, as I worked on each.’ Claire shrugged. ‘Which is the general principle of the whole period. I did not do a project. I did the enrichment as part of ordinary work, and after a month it was largely done.’

She mentioned one thing she had done immediately rather than gradually.

‘Protection,’ she said. ‘That was the first evening.’

‘Say why it could not wait.’

‘Because a substantial amount of my material is privileged, and it had been unprotected in my previous arrangement for four years, which I had been aware of and had rationalised.’ Claire was frank. ‘And the migration made me look at all of it at once, which removed the rationalisation.’

‘How much?’

‘About eleven percent of my entries, and it took an evening, and I should have done it four years earlier.’ She paused. ‘Which is the other thing a migration does. It shows you what you have been ignoring.’

Tomas described something he had done in the same period.

‘Dates,’ he said. ‘My material had deadlines in it and none of them were recorded anywhere except in my head and the school calendar.’

‘Say what changed.’

‘I went through the current semester and put dates on anything with one, which took about forty minutes.’ He shrugged. ‘And the assessment schedule for a whole term now exists as a thing rather than as something I reconstruct each August.’

‘And the duplicates?’

‘I found several using search, which I had not anticipated as a use.’ Tomas was interested. ‘I searched a phrase I knew I had written and got three results, which is how I discovered two of the triplicates.’

‘Search as a duplicate detector.’

‘Search as a duplicate detector for material I had brought in from three places, which is exactly the situation in which duplicates exist and nobody knows.’


## The things that did not survive

‘I want to ask what you actually lost,’ Tomas said, ‘because both of us have described this as a success and I do not believe anybody loses nothing.’

‘I lost two things and one of them still irritates me.’

‘Say the irritating one.’

‘The dates on which I originally wrote things.’ Claire was flat about it. ‘My old files carried creation dates in the file system, and when the content came across, the entries were created on the day I imported them.’

‘So everything looks like it was written in one week.’

‘Everything from four years looks like it was written across nine days in the autumn, which is obviously false and is also what the record now says.’ She shrugged. ‘Which matters in my work more than it would in most, because when something was written is occasionally a question somebody asks.’

‘What did you do?’

‘For about eighty entries where it genuinely matters, I wrote the original date into the body as a line at the top.’ Claire was matter of fact. ‘Which is manual, which took an evening, and which is the honest solution rather than a clever one.’

‘And the rest?’

‘The rest carry a false creation date and I have stopped minding, because for the other nineteen hundred nobody will ever ask.’

‘Say the second thing.’

‘The connections.’ She paused. ‘Which I have described as redundant and which were not entirely redundant. Perhaps one link in ten was saying something the hierarchy does not, which is that two unrelated matters had a substantive relationship.’

‘Cross-references.’

‘Genuine cross-references, between things in different branches, and those are the ones I miss.’ Claire was frank. ‘I rebuilt about forty of them by hand over several months as I encountered them, and I am sure there are others I have not noticed are gone.’

Tomas asked his own.

‘Mine is smaller and stranger,’ he said. ‘I lost the mess.’

‘Say what that means.’

‘My old arrangement was chaotic and the chaos contained information.’ He was working it out. ‘A folder that had grown enormous told me that was where I had been spending my time. A file I had renamed four times told me I had been uncertain about something.’

‘And in an organised structure.’

‘And in an organised structure everything looks equally deliberate, because I put it there deliberately.’ Tomas shrugged. ‘Which is better in every practical respect and which flattened something I could previously read.’

‘Do you want it back?’

‘Not remotely, and I noticed its absence and thought it was worth saying,’ he said. ‘An organised system tells you what you decided. A disorganised one occasionally tells you what you actually did, and those are different.’


## The step both of them nearly skipped

‘There is one thing I did not know to do and somebody told me,’ Claire said.

‘Rebuilding.’

‘Rebuilding, after the bulk of the attachments were in.’ She described it. ‘Because material indexes as it arrives, which is fine for ordinary use, and I had added several hundred documents over a month in a fairly chaotic manner.’

‘What did you actually do?’

‘Ran it once, in an evening, and went to bed.’ Claire shrugged. ‘And the following morning the contents of every document in my collection were searchable, including the ones I had attached in a hurry on days when I was not paying attention.’

‘Was it necessary?’

‘I do not know, honestly, and that is the reason to do it.’ She was frank. ‘A month of unsystematic bulk addition is exactly the situation where I would not be confident, and one evening removes the question.’

‘Say what it changed.’

‘I searched a claim phrase the next day and got a result from inside a document I had attached three weeks earlier without opening.’ Claire paused. ‘Which I would not have found otherwise, because I did not know what was in it.’

Tomas said his experience was smaller and more surprising.

‘I did not know my attached documents were searchable at all,’ he said.

‘You had not tried.’

‘I had not tried, because in every previous arrangement of mine an attachment was a thing you opened.’ He shrugged. ‘I ran the rebuild because Claire mentioned it in the group and I did it out of obedience rather than understanding.’

‘And?’

‘And the following week I searched a chemical name and got back a safety document I had attached from the memory stick, and I stopped what I was doing.’ Tomas laughed. ‘Because I had forty of those and I had been treating them as forty things I would open when I remembered which one.’

‘Whereas.’

‘Whereas they are forty documents whose contents participate, which means a student asking about a substance produces an answer in four seconds rather than in four minutes of looking through a folder.’

‘That is worth an evening of processing.’

‘That is worth considerably more than an evening and I would have missed it entirely, because nothing prompts you to run a rebuild and the benefit is invisible until you happen to search for something.’


## What each would tell somebody starting tomorrow

‘Distil it,’ Tomas said. ‘Because somebody is going to ask both of us this and I would rather have an answer.’

‘Four things,’ Claire said. ‘Design the shape before you import anything. Do not reproduce your old arrangement. Bring the content in first and reorganise afterward rather than trying to do both at once. And protect anything sensitive on the first evening rather than intending to.’

‘Say more about the second.’

‘Because it is the one I got wrong and it cost me six weeks.’ She was definite. ‘Your old structure was designed for the constraints of your old tool. Reproducing it means importing those constraints along with your content, and they are the one thing you were trying to leave behind.’

‘And the third sounds contradictory with the first.’

‘It sounds contradictory and it is not.’ Claire counted. ‘Design the shape, meaning the top two levels and the categories, which is a weekend. Then import everything into that shape roughly. Then refine over weeks, against real use.’

‘Rather than designing the whole thing.’

‘Rather than designing the whole thing in advance, which is designing for a system you have not used yet and which is how people produce elaborate arrangements they abandon in a month.’

Tomas gave his, which he said was for people with no source system.

‘Five, and mine are less elegant.’ He counted. ‘Start with what you are using now rather than with the archive. Create the categories first and migrate into them rather than labelling afterward. Photograph physical material and let the reading make it searchable. Give yourself a month before deciding whether the structure is right. And use search to find what you misfiled, because you will misfile things.’

‘Say the first one, because it is the one people resist.’

‘Because everybody wants to start with the oldest material, on the reasonable grounds that it is the most at risk.’ Tomas was emphatic. ‘And it is the worst place to start, because you do not yet know what the structure should be and you are making four hundred filing decisions with no experience.’

‘Whereas current material.’

‘Whereas the current semester is material I understand completely, which means the decisions are easy, and by the time I reached the archive I had made those decisions a hundred times and knew what I was doing.’

‘Did the archive suffer from waiting?’

‘The archive had waited six years. It could wait another two months.’ He shrugged. ‘And the version of the archive I imported in month three is considerably better organised than the version I would have imported in week one.’

Claire raised something she said applied to both.

‘Neither of us finished,’ she said.

‘Say what you mean.’

‘I have material from before the four years covered by my old arrangement that is still in a filing cabinet and a set of directories.’ She was matter of fact. ‘Perhaps a fifth of my working life, and I have not brought it in, and I am not certain I will.’

‘Is that a failure?’

‘I decided it is a judgment.’ Claire counted. ‘It is closed matters from a period I rarely revisit, and the cost of importing it is substantial and the benefit is speculative. So it sits where it is, catalogued, and if I need something I know where to look.’

‘Mine is similar,’ Tomas said. ‘Two courses I no longer teach and will not teach again, and their material is on a drive, and I have decided it stays there.’

‘Which is a decision rather than an omission.’

‘Which is a decision I made deliberately about eight months in, and the deliberateness is what stopped it nagging me,’ he said. ‘An unfinished migration produces guilt. A migration with a stated boundary does not.’


## What Claire did before touching anything

‘There is one thing I did that I have not mentioned and I think it is the most important,’ Claire said.

‘Say it.’

‘I copied everything, twice, before I imported a single file.’ She was matter of fact. ‘Two copies of my entire previous arrangement, on two drives, and I put one of them in a drawer and did not touch it.’

‘Because the import might have gone wrong.’

‘Because I did not know it would go right, and the material was four years of work, and the cost of being wrong was catastrophic and the cost of the precaution was twenty minutes.’ Claire shrugged. ‘Which is not a sophisticated observation and it is the thing I would say first to anybody.’

‘Did you need it?’

‘Not once, and that is not the point.’ She was definite. ‘I have the drive. It is still in the drawer. I will keep it for another year and then probably forever, because it costs nothing.’

Tomas was slightly sheepish.

‘I did not do that,’ he said.

‘Say why not.’

‘Because my sources were still in their original places, which is a genuine reason and which was also luck rather than planning.’ He shrugged. ‘I was copying out of documents rather than moving them, so the originals sat where they were.’

‘Which is a backup you did not have to make.’

‘Which is a backup I had by accident, and if my sources had been a single system I would have moved things and would probably not have thought about it.’ Tomas was frank. ‘Which I only realised while you were talking.’

‘There is a second precaution and it is smaller,’ Claire said.

‘Go on.’

‘I did the first hundred files as a test rather than the whole two thousand.’ She counted. ‘Imported them, looked at what arrived, checked the formatting, and only then did the rest.’

‘Did anything come across badly?’

‘Two things, both of which I could correct in how I prepared the remainder.’ She was matter of fact. ‘Which would have been two thousand corrections rather than a hundred if I had imported everything first.’

‘That is a rule.’

‘That is a rule for anything bulk, in any context, and I did not invent it,’ Claire said. ‘Do a hundred, look, then do the rest. It costs an hour and it is the difference between one correction and a great many.’


## The month neither of them enjoyed

‘I want to be honest about something,’ Claire said, ‘because everything we have described sounds orderly and there was a period that was not.’

‘Say it.’

‘Weeks three to six, when I was running two systems.’ She was blunt. ‘My old arrangement still had things in it that I had not brought across, and the new one had everything else, and for a month I did not know which one to look in first.’

‘Which is the thing everybody dreads.’

‘Which is the thing everybody dreads and it is genuinely unpleasant and it is also finite.’ Claire shrugged. ‘I told myself it would last a month and it lasted about five weeks, and knowing roughly how long was most of what made it bearable.’

‘How did you handle the ambiguity?’

‘Badly for the first fortnight, and then I made a rule.’ She counted. ‘Search the new one first, always. If it is not there, look in the old one, and then immediately bring it across.’

‘So the searching populated the migration.’

‘The searching populated the migration, which meant the things I actually needed arrived first, ordered by how much I needed them.’ Claire was definite. ‘Which is a considerably better ordering than anything I would have designed, because it was determined by my actual work rather than by my sense of what mattered.’

Tomas was nodding.

‘I did the same thing without formulating it,’ he said.

‘Say yours.’

‘Something would come up in a lesson and I would go looking, and half the time it was not in yet, and I would bring it in on the spot.’ He shrugged. ‘Which took two minutes and which meant the material arrived in order of usefulness.’

‘How long did yours last?’

‘Longer, because my sources were scattered and there was no single old system to abandon.’ Tomas thought. ‘Perhaps three months before I stopped going anywhere else, and about six before the memory stick genuinely stopped being consulted.’

‘Was there a moment?’

‘There was a specific moment and I remember it.’ He was slightly amused. ‘A student asked about something in November and I went to search, and it did not occur to me to look anywhere else, and afterward I noticed that it had not occurred to me.’

‘Which is when a migration is actually finished.’

‘Which is when a migration is finished, and it is not when the last file moves.’ He was definite. ‘It is when you stop checking the old place, and that is a habit rather than a state of the data.’

Claire said something she had thought about since.

‘There is a thing nobody tells you about the difficult month,’ she said.

‘Go on.’

‘It is the period during which you learn the new system, and you learn it because you are forced to use it for real work under mild pressure.’ She paused. ‘If the migration had been instantaneous I would have arrived in a fully populated arrangement that I did not understand, and I would have used perhaps a fifth of it.’

‘So the awkwardness was instructive.’

‘The awkwardness was where the learning happened, and I would not choose to repeat it and I would not want it removed.’


## Start where you are

Migration fails for reasons that have almost nothing to do with the destination. Content transfers easily, because text is text and files are files. What frequently does not transfer is the organisation, which took years, which nobody itemised because it lived in the arrangement rather than in the material, and whose absence is invisible to the person who ran the move because they still remember what everything is.

Both practitioners here reached the same conclusion from opposite starting points, and it is the least intuitive advice in the conversation: do not reproduce the old structure. One had four years of careful organisation in a competing tool and spent a fortnight preserving it faithfully before concluding that faithful preservation was the wrong objective. A structure designed for the constraints of one system carries those constraints along with the content, and they are precisely what somebody was trying to leave. Her flat, densely linked arrangement produced two thousand items under almost no hierarchy, with connections that had lost most of their meaning because relatedness was now expressed by position.

The other had no structure at all, which he described as an advantage and which the first envied openly. Six years of material across documents, a memory stick, mail attachments, and physical notebooks gave him nothing to undo, so his migration was the first time he had organised anything. He built the shape first, over a weekend, designed against how he actually teaches, and then populated it source by source with a different method for each.

Several specific findings recur. Three levels of hierarchy, arrived at independently in unrelated professions, with both having tried four and abandoned it within a fortnight because a fourth level breaks the ability to see a whole unit at once. A large reduction in categories, from sixty to nine in one case, on the principle that anything the structure already expresses should not also be a category. And the section-or-entry question, which both got wrong in both directions before settling on the rule that same subject and looked at together means a section, while its own date, categories, or protection means an entry.

The enrichment period is where the substance actually lands, and both describe it as ordinary work rather than as a project. Categories suggested from an existing vocabulary and accepted a few at a time across three weeks. Several hundred documents attached matter by matter as each came up. Dates added to a term's worth of deadlines in forty minutes. Protection applied on the first evening to material that had been unprotected for four years, because looking at everything at once removed a rationalisation that had survived because nobody ever looked at everything at once.

The step both nearly missed is worth stating separately. After a month of unsystematic bulk addition, rebuilding the index took an evening and made the contents of everything searchable, including documents attached in a hurry and never opened. One practitioner did it out of deference rather than understanding and discovered a week later that forty safety documents he had treated as things to open when he remembered which one had become material whose contents participate. Nothing prompts anybody to do this and the benefit is invisible until somebody happens to search.

The advice both distilled points in the same direction. Design the top levels and the categories, which is a weekend. Import roughly into that shape. ## FAQs

### Why do migrations fail even when the content transfers?

Because organisation and content are different things, and only one of them moves easily. Text and files transfer without difficulty, while the structure expressing how somebody thought about their work frequently flattens, categories arrive attached to nothing, and documents separate from the notes explaining them. Every item survives and the relationships do not, producing an archive that is technically complete and practically unusable, which is worse than an obvious failure because it looks like success. Anybody planning a move should itemise what their organisation actually expresses before touching anything, since it is much easier to describe while it still exists. Practitioners in unrelated professions arrive at the same conclusion independently, which suggests it follows from the shape of migration rather than from any particular kind of work.

### Why is that failure hard to detect?

Because the person who ran the migration still remembers what everything is, so a flattened arrangement remains navigable for them through memory alone. The loss becomes visible months later, when the memory has faded or somebody else needs the material, and by then the original structure is gone and reconstructing it means recalling decisions made across several years. Practitioners describe discovering this well after the point at which it could have been prevented. Asking somebody else to find three things in the new arrangement is a quick and uncomfortable test that reveals whether the structure or the memory is doing the work. It is one of the decisions worth making deliberately at the outset, since correcting it later means undoing work rather than simply doing different work.

### Should somebody reproduce their old structure in the new system?

No, and practitioners who tried describe it as the mistake that cost them the most time. A structure was designed for the constraints of the system it lived in, and reproducing it imports those constraints along with the content. One practitioner spent a fortnight faithfully preserving a flat, densely linked arrangement before concluding that the flatness was a property of the old tool rather than a property of her work. Practitioners who resisted the instinct describe saving several weeks, and those who did not describe the weeks as entirely wasted. Anybody uncertain can test the question on a small sample before committing to anything wider.

### What happens when a flat structure is moved into a hierarchy?

The connections lose most of their meaning. In a flat system, a link between two items is the only structural information available and therefore carries a great deal. In a hierarchy, most of that relatedness is expressed by position: two documents about the same matter are related because both sit under the matter. Practitioners describe hundreds of links becoming redundant, which is not obvious until somebody looks at them all at once. Reviewing a sample of links after importing, rather than migrating all of them, is a quick way to establish how much of the connective work the hierarchy has absorbed. The effect is only visible in retrospect, which is why it is worth hearing from somebody who has already been through it.

### How deep should a page hierarchy go?

Three levels, according to practitioners in unrelated professions who arrived at it independently and who both tried four. Beyond three, somebody can no longer see a whole unit at once, whether that unit is a legal matter or a teaching unit, and every additional level is something that must be opened, which is a decision, and decisions accumulate. The general statement is that a hierarchy stops helping and starts costing somewhere around the fourth level. Trying a fourth level and abandoning it is common enough that anybody tempted should simply expect to do so. Practitioners in unrelated professions arrive at the same conclusion independently, which suggests it follows from the shape of migration rather than from any particular kind of work.

### How many categories should survive a migration?

Considerably fewer than existed before. One practitioner reduced sixty tags to nine, and describes almost all of the discarded ones as things the new structure now expresses: a tag per client became a page per client, and tags for years became dates on entries. The surviving categories were the ones cutting across the structure, principally document types. Anything a hierarchy already says should not also be a category. The reduction feels drastic at the time and practitioners consistently describe never missing what they dropped. It is one of the decisions worth making deliberately at the outset, since correcting it later means undoing work rather than simply doing different work.

### How does somebody decide which categories to drop?

### Is bulk migration or gradual migration better?

Gradual, and practitioners are emphatic about it. ### Is a migration ever not worth doing?

### What does a successful migration look like a year later?

An arrangement nobody thinks about. ### What is the first practical step for somebody who has decided to move?

Copy the source material twice before touching anything, then spend one sitting writing down the top two levels of structure and the handful of categories that survive scrutiny. That is the whole of the preparation, and it takes an afternoon. Everything after it is import, work, and gradual refinement, and practitioners who did exactly this describe the remainder as ordinary effort rather than as a project requiring any particular resolve. The temptation at this point is to keep planning, since planning is comfortable and importing is not. Practitioners describe stopping the design deliberately once the top levels exist, on the grounds that every further decision is better made with a fortnight of real use behind it than with none.


### Does a migration change how somebody thinks about their own work?

More than they expect. Designing a structure requires naming the units somebody actually works in, which most people have never been asked to do explicitly.
