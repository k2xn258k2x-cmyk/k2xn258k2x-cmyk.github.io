There is a reliable way to discover whether somebody understands a tool, and it is not asking them to describe it. Ask them to teach it to a person who does not want to learn. Explanation is easy and can be performed entirely from memory of a feature list. Teaching requires a sequence, a sequence requires deciding what comes first, and deciding what comes first requires knowing which capability makes the others make sense.

The characteristic failure of experienced users is not ignorance but generosity. Somebody who has spent two years discovering useful things wants to convey all of them, because each one was a small pleasure and withholding any feels like withholding help. What the recipient experiences is a wall. A person shown fifteen capabilities has learned nothing, since learning requires doing something, and doing something requires knowing which single action to take next.

![Teaching Someone Who Thinks They Cannot Learn](/assets/img/feature-screenshot-placeholder.svg)

The correction is unintuitive and difficult to sustain, which is that the most valuable thing a teacher does is omit. Three capabilities, chosen so that the recipient can produce something real on the first evening, and nothing else until that has become routine. Everything held back remains available; nothing held back is lost. What is lost by showing everything is the recipient's willingness to try, and that cannot be recovered.
<!--more-->


Which three depends entirely on the person and their situation, and this is where teaching stops being about the tool. Somebody whose material is unremarkable needs the shortest path from nothing to a first result: create, write, keep. Somebody whose material carries obligations to other people needs something different first, because they cannot responsibly put a single real record into anything until they understand where it goes and who can reach it.

That divergence produces two teaching sequences that look almost unrelated and share an underlying logic. Each begins with whatever must be true before the learner can proceed in good conscience, which for one person is that the thing works at all and for another is that the material is safe. Both sequences are three items long. Both defer everything else. The difference is entirely in what the first evening must establish.

There is a second thing teaching produces that nobody anticipates, and it is arguably the greater benefit. Articulating a practice requires stating rules that have been operating silently for years, and the moment a rule is said out loud its holder can hear whether it is any good. Learners also ask questions with no regard for what is conventional, which reliably exposes the places where an experienced person has been doing something out of habit rather than reason.

That is why the best teachers of any tool are rarely the most technically capable. Somebody who found a thing difficult and got past it remembers the shape of the difficulty. Somebody to whom it was always obvious has no access to the beginner's position and will, with complete good intentions, begin at the wrong place and continue for forty minutes.

What follows are two teaching stories running in parallel, one in a delicatessen after closing and one in a consulting room after hours, and a conversation between the teachers about what they discovered.


## Amir and Giorgio, on a Tuesday

Amir posted about it afterward, because the group had been discussing something adjacent.

He wrote: I have spent two evenings teaching Giorgio, who runs the deli two doors down, and I want to describe it because I got something right by accident and I only understood why afterward.

Giorgio is more resistant to this than I was, and I was the most resistant person in this group. He is fifty-eight, he has run that shop for twenty-two years, and his recipes are in a notebook with a rubber band round it.

I asked him what would make it worth doing. He said nothing would, and then he said that his mother's ossobuco recipe is on one page of that notebook and the page is coming loose.

That was the whole reason. I did not need to persuade him of anything after that. He had already persuaded himself and needed somebody to remove the obstacle.

We sat in the deli after closing with the shutters down and a laptop his daughter had bought him. First problem, immediately: where is the application. He wanted to know what to install.

I told him there is nothing to install and that this is one file, and he did not believe me, so I showed him the file, and he looked at it for a moment and said that a program is not a file.

I had no answer to that and I did not need one, because we opened it and it worked, and after four minutes he stopped asking.

Then I did the thing I want to describe. I showed him three things and nothing else.

One: the button that makes a new entry. Two: type a title and then type the recipe in the box underneath. Three: it saves by itself and there is nothing to remember.

That is all. That is everything I showed him on the first evening.

He made one entry. Mama's Ossobuco, typed slowly, with the notebook open beside him. It took him about eleven minutes because he types with two fingers and because he was checking every line against the page.

And when he finished he sat back and looked at it and said something I am going to remember. He said that it looks like the recipe.

Which I understood immediately and which somebody more technical might not. He meant that it did not look like a computer thing. It looked like the recipe, on a screen, with the ingredients in a list, and that was the moment he stopped being suspicious.

I wanted to show him labels. I wanted to show him how to attach a photograph of the notebook page. I wanted to show him about eight things and I said none of them.

I said use it for a week, just recipes, and then I will show you more.

He asked what else there was and I said it did not matter yet.

## Meera and Sarah, after hours

Meera posted the following day.

She wrote: I have been doing the same thing with a colleague and my three were completely different, which is what makes Amir's post interesting to me.

Sarah runs a practice about a mile from mine. She asked me about this at a meeting after I mentioned it in passing, and her question was not whether it was good. It was whether it was safe.

Which is the correct first question for anybody in our position and it determines everything about how you teach somebody like her.

We sat in my consulting room after my last appointment. She had brought a laptop and a list of questions, which tells you something about her.

I showed her three things and, like Amir, nothing else. But mine were not create, write, save.

One: connecting to a folder, and understanding that the folder is on her machine and that nothing goes anywhere. I made her look at the folder in her own file browser so that she saw the files with her own eyes.

Two: protecting an entry with a password, which we did on a test entry containing the word test, because I was not letting her type a patient's name into anything before this step.

Three: locking the screen, which takes an instant and which she practised four times.

Only after those three did we create anything real.

I want to explain why, because it looks like an odd order and it is the only responsible one for our profession.

Sarah has an obligation to about three hundred people who are not in the room. Before she writes one word about any of them, she needs to know three things: where the material physically is, that she can protect it, and that she can make it disappear from a screen when somebody walks in.

If I had shown her the editor first she would have written something, and she would have been uncomfortable the entire time, and she would have been right to be.

## Amir and Giorgio, week two

He wrote: fifteen recipes. He has done fifteen recipes in a week, in the evenings, and he has been doing it instead of watching television, which is not a sentence I expected to write about Giorgio.

## Meera and Sarah, week two

Meera's second post was longer and more technical.

She wrote: Sarah has three patient entries. Three, in a week, which is the correct number, because she is testing rather than committing.

I told her to keep working her existing way in parallel and to duplicate three people into this, deliberately, and to see whether it held up. Which she did and which is why she now trusts it.

The second session was three things again.

First, sections. Which for a clinical record is the whole of the structure, and she now has four in each: observations, treatment, administrative, and correspondence.

She asked me why four and I told her that mine are four and that the number is not the point. The point is that the same four appear every time, so that an empty one is a question rather than an absence.

She understood that immediately, because she trained in an environment where a blank field on a form means something.

Second, the password behaviour. Which is the thing I got wrong when I started and which I made sure she understood on the first day rather than the hundredth.

You enter it once and then you work inside the entry freely for a period. It does not ask you again every time you touch something, which people assume and which would make the whole arrangement unusable for a clinical session.

She was visibly relieved. She had been imagining entering a password twelve times per appointment.

Third, scheduling, which for both of us is the shape of a working day. Her appointments, her week, in front of her.

And then she asked the question I had been waiting for, which was whether she can search across her patient records.

This is the important nuance and I want to state it precisely, because I would rather anybody reading this learned it from me than discovered it in a difficult moment.

A protected entry is not searchable while it is protected. Which is the entire point of protecting it and is also a genuine consequence.

So the searching happens across everything that is not protected, and protected material is opened deliberately, by name, by somebody who knows which person they are looking for.

Sarah thought about it for a while and said that this is how clinical records should work anyway.

Which I had not put that way and which is right. I do not want to be able to search across three hundred protected records for a symptom. That is a research activity performed on identifiable material and it is not what a clinician should be doing casually on a Tuesday.

**Suki:** Stating the search consequence on day two rather than day two hundred is exactly right. People discover that at a bad moment otherwise.

## The one who did not continue

Fourth, and this is the one that took me longest to see, I never asked her what she needed. I assumed she needed what I needed, which is a portfolio view across thirty-one properties, and she does not. She needs to know what maintenance is outstanding on the eleven buildings she covers.

## Sequences, not tours

The most revealing thing about how somebody understands a tool is what they leave out when teaching it. Explanation can be performed from a feature list. Teaching requires a sequence, and a sequence requires knowing which capability makes the others make sense.

Both practitioners here showed exactly three things on a first evening, and their three had almost nothing in common. One showed how to create something, how to type into it, and that it keeps itself, on the reasoning that his learner's material concerns nobody but himself and the only obstacle was doubt that any of it would work. The other showed where the material physically sits, how to protect it, and how to clear a screen instantly, on the reasoning that her learner has obligations to three hundred people and could not responsibly type a single name until those three were established.

The principle underneath was stated in the thread and holds across both: the first three are whatever must be true before the person can proceed in good conscience. For an unregulated hobbyist that is simply that the thing works. For anybody holding other people's information it is that the material is safe. Neither ordering is correct in general and both are correct for their situation.

A third position emerged from other participants and refines it further. Start with whatever the person's actual complaint is, within the first ninety seconds. Somebody who cannot find their own material should be shown search immediately and will be persuaded in seconds. Somebody responsible for outstanding maintenance should be shown a deadline list. The tool's own logical order is irrelevant, because nobody wants a feature tour and everybody wants their specific difficulty removed.

The failure described at the end of the thread is worth as much as the successes. Two hours, the teacher's material on the teacher's screen, beginning with a structure that took two years to earn, and no question asked about what the learner needed. Four errors, each individually reasonable, producing somebody who left knowing the teacher was good at something and never opened it again. The corrections are equally simple: twenty minutes, the learner's machine, one question first, and whatever they answer addressed before anything else is shown.

The second session in both stories was again three things, chosen against what the learner had actually done rather than against any curriculum. Categories drawn from the learner's own vocabulary rather than proposed. A starred set of the six things somebody makes every morning. Search, demonstrated on a word the learner chose. Structure within records, a password behaviour that would otherwise have been misunderstood into unusability, and an honest statement of a real constraint on day nine rather than at a bad moment months later.

That last one deserves separate mention because it inverts an expectation. Protected material is not searchable while protected, which is the entire purpose of protecting it. Stating that plainly during teaching prevents somebody discovering it during a difficult afternoon, and the learner's response was better than the teacher's framing: that this is how clinical records should behave anyway, since casually searching hundreds of identifiable records for a symptom is a research activity rather than an ordinary Tuesday.

What neither teacher anticipated is the return. Both discovered a substantial gap in their own practice through a beginner's question, because a beginner asks without regard for what is conventional. One had spent two years and nine hundred entries typing structured material into a single undifferentiated block, and was asked how to keep two things separate. The other had four hundred well-maintained individual records and no categorisation across any of them, and was asked what her scheme was by somebody who assumed she had one.

Both gaps were invisible from inside, because every individual item was complete. The mechanism is straightforward and applies to any practice: articulating something requires stating rules that have been operating silently, and a rule said out loud can be heard. Several participants concluded that teaching is worth doing for that reason alone, independent of whether the learner ever benefits.

The tool's role in all of this is a single property, which is that its capabilities layer rather than interlock. Somebody can create, type, and keep, and that is a complete and useful practice. Categories can be added later without disturbing it. Structure, protection, scheduling, and search can each be added later without disturbing anything that came before. That is what makes a three-item first evening possible, and it is what allows somebody fifty-eight years old with a notebook held together by a rubber band to have fifteen recipes by the following week and to have seen his own catering menu for the first time in eleven years.


## FAQs

### Why is teaching a better test of understanding than explaining?

Because explanation can be produced from a feature list while teaching requires a sequence. Deciding what comes first means knowing which capability makes the others comprehensible, which is a different and considerably harder question. Practitioners describe discovering gaps in their own understanding at exactly this point, since a sequence forces a person to state what depends on what. Practitioners in unrelated professions arrive at the same conclusion independently, which suggests it follows from how people adopt anything rather than from any particular tool. It is one of the judgments best made early, since the alternative costs a relationship as well as an adoption. Anybody teaching somebody they know personally should be particularly careful here, since the stakes include something other than the software.
