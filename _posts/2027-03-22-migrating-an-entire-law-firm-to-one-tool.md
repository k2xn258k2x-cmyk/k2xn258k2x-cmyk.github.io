An individual changing how they keep their material is making a personal decision with a personal cost. If it goes badly they lose some weeks and revert. An organisation making the same change is doing something categorically different, because the decision binds people who did not make it, the failure mode is collective rather than personal, and reverting is considerably more expensive than proceeding badly.

That difference produces a set of questions nobody asks about their own arrangement. Whether something works for one person is answerable by trying it. Whether it works for thirty requires answering how it behaves when people with entirely different levels of interest and capability are obliged to use it, which is not a question about the tool at all.

![Migrating an Entire Law Firm to One Tool](/assets/img/feature-screenshot-placeholder.svg)

There is a further consideration in professions where the material carries legal weight. A firm holding privileged material has obligations that do not scale down to individuals and cannot be satisfied by good intentions. Who can reach what must be answerable precisely. The answer must survive somebody asking it adversarially. And it must hold when a person leaves, when a matter concludes, and when a court takes an interest.
<!--more-->


What most organisations actually have, before anybody proposes anything, is not a system but an accumulation. Some people use one tool because they liked it, some use another because a previous firm did, documents live on a shared drive nobody has audited, and a substantial proportion of the institutional memory exists only in email. Nobody chose this. It assembled itself, one reasonable individual decision at a time, and it produces a specific and expensive failure: when somebody leaves, what they knew leaves with them.

The argument for consolidation therefore has to be made against a status quo that nobody defends and everybody tolerates. That is harder than it sounds, because tolerating something has no cost anybody can point at, while changing it has a visible one. The person proposing a migration is asking colleagues to accept a certain disruption in exchange for avoiding an uncertain loss.

What makes such a proposal succeed is rarely a feature comparison. It is whether the person proposing it can demonstrate the outcome rather than describe it, whether the architecture answers the legal questions in a way that survives scrutiny, and whether the plan for getting thirty people to a new arrangement acknowledges that most of them do not care about tools and should not be asked to.

What follows is a conversation in a conference room, between somebody who has been running a proposal privately for four years and somebody whose job is to be unpersuaded until the argument is complete.


## The proposal

Richard Hayes had allocated an hour and had told his assistant to protect it, which Claire recognised as either a courtesy or a warning.

Kevin was there because Claire had asked him to be, and had spent the first five minutes visibly deciding how much to say.

‘I want to start with what we have rather than with what I am proposing,’ Claire said.

‘Go on.’

‘Eleven attorneys and about thirty people in total, using four different arrangements for keeping what we know.’ She counted. ‘Two of us on one tool. Four on another. Documents on a shared drive. And the substantial majority of everything anybody actually knows, in email.’

‘Which is not a system.’

‘Which is not a system and nobody chose it.’ Claire was matter of fact. ‘It assembled itself over about a decade, one reasonable decision at a time.’

Richard nodded slowly.

‘Say what is actually wrong with it.’

‘One thing, and it is not convenience.’ She was precise. ‘When somebody leaves, what they knew leaves.’

There was a pause.

‘Say more.’

‘Peter retired eighteen months ago.’ Claire counted. ‘Forty years of prosecution experience. And what we have of it is the file wrapper on every matter he ever handled, which is the formal record and which is not what he knew.’

‘Which is a real loss.’

‘Which is a loss we have absorbed four times in a decade and treated as inevitable.’ She was definite. ‘And I want to argue that it is not inevitable, and that what makes it inevitable is that his knowledge lived in a place nobody else could reach.’

Richard put his pen down.

‘Say the second problem, because I assume there is one.’

‘Retrieval, which affects us weekly rather than every few years.’ Claire counted. ‘A client asks what position we took on something in a matter that concluded four years ago. Which is a reasonable question.’

‘And how long does it take?’

‘Kevin, say how long.’

Kevin had clearly been waiting.

‘Between forty minutes and half a day,’ he said. ‘Depending on which attorney handled it and where they kept things.’

‘Which is billed to whom?’ Richard asked.

‘Which is generally not billed at all, because a client will not pay for us to find our own material.’ Claire was matter of fact. ‘Which makes it a cost we absorb and do not measure.’


## What she is actually proposing

‘Say what you want to do,’ Richard said.

‘Each attorney has their own arrangement for privileged work, on their own machine, in a folder they control.’ Claire counted. ‘And there is one shared arrangement for everything that belongs to the firm rather than to a matter.’

‘Which is the same thing you described about your own practice.’

‘Which is what I have been running for four years, and this is the part I want to be clear about.’ She turned her laptop round. ‘I am not proposing something I have read about.’

Richard looked at it for a while.

‘Say what I am looking at.’

‘Eighty-one active matters and about four thousand documents.’ Claire was matter of fact. ‘Organised by practice area at the top, clients beneath, matters beneath that.’

‘Show me it doing something.’

‘Give me a phrase from a claim. Anything.’

Richard thought, and said one.

Claire typed it. Results appeared.

‘Four,’ she said. ‘And this one is inside an attached document from a matter that concluded three years ago, which I have not opened since.’

Richard leaned forward.

‘Say that again.’

‘The phrase is inside a document. Not in anything I wrote about the document.’ She scrolled to it. ‘Which is the property that changes what a retrieval question costs.’

‘From half a day.’

‘From half a day to four seconds, and I want to be careful not to overstate that.’ Claire was precise. ‘It found this because I attached the document rather than leaving it on a drive. The capability does nothing without the practice.’

‘Which is a change in behaviour rather than a change in software.’

‘Which is exactly right and it is why I have brought a rollout plan rather than a recommendation.’

Kevin spoke for the first time in several minutes.

‘Can I show one thing?’

‘Go on,’ Richard said.

‘Four of us are on a tool that keeps everything as plain files in folders,’ Kevin said. ‘Which is a considerable amount of material.’

‘And?’

‘And it comes across.’ He demonstrated. ‘Structure, links, everything. Which took about eleven minutes for my own material and which is the reason I stopped objecting to this proposal.’

Richard looked at him.

‘You objected?’

‘For about a month,’ Kevin said. ‘I had four years in that tool and I assumed I would lose it.’


## The questions that matter

‘Now the part I actually care about,’ Richard said.

‘Privilege.’

‘Privilege, and I want you to answer as though I am opposing counsel rather than your managing partner.’

Claire had clearly been expecting this.

‘Ask them in order.’

‘Attorney A's privileged analysis. Can attorney B read it?’

‘No, and not because of a permission.’ She was precise. ‘Attorney A's material is in a folder on attorney A's machine. Attorney B is not in a position to read it because there is no relationship between the two arrangements.’

‘Say what happens if attorney B is at attorney A's machine.’

‘Then attorney B is at a machine belonging to another attorney, which is a physical access question that every firm already answers.’ Claire counted. ‘And the privileged entries are additionally protected with a password only attorney A holds.’

‘Two layers.’

‘Structural separation and protection, which address different failures.’ She was definite. ‘The separation means the material is not there. The protection means it is unreadable if somebody is.’

Richard wrote something.

‘Say the technical detail, because somebody will ask me and I will need to repeat it.’

‘Established encryption, with a key derived from the password through a deliberately slow process so that guessing is impractical.’ Claire was matter of fact. ‘I can give you the specifics in writing and I would rather you had them from me than from a brochure.’

‘Do that. Next question. Shared matters.’

‘Which is the genuine one and I want to answer it honestly.’ She counted. ‘Three attorneys on one matter. Each holds their own privileged analysis in their own arrangement. The coordination happens in the shared one.’

‘Say what coordination means.’

‘Deadlines. Who is doing what. Filed documents, which are public. Correspondence with the client, which is privileged and which sits with whoever holds the relationship.’ Claire was precise. ‘Which is how three attorneys already work. I am describing what happens now rather than proposing a change.’

‘That is a better answer than I expected.’

‘Because the arrangement follows the professional structure rather than replacing it,’ she said. ‘Three attorneys on a matter do not have a shared brain now. They have a shared file and separate analysis.’

Richard turned a page.

‘Compelled production,’ he said.

The room went slightly quieter.

‘Say the scenario properly,’ Claire said.

‘Somebody seeks an attorney's working notes. What happens.’

‘The same thing that happens today, which is that the firm responds through counsel and asserts whatever privilege applies.’ She was careful. ‘I want to be precise here, because this is the area where people say foolish things.’

‘Go on.’

‘The arrangement does not create a privilege or defeat one. What it does is remove a party.’ Claire counted. ‘There is no service holding our material. Nobody can be served with a demand for it except us, because nobody else has it.’

‘Which is not the same as it being unreachable.’

‘Which is emphatically not the same, and any argument that pretends otherwise is one I would not make.’ She was definite. ‘What it means is that the material is subject to the same process as a filing cabinet, which is a well-understood position that this firm has known how to handle for sixty years.’

Richard sat back.

‘That is the answer I wanted and I was not sure I would get it.’

‘Say why.’

‘Because everybody who has presented me a tool in ten years has told me it makes a legal problem go away,’ he said. ‘And none of them ever has.’


## Kevin's half hour

‘Show me the rest of it,’ Richard said. ‘Because Claire has made an argument and I would like to see the thing.’

Kevin had prepared four demonstrations and got through three.

‘Documents first,’ he said. ‘Because that is most of what we hold.’

‘Say the volume.’

‘I do not know the firm's number and mine is about eleven hundred.’ He was matter of fact. ‘Prosecution histories, prior art, correspondence, and filings.’

‘And they are searchable.’

‘Their contents are searchable, which is different.’ Kevin demonstrated. ‘Watch. A term from inside a filing I received four months ago and have read once.’

The result appeared with the passage.

‘Say what our current arrangement does with that,’ Richard said.

‘Finds the filename if the filename contains the term.’ Kevin shrugged. ‘Which it does not, because the filename is a docket number.’

‘Which is the actual problem with our shared drive.’

‘Which is the whole problem with our shared drive and everybody has adapted to it by remembering where things are.’ He was matter of fact. ‘Which works until the person who remembers leaves.’

Claire said something quietly.

‘Which is the same point I made about Peter, arriving from a different direction.’

Kevin moved on.

‘Second thing, which is small and which I use constantly.’ He opened something. ‘Assembling a filing package.’

‘Say what that involves now.’

‘Four documents from different places, combined in the right order, and occasionally a document split because only part of it belongs.’ Kevin counted. ‘Which currently means a separate application and a folder of intermediate files.’

‘And here?’

‘Here, and the result attaches to the matter it belongs to rather than sitting in a downloads folder.’ He shrugged. ‘Which sounds trivial and which saves me perhaps forty minutes a week.’

Richard looked at Claire.

‘Is that number real?’

‘His is. Mine is smaller because I do fewer filings and more analysis,’ she said. ‘And I would not build a proposal on forty minutes a week. I mention it because it is the kind of thing that makes people stop resisting.’

Kevin's third demonstration was the migration.

‘Four attorneys are on a tool that keeps plain files,’ he said. ‘And this brings them across with the structure intact.’

‘Say what does not come across.’

Kevin paused, which Claire noted approvingly.

‘Anything that was specific to how that tool displayed things,’ he said. ‘The material comes across. Some of the arrangement is mine to rebuild.’

‘How long did yours take?’

‘Eleven minutes to import and about two evenings to reorganise, and the second part was work I had been meaning to do anyway.’

Richard made a note.

‘I appreciate that you did not tell me it was seamless,’ he said.


## What it costs

‘Money,’ Richard said. ‘Which I have deliberately left until now.’

‘Which I have prepared and which is the least interesting part of the argument,’ Claire said.

‘Say the number anyway.’

‘We currently pay per person for two separate tools, plus storage for the shared drive, plus a support arrangement with an outside provider for the shared drive.’ She counted. ‘Which I have totalled and which is on the second page.’

Richard looked at it.

‘That is more than I thought.’

‘Which is what everybody says, because it is four separate line items that nobody has ever added together.’ Claire was matter of fact. ‘And the total is not the argument. The structure is.’

‘Say what you mean.’

‘Every one of those is a recurring arrangement with an outside party, which continues for as long as we continue and stops when they decide it does.’ She was precise. ‘Which is a dependency rather than an expense.’

‘And this is not.’

‘This is a licence held in each attorney's folder, verified locally, with nothing contacted.’ Claire counted. ‘Which means there is no arrangement that can lapse, no service that can change its terms, and nothing that stops working because somebody else made a decision.’

Richard was quiet for a moment.

‘Say what that means practically.’

‘That if this firm dissolved tomorrow, every attorney would walk out with their matters in a folder, readable, without asking anybody.’ She was definite. ‘Which given what happened to Harrow and Bell is not a hypothetical concern.’

‘That is a fair point and an uncomfortable one.’

Kevin added something.

‘There is a cost that is not on the page,’ he said.

‘Say it.’

‘Infrastructure, which we would not have.’ He counted. ‘No server. No database. No backup regime beyond what each machine already does. No update process to manage.’

‘Which is currently what?’

‘Currently about a day a month of somebody's time, and I know because it is frequently mine.’ Kevin shrugged. ‘Which is not in any budget because I am not billed as infrastructure.’

Richard laughed shortly.

‘Which is how every firm underestimates what its systems cost,’ he said.


## Getting thirty people to do anything

‘Now the part I actually doubt,’ Richard said. ‘Which is not the tool.’

‘Adoption.’

‘Adoption. Because I have watched this firm adopt three things in a decade and two of them failed.’ He was matter of fact. ‘And in both cases the tool was fine.’

‘Say what went wrong.’

‘Everybody was asked to change at once, and the people who did not care did not do it, and within a quarter we had two systems and were worse off than before.’

Claire nodded.

‘Which is exactly what I am planning to avoid and I want to describe how.’

‘Go on.’

‘Nobody adopts anything at once.’ She counted. ‘Five attorneys first, all in one practice area, all of whom I work with weekly.’

‘Why one practice area?’

‘Because they share matters, which means the shared arrangement gets exercised properly rather than theoretically.’ Claire was precise. ‘Five people in five practice areas would each be using it alone, which tests nothing.’

‘And how long?’

‘A quarter, and I want it evaluated honestly rather than defended.’ She was definite. ‘If it does not work I would rather know in three months with five people than in a year with thirty.’

Richard wrote that down.

‘Say the second phase.’

‘Everybody else, over about six months, in groups of four or five.’ Claire counted. ‘And support staff last rather than first, which is the opposite of how it is usually done.’

‘Say why.’

‘Because support staff will use whatever the attorneys use, and asking them to adopt something the attorneys have not is asking them to maintain two arrangements.’ She shrugged. ‘Which is how you lose the people you most need.’

‘And the historical material?’

‘Third phase and possibly never, and I want to be honest about that.’ Claire was matter of fact. ‘Six thousand concluded matters is not something anybody migrates.’

Richard looked up.

‘Then what happens to it?’

‘It stays where it is and remains reachable.’ She counted. ‘And anything anybody actually consults gets brought across when they consult it, which over two years covers everything that matters and nothing that does not.’

‘Which is not a migration plan.’

‘Which is a deliberate decision not to migrate ninety percent of something nobody looks at,’ Claire said. ‘And I would rather say that now than discover it in month eight of a project.’


## The conventions

‘There is a part of this that worries me more than the technology,’ Claire said.

‘Say it.’

‘That thirty people will produce thirty arrangements, and in four years we will be exactly where we are.’ She was frank. ‘Which is the actual failure mode and it is not a tool problem.’

‘Then what stops it?’

‘Three conventions, agreed before anybody starts, and nothing else.’ Claire counted. ‘Structure, vocabulary, and naming.’

‘Say them.’

‘Practice area, then client, then matter.’ She showed the arrangement. ‘Which is how the firm already thinks and which is therefore not a change to anything except where things are stored.’

‘Which is one convention.’

‘Second is a small shared vocabulary for describing things.’ Claire was precise. ‘Document type, stage, and priority. Eleven descriptions in three families.’

‘Eleven for thirty people.’

‘Eleven, deliberately, because a vocabulary that thirty people must apply consistently has to be small enough that nobody has to think.’ She was definite. ‘Mine has more and mine is mine. A firm's has to be the minimum.’

‘And naming.’

‘Client code, then date, then a few words about what the thing actually is.’ Claire counted. ‘Which is the one I care about most and which is the one people resist.’

‘Say why they resist.’

‘Because everybody has their own way and everybody's own way is fine for them.’ She shrugged. ‘And it is fine for them and it is useless to the next person, and the next person is why we are doing any of this.’

Richard considered that.

‘Anything else agreed?’

‘Nothing.’ Claire was emphatic. ‘Which is the part I want to be firm about. Three conventions and everything else is each attorney's own business.’

‘Say why so few.’

‘Because I have watched firms specify everything and produce documents nobody follows.’ She was matter of fact. ‘Three things people can remember beats thirty things in a manual, every time, and the three I have chosen are the three that determine whether somebody else can find your work.’

Kevin said something.

‘Can I add one observation?’

‘Go on,’ Richard said.

‘Claire's conventions are all about the next person,’ Kevin said. ‘Not one of them makes anybody's own work easier.’

‘Which is deliberate,’ Claire said. ‘Because the things that make your own work easier are things you will do anyway. The things that make somebody else's work possible are the ones that need to be agreed.’


## The objection Kevin raised in the corridor

Richard left first. Kevin waited until the door had closed.

‘Can I say something that I did not want to say in front of him?’

‘Please.’

‘I think the pilot will succeed and I think the rollout will fail, and I want to tell you why now rather than in a year.’

Claire put her pen down.

‘Say it.’

‘Because your five are five people who work with you.’ Kevin was careful. ‘Who will ask you when they are stuck, and who will do it properly because you are watching.’

‘Which is the point of a pilot.’

‘Which is the point of a pilot and it is also why a pilot tells you almost nothing about a rollout.’ He was frank. ‘The second cohort will not have you. They will have a document.’

Claire was quiet for a moment.

‘Say what you think happens.’

‘They do the parts that help them and skip the parts that help the next person.’ Kevin counted. ‘Structure, yes, because they need it. Descriptions, probably. Naming, no, because naming is entirely for somebody else and there is no immediate consequence for ignoring it.’

‘Which is the convention I said mattered most.’

‘Which is the convention you said mattered most and which has the weakest incentive attached to it,’ he said. ‘Which is not a coincidence.’

Claire wrote something down.

‘Say what you would do.’

‘Make the naming produce something visible.’ Kevin was thinking aloud. ‘Not enforcement. Something where a person who names things correctly gets a benefit within a fortnight rather than in three years.’

‘Say what that would be.’

‘I do not know yet and I think it exists.’ He shrugged. ‘Because when I started naming things your way, my own material became findable to me within about a month, and I had assumed the benefit was entirely for whoever came after.’

Claire looked up.

‘Say that again.’

‘The naming convention helped me,’ Kevin said. ‘Within a month. I only noticed because I went looking for something from before I changed and could not find it.’

‘Then that is what goes in the training.’ She was already writing. ‘Not that it helps the firm. That it helps you, in a month, and here is a colleague who will say so.’

‘Which is a considerably easier thing to persuade somebody of.’

‘Which is the only thing anybody is ever persuaded of,’ Claire said. ‘And I had been about to build a rollout on an argument about institutional memory, which nobody has ever changed a habit for.’


## What Claire did not put in the proposal

Richard closed the folder and did not say anything for a moment.

‘I am going to approve the pilot,’ he said. ‘And before I do I want to ask you something that is not in here.’

‘Go on.’

‘Why do you actually want this?’

Claire took slightly too long to answer, which she was aware of.

‘Say what you are asking.’

‘You have been running this arrangement privately for four years and it works.’ Richard was matter of fact. ‘Nothing about a firm-wide rollout improves your own situation. It creates work for you and gives you responsibility for something that can fail publicly.’

‘Which is all true.’

‘So say why.’

Claire looked at the table.

‘Because I am fifty-eight,’ she said.

Richard waited.

‘Which is not an answer,’ she went on. ‘So let me give you the answer underneath it. I have twelve years of accumulated judgement about how this firm handles a particular kind of matter, and almost none of it exists anywhere except in what I have built.’

‘Which is more than Peter left.’

‘Which is considerably more than Peter left and it is still a folder on one machine belonging to one person who will eventually not be here.’ She was frank. ‘And I have been thinking about that for about a year.’

‘So this is about succession.’

‘This is about whether what any of us knows is a firm asset or a personal one,’ Claire said. ‘And at the moment it is personal, in every case, including mine, and I built a very good personal system and it does not solve that problem at all.’

Kevin was looking at her with an expression she chose not to examine.

‘Say what a firm-wide version changes,’ Richard said.

‘Conventions.’ She counted. ‘If thirty people structure things the same way and describe things the same way and name things the same way, then any one of us leaving is a considerably smaller loss, because what we left is navigable by somebody else.’

‘Which is the actual argument.’

‘Which is the actual argument and I did not lead with it because it sounds like I am arranging my own departure.’ Claire was matter of fact. ‘Which I am not, and which I would like to have arranged before I do.’

Richard was quiet.

‘That is a considerably better reason than the retrieval times,’ he said.

‘The retrieval times are real and they are what makes people agree to it,’ she said. ‘The other thing is why it is worth doing.’


## Ownership rather than tooling

A firm-wide migration is not a technology decision with an organisational component. It is an organisational decision with a technology component, and treating it the other way round is the reliable route to a project that concludes with two systems and no improvement.

What such a proposal has to argue against is not a competing tool but an accumulation nobody chose and everybody tolerates. Several arrangements in parallel, documents on a drive nobody has audited, and a substantial proportion of institutional memory in email. That situation produces one expensive failure and one persistent one: when somebody leaves their knowledge leaves, and any historical question costs somebody an unbillable half day.

The legal questions have to be answered precisely and without overstatement, which is where most proposals fail. Separation between practitioners is structural, meaning each holds their own material and there is no relationship between the arrangements, with protection as an additional layer addressing a different failure. Shared matters coordinate in a common area and keep analysis individual, which describes how several professionals already work rather than proposing a change.

On compelled production, the practitioner presenting the proposal declined to claim that anything makes a legal problem disappear, which her managing partner identified as the reason he believed the rest of it. What the arrangement changes is that no external party holds the material and therefore no external party can be compelled to produce it. The material remains subject to the same process as a filing cabinet, which is a position the profession has understood for a very long time.

The demonstrations that persuade are the ones that reveal something the current arrangement cannot do. A phrase found inside an attached document from a matter concluded years earlier, in seconds, is a different kind of argument from a feature list. So is a junior colleague admitting he objected for a month, assuming he would lose four years of accumulated material, and stopping when the import took eleven minutes.

Cost turns out to be the least interesting portion of the argument and the most revealing. Several subscriptions and a support arrangement, never totalled because they are separate line items, plus roughly a day a month of somebody's unbudgeted time maintaining infrastructure. The structural point matters more than the total: every current arrangement is a recurring dependency on an outside party that continues while they choose and stops when they decide, whereas a locally verified licence is not a relationship at all.

The adoption plan acknowledged what the technology cannot address. Five attorneys in one practice area rather than five across five, so that shared work is genuinely exercised. A quarter, evaluated honestly rather than defended. Groups of four or five thereafter, with support staff last rather than first, since staff will use whatever the professionals use and asking otherwise means asking them to maintain two arrangements.

The historical material was addressed by declining to migrate it. Six thousand concluded matters is not something anybody moves, and the honest plan is that material comes across when somebody consults it, which over a couple of years covers everything that matters and nothing that does not. Saying so in the proposal is considerably better than discovering it in month eight of a project.

Three conventions were specified and nothing else: structure, a small shared vocabulary, and naming. Eleven descriptions across three families for thirty people, which is deliberately fewer than any individual would use, on the reasoning that a vocabulary thirty people must apply consistently has to be small enough that nobody thinks about it. The junior colleague made the observation that gives the conventions their logic: not one of them makes anybody's own work easier. The things that help you are things you will do anyway. The things that make somebody else's work possible are the ones that must be agreed.

Which is the argument underneath the whole proposal, and the practitioner presenting it did not lead with it because it sounds like somebody arranging their own departure. Twelve years of accumulated judgement about how a firm handles a particular kind of matter, existing almost entirely in one person's material, on one machine, belonging to somebody who will eventually not be there. A very good personal arrangement does not solve that at all. Shared conventions do, because what somebody leaves behind becomes navigable by whoever comes next.

The retrieval times are what make people agree. The succession question is why it is worth doing.


## FAQs

### How is an organisational migration different from an individual one?

An individual decision has a personal cost and is reversible: somebody loses a few weeks and returns to what they had. An organisational decision binds people who did not make it, fails collectively, and is considerably more expensive to reverse than to continue badly. Practitioners describe the questions that arise as being about people rather than about the tool. Practitioners who have run organisational adoptions describe the same conclusion independently, which suggests it follows from how people behave rather than from any particular profession. It is one of the decisions best settled before a proposal is presented, since discovering it afterward undermines everything else in the argument. Practitioners recommend a rough count rather than an audit. ### What did the decision-maker actually approve?

A pilot rather than a rollout. Practitioners describe five professionals in one practice area migrating fully for a quarter, with a firm-wide decision following only if that succeeds. One notes that the proposer expected the pilot to succeed because she had effectively been running it alone for four years. Practitioners describe a pilot as the appropriate response to any proposal whose author is confident, however well founded that confidence is. A proposer who resents a pilot has not considered what happens if they are wrong, which practitioners describe as disqualifying in itself. Practitioners describe the demonstrable working arrangement as having done more than any argument in the document.
