Every organised collection of material eventually confronts the same problem, and it is a geometric one. A hierarchy places each thing in exactly one location, which is what makes a hierarchy navigable. But almost nothing in professional life belongs in exactly one place. A document concerns a client and a subject and a stage of work and a category of risk, and a structure that files it under the first of those has silently discarded the other three.

The usual response is to build the hierarchy more carefully, which does not work and cannot work. Choosing a deeper structure means choosing which single dimension takes precedence, and any choice is wrong for some proportion of the questions somebody will later ask. Filing by client makes questions about subject impossible. Filing by subject makes questions about client impossible. The material has more dimensions than the structure can express, and no amount of care resolves that.

![Labels as the Secret Architecture of Knowledge](/assets/img/feature-screenshot-placeholder.svg)

What resolves it is adding a second axis that runs perpendicular to the first. A location says where something lives; a category says what it is. Something can live in exactly one place while carrying as many descriptions as it deserves, and a query that would be impossible against the structure becomes trivial against the descriptions. The hierarchy answers where; the categories answer what connects to this across everything.
<!--more-->


That reframing sounds modest and produces a different kind of collection. Questions become askable that were previously unaskable: everything about one technique regardless of which project it served, everything at one stage of work regardless of which client, everything a person marked as important regardless of where it sits. Each of those cuts across the structure rather than descending through it, and each is a question professionals ask constantly and have simply learned not to expect answers to.

The reason people underuse the second axis is that it appears trivial. Adding a word to something is an operation of no evident sophistication, and the mental model most people carry is of a loose keyword rather than of a structural element. That model produces the characteristic failure: an accumulation of one-off words applied inconsistently, which describes nothing reliably and which nobody trusts enough to filter by.

The alternative is to treat the second axis as a designed vocabulary rather than as a growing pile. A small number of families, each answering a different question about a thing, applied consistently enough that their absence is informative. Somebody who knows that every item carries a stage marker can ask about stages. Somebody whose stage markers are applied to two thirds of things cannot, because the answer would be wrong in a way they could not detect.

There is a further consequence that people discover late and value disproportionately. A category applied to something is a statement about it, and statements accumulate into a description of the collection itself. Counting them reveals what somebody actually works on, which is frequently not what they believe they work on. The distribution is a mirror, and it is unusually honest because nobody was constructing it deliberately.

What follows is a conversation between two people whose material could hardly be more different, one holding scientific literature and one holding legal filings, who arrived at structurally identical vocabularies without ever having discussed it.


## Forty labels and eleven

Priya had suggested it after something in the group thread about naming.

‘I want to compare vocabularies,’ she said, ‘because I have a suspicion that mine and yours are the same shape and I cannot prove it.’

‘Say the suspicion.’

‘That we both started with a flat list of whatever occurred to us and both ended up with families.’

Claire laughed.

‘That is exactly what happened and I have never described it as families.’ She pulled hers up. ‘Say your count.’

‘Forty-one.’

‘Which sounds enormous.’

‘It sounds enormous and it is five families with between four and eight in each, which is a different object entirely.’ Priya shared. ‘Technique, organism, journal, chapter, and status.’

‘Say them properly.’

‘Technique is what was done: the editing method, the amplification method, the blotting method, and about six others.’ She counted. ‘Organism is what it was done in. Journal is where it was published. Chapter is which part of my thesis it serves. And status is what I have decided about it.’

‘And each entry carries one from several families.’

‘Most papers carry a technique, an organism, a journal, and a status, and about half carry a chapter.’ Priya shrugged. ‘Which is four or five on a typical entry, and none of them is redundant with any other.’

‘Mine is smaller and I think structurally identical,’ Claire said. ‘Nine that I use constantly and two I use rarely.’

‘Say the families.’

‘Matter type, stage, document type, and priority.’ She counted. ‘Type is what kind of protection is being sought. Stage is where in the process it sits. Document type is what a particular item is. And priority is a client judgment.’

Priya was nodding.

‘Four families to my five and the same logic.’

‘Say the logic, because I want to hear you state it.’

‘Each family answers a different question about the same object, and they are independent.’ Priya was precise. ‘Knowing the technique tells you nothing about the organism. Knowing your stage tells you nothing about the document type.’

‘Which is the test.’

‘Which is the test I arrived at after building it wrong twice, and it is the only rule that survived.’ She was definite. ‘If two families are correlated, they are one family and I have split something that did not want splitting.’

‘Give me a case.’

‘I had a family for journal and a family for prestige, and they were the same family expressed twice.’ Priya laughed. ‘Which took me four months to notice because I was applying both diligently.’

Claire described her own error.

‘Mine was granularity rather than correlation,’ she said. ‘I had eleven document types and I now have four.’

‘What happened to the other seven?’

‘They were distinctions that mattered to me while filing and to nobody while searching.’ She shrugged. ‘I could tell you the difference between three kinds of response and I have never once needed to filter by that difference, which is the definition of a distinction not worth maintaining.’

‘So the test is whether you would filter by it.’

‘The test is whether you have ever filtered by it, which is stricter and which is available because the answer is observable.’ Claire was matter of fact. ‘I looked at what I actually did and removed everything I had never used.’


## Reading by colour

‘There is a thing we both do and I want to know whether you do it deliberately,’ Priya said.

‘Colour.’

‘Colour, by family rather than by individual meaning.’

‘Which I do and which took me a year to arrive at.’ Claire shared a list. ‘Everything about stage is in one range. Everything about document type is in another.’

‘Say your ranges.’

‘Stage runs warm to cool by urgency.’ She counted. ‘Anything with a response deadline is red. Anything active is amber. Anything concluded is green.’

‘So the colour carries the meaning rather than identifying the label.’

‘The colour carries the meaning and that is the entire point, because I do not read the words.’ Claire was definite. ‘I scan a list of eighty matters and what I see is red, and I count four reds, and I know what my week is.’

‘Which is faster than reading.’

‘It is not comparable to reading. It is a different operation, and it takes about two seconds for something that would take forty.’

Priya described hers.

‘Mine is by family rather than by urgency, because nothing in my work is urgent in that sense.’ She counted. ‘Technique is blue. Organism is green. Chapter is purple. Status is red or orange.’

‘Why those two for status?’

‘Because status is the only family where I need to distinguish within it at a glance.’ Priya was precise. ‘Something marked as important is red. Something marked as unread is orange. And those two are the only distinctions I make dozens of times a day.’

‘So the family colouring has one exception.’

‘The family colouring has one deliberate exception and I would not have a second, because two exceptions is not a system.’ She shrugged. ‘The moment I could not state the rule in a sentence, the colours would stop meaning anything.’

Claire asked about the sidebar.

‘Do you use the distribution view?’

‘Constantly, and for a reason I did not expect.’ Priya pulled it up. ‘The pills in the sidebar show the whole vocabulary with counts, and what I look at is not the large ones.’

‘The small ones.’

‘The small ones, because a label with three entries on it is either a mistake or the beginning of something.’ She was definite. ‘I check it about once a month and I find one or two every time.’

‘Say what a mistake looks like.’

‘A label I created once, applied twice, and forgot about, which is now noise in the vocabulary.’ Priya shrugged. ‘I either commit to it and apply it properly or I remove it, and I remove about four times as often as I commit.’

‘Which keeps the vocabulary honest.’

‘Which is the only maintenance I do and it takes eleven minutes a month.’


## The suggestions

‘Show me something,’ Claire said. ‘Because you mentioned once that the suggestions have taught you things and I have barely used them.’

‘Then I will do it live rather than describing it.’ Priya created an entry and pasted in an abstract. ‘Watch what appears.’

There was a pause.

‘Those are labels.’

‘Those are labels from my own vocabulary, proposed based on what is in the entry, with counts showing how many other things carry each one.’ She pointed. ‘Technique, correctly. Organism, correctly. And that third one I would not have applied.’

‘Is it wrong?’

‘It is not wrong and it is not what I was thinking about.’ Priya read it again. ‘This paper is about a delivery method and I filed it mentally as a delivery paper. It also uses a particular imaging approach, prominently, and I would have missed that.’

‘Which matters?’

‘It matters in about four months, when I am writing the chapter about imaging and I search for that technique.’ She shrugged. ‘At which point this paper appears, because a suggestion I accepted in eleven seconds put it there.’

‘So the value is not the labelling.’

‘The value is that it reads the whole entry and I read it with an intention.’ Priya was precise. ‘I know why I saved something. The suggestion knows what is in it, and those are different, and the difference is where the useful proposals come from.’

Claire tried it with a filing.

‘It has proposed two of mine,’ she said. ‘Correctly.’

‘Say whether it surprised you.’

‘Not at all, because a patent filing is an extremely conventional document and there is nothing in it I did not expect.’ Claire was matter of fact. ‘Which is a reasonable outcome and it does something else that I do value.’

‘Say it.’

‘It applies the label I would have applied and did not, on the days when I am moving quickly.’ She shrugged. ‘Which is perhaps one entry in five, and those are the entries that would otherwise be under-labelled and therefore harder to find later.’

‘So the value for you is consistency rather than discovery.’

‘Consistency rather than discovery, which for somebody whose vocabulary is small and conventional is the correct benefit.’ Claire paused. ‘And I would say plainly that I dismissed this feature for a year because I assumed I knew my own labels.’

‘Which you do.’

‘Which I do, and knowing them is not the same as applying them at four in the afternoon on a Friday.’

Priya said something about the counts.

‘There is a detail on those chips that I use more than the suggestion itself,’ she said.

‘The number.’

‘The number, which tells me how many other things carry that label.’ She was definite. ‘A proposal for a label with two hundred entries behind it is a well-established category. A proposal for one with three is a category I am not really maintaining.’

‘So the count is a warning as well as information.’

‘The count tells me whether I am about to add something to a real family or to a stub, and that has stopped me creating duplicates several times.’


## The thing about search that Claire did not know

‘I want to raise something Suki told me and I have not seen anybody mention,’ Priya said.

‘Go on.’

‘Labels are weighted second in search, above body text and above everything except titles.’

Claire stopped.

‘Say that again.’

‘A match on a label counts for more than a match in the body of an entry.’ Priya was precise. ‘Titles first, labels second, and everything else below that.’

‘Which means labelling affects findability rather than only organisation.’

‘Which means the two things I had been treating as separate are the same thing, and I found this out about eight months in.’

‘Demonstrate it.’

Priya searched a technique name.

‘Two entries contain that term,’ she said. ‘One has it as a label and mentions it once in the body. The other mentions it four times in the body and has no label.’

‘Which comes first?’

‘The labelled one, comfortably, and the body-only one is on the second page.’ She scrolled. ‘Which is correct behaviour and which is not obvious.’

‘Say why it is correct.’

‘Because a label is a deliberate statement about what something is and a body mention is an occurrence.’ Priya counted. ‘A paper that mentions a technique four times in passing is about something else. A paper I labelled with that technique is about it.’

‘The label is a claim and the text is evidence.’

‘The label is a claim about the whole entry and the text is evidence about a sentence, and weighting the claim higher is the only sensible arrangement.’

Claire was working through something.

‘This explains a thing that has puzzled me for a year,’ she said.

‘Say it.’

‘My older material, the entries I brought across from my previous arrangement, is measurably harder to find than anything I have created since.’ She was frank. ‘And I had assumed it was a search problem or an indexing problem.’

‘And it is a labelling problem.’

‘It is a labelling problem, because I imported two thousand items and labelled perhaps a third of them, and the two thirds without labels are competing on body text alone.’ Claire shrugged. ‘Which puts them behind everything I have labelled properly, permanently.’

‘Are you going to fix it?’

‘I am going to fix the ones that matter and leave the rest, which is the honest answer.’ She was matter of fact. ‘Two thousand items is not an evening. But the active matters are perhaps eighty entries and those I will do this week, now that I know it changes where they appear.’

‘Which is a better reason than tidiness.’

‘It is a considerably better reason than tidiness and it is the first argument for labelling that has ever actually motivated me,’ Claire said. ‘Organisation is a virtue. Findability is a consequence I experience daily.’


## Asking questions the structure cannot answer

‘Show me your filtering,’ Priya said. ‘Because mine is simple and yours is legal and I suspect more elaborate.’

‘Mine is two combinations and I use them constantly.’ Claire demonstrated. ‘The first is a stage label combined with a date range.’

‘Say the question it answers.’

‘Everything that received an examiner response in the last month.’ She was precise. ‘Which is a question I ask every Monday and which the page structure cannot answer at all, because those matters are under different clients.’

‘So it cuts across.’

‘It cuts across every client I have, which is the entire point, and previously I answered it by looking at eighty matters individually.’

‘How long did that take?’

‘Perhaps forty minutes, weekly, and I did it badly because forty minutes of scanning is forty minutes of degrading attention.’ Claire shrugged. ‘Now it is one filter and about four seconds and it does not degrade.’

‘And the second combination?’

‘A document type label combined with a file type.’ She showed it. ‘Everything that is prior art and is a document, across all clients, which is what I want when I am preparing an argument.’

‘Why the file type as well?’

‘Because prior art arrives as documents and as correspondence about documents, and when I am building an argument I want the documents.’ Claire was definite. ‘The label alone gives me both and the combination gives me what I actually want.’

Priya said hers was one filter and one purpose.

‘I filter by status,’ she said. ‘Specifically by whichever things I have marked as important.’

‘How many?’

‘About thirty out of eleven hundred, which is the number I want it to be.’ She was emphatic. ‘Because the thirty are the papers my thesis actually rests on, and they are scattered across six chapters and would be findable in no other way.’

‘Which is a cross-structure question.’

‘Which is the cross-structure question that matters most in my work, and I ask it perhaps twice a week.’ Priya paused. ‘And I want to say something about that number, because keeping it at thirty is a discipline.’

‘Say it.’

‘The temptation is to mark everything good, and there are perhaps three hundred good papers.’ She shrugged. ‘A marker applied to three hundred things answers no question. The marker only works because I have been ruthless about it, and every time I add one I consider whether something should come off.’

‘That is a considerably more demanding practice than mine.’

‘It is demanding for one label out of forty-one and effortless for the other forty,’ Priya said. ‘The status family is the only one requiring judgment. Everything else is a description of what a thing is, which is not a decision.’


## The label neither of them will create

‘There is a category I have wanted for two years and have deliberately not built,’ Claire said.

‘Say it.’

‘Difficulty.’

Priya made a noise of recognition.

‘Say why you have not.’

‘Because it would be a judgment made at the wrong moment.’ Claire was precise. ‘A matter looks difficult when it arrives and turns out to be routine. Another looks routine and consumes four months.’

‘So the judgment is unreliable.’

‘The judgment is reliably wrong at the moment I would be making it, which is the moment I know least.’ She shrugged. ‘And a category applied unreliably is worse than no category, which we have already established.’

‘Could you apply it afterward?’

‘I could apply it on conclusion, which would produce something genuinely useful, and I have never done it because by the time a matter concludes I have stopped thinking about it.’ Claire laughed. ‘Which is an honest description of why a good idea has not happened for two years.’

‘Mine is the same shape,’ Priya said. ‘Quality of the work.’

‘Say why not.’

‘Because I would be marking somebody else's research as good or poor, permanently, based on one reading, frequently while tired.’ She was serious. ‘And I would then filter it out and never revisit that judgment.’

‘Which is the actual risk.’

‘Which is the risk that stops me, because the marker would not merely be unreliable, it would be self-reinforcing.’ Priya counted. ‘A paper I marked as weak on a Tuesday is a paper I never see again, and the judgment never gets tested.’

‘Whereas your important marker.’

‘Whereas the important marker is additive rather than subtractive, which is the entire difference.’ She was definite. ‘Marking something in does not hide anything. Marking something out does, and I will not build a category that hides things.’

Claire sat with that.

‘That is a distinction I have not made and should,’ she said.

‘Say it back to me.’

‘Categories that promote and categories that suppress are different objects, and the second requires a standard of accuracy the first does not.’ She nodded. ‘Which means my priority marker is safe and a difficulty marker would not have been, and I have been thinking about the wrong problem.’


## Where labels meet everything else

‘I want to close on the connections,’ Claire said, ‘because I have started to think of labels as the thing joining every other part together.’

‘Say what you mean.’

‘They appear everywhere.’ She counted. ‘On the cards in my board. In the analysis. In the filters. In the search ranking. On every entry in the list. There is no part of this I use where labels are absent.’

‘Which is not true of anything else.’

‘Which is not true of pages, which live in the sidebar and nowhere else, and not true of sections, which live inside entries.’ Claire was definite. ‘Labels are the only thing that appears in every surface.’

Priya described the board.

‘Mine is two-dimensional because of that,’ she said. ‘The columns are stage, which comes from inline markers, and the colours on the cards are family, which comes from labels.’

‘So you read both axes at once.’

‘I look at a board and I can see that my drafting column is entirely one colour, which tells me I am drafting from one chapter and neglecting another.’ She shrugged. ‘Which is a thing I would not otherwise notice for weeks.’

‘That is an analysis rather than a status view.’

‘Which is what a board becomes once the cards carry more than a title, and I did not design it. It emerged because the labels were already there.’

Claire raised the distribution chart.

‘I want to tell you what mine showed me, because it was uncomfortable.’

‘Go on.’

‘One label dominates completely, which is the one meaning active.’ She was frank. ‘Which I expected. What I did not expect was the second largest, which is my highest priority marker.’

‘Say why that is uncomfortable.’

‘Because it should be a small category and it is a third of my matters.’ Claire shrugged. ‘Which means I have been marking things as urgent on receipt rather than on assessment, for about two years, and the marker has stopped distinguishing anything.’

‘That is exactly my three hundred good papers.’

‘It is precisely your three hundred good papers in a different profession, and I did not see it until I looked at the distribution.’ She was definite. ‘The chart is the only thing that would have told me, because in daily use every individual decision looked reasonable.’

‘Which is how these things go wrong.’

‘Which is how every judgment category goes wrong, one reasonable decision at a time, and the distribution is the only place where the accumulation is visible.’

Priya said hers had shown the opposite problem.

‘Mine showed under-use rather than over-use,’ she said. ‘Two techniques with four entries each, which should have thirty.’

‘Say what that meant.’

‘That I stopped applying them after the first month and never noticed.’ She shrugged. ‘Which is not a vocabulary problem. It is that I built the family and then got busy, and the family exists and describes almost nothing.’

‘And you fixed it?’

‘I fixed it over about three weeks, a few a day, which is the only way anybody fixes anything of that shape.’ Priya laughed. ‘And I now check the distribution monthly specifically to catch that, because it took me a year to notice the first time.’


## What they would do differently

‘I want the mistakes,’ Priya said. ‘Because we have described two systems that work and neither of us built them correctly the first time.’

‘Mine was three separate mistakes and they came in order.’ Claire counted. ‘Too many, then too few, then inconsistently applied.’

‘Say the first.’

‘Forty-something, of which about thirty were single-use.’ She shrugged. ‘Which felt like precision at the time and was actually a habit of creating a word whenever I could not immediately decide where something fitted.’

‘Which is a decision deferred rather than made.’

‘Which is exactly what it was, and every one of those thirty was a moment where I should have thought for four seconds and instead invented a category.’

‘And the second?’

‘I overcorrected to six and lost distinctions that mattered.’ Claire was rueful. ‘Which lasted about two months before I noticed I could not tell an active matter from a concluded one.’

‘So the correct number was between.’

‘The correct number was eleven and I found it by removing rather than by planning, which is the only way I know to find it.’ She was definite. ‘Nobody designs the right vocabulary in advance. You build one, use it for a season, and remove whatever you have never filtered by.’

‘And the third mistake?’

‘Application, which is the one that persists.’ Claire was frank. ‘I have eleven labels and I apply perhaps nine consistently, and the two I do not are effectively decorative.’

‘Which makes them worse than absent.’

‘Which makes them considerably worse than absent, because a filter on a partially applied label returns a subset and looks like an answer.’ She was emphatic. ‘An absent category tells you nothing and you know it. A half-applied one tells you something false and you do not.’

Priya said hers was one mistake with two faces.

‘I built taxonomies for their own sake,’ she said.

‘Say what that means.’

‘I designed a family because it was intellectually satisfying rather than because I had a question it answered.’ She was slightly embarrassed. ‘Which for somebody in research is a genuine temptation, because classification is fun.’

‘Give me an example.’

‘I had a family for methodological rigour, on a four-point scale, which I applied to about two hundred papers.’ Priya laughed. ‘And I never once filtered by it, in two years, and I removed the whole family last spring.’

‘Two hundred applications wasted.’

‘Two hundred applications and probably six hours, and the lesson cost me exactly that.’ She shrugged. ‘Which is why my rule is now that a family must correspond to a question I have actually asked out loud.’

‘Not a question you might ask.’

‘Not a question I might ask, because I might ask anything, and that standard admits everything.’ Priya was definite. ‘A question I have asked, and can remember asking, and was frustrated not to be able to answer.’

Claire said that was better than her own rule.

‘Mine is whether I have ever filtered by it, which requires the label to exist first.’

‘Whereas mine works before you build anything.’

‘Yours is a design rule and mine is a pruning rule and both are necessary,’ Claire said. ‘Build only what answers a question you have had. Remove anything you have never used. Those two together are the whole of vocabulary maintenance.’


## What Priya does that Claire cannot

‘There is one part of my system that would not work in your profession,’ Priya said, ‘and I want to describe it because the reason is interesting.’

‘Go on.’

‘My chapter family is temporary.’

‘Say what you mean.’

‘Six categories, one per thesis chapter, which will be meaningless in about eighteen months.’ She was matter of fact. ‘When the thesis is submitted, that family describes a document that is finished and a structure that no longer organises anything.’

‘And you built it knowing that.’

‘I built it knowing that and I would build it again, because for the four years it matters it is the most useful family I have.’ Priya shrugged. ‘Eleven hundred papers and the question I ask most often is which chapter something serves.’

‘What happens to it afterward?’

‘I do not know, and I have deliberately not decided.’ She was frank. ‘Probably it stays, as a historical record of what the thesis drew on, which is not nothing.’

‘Would you remove it?’

‘I would not remove it and I would stop applying it, which is different and which is the honest fate of most temporary structure.’ Priya paused. ‘Things do not get deleted. They stop being maintained, and the useful question is whether that is a decision or a drift.’

Claire said her equivalent was different.

‘Nothing in mine is temporary,’ she said. ‘Which I had not noticed until you said that.’

‘Say why.’

‘Because my families describe permanent properties of a matter.’ She counted. ‘What kind of protection. What stage. What kind of document. None of those expires, because a granted patent is still a granted patent in fifteen years.’

‘So your vocabulary outlives everything.’

‘My vocabulary outlives every individual matter, which means I should be considerably more careful about it than you need to be about yours.’ Claire was thoughtful. ‘Yours has a horizon. Mine does not, and I have been treating both as though they were the same kind of decision.’

‘Which changes what?’

‘Which changes how much I should think before adding anything, because I am not adding a category for a project. I am adding one to a vocabulary that will describe my practice until I retire.’


## The part neither of them expected

‘There is something I want to raise that is not about organisation at all,’ Priya said, near the end.

‘Go on.’

‘My labels have told me things about my own research that I did not know.’

‘Say one.’

‘When I looked at the distribution across my chapter family, one chapter had a third as many papers as the others.’ She was matter of fact. ‘Which I took as a labelling gap and it was not.’

‘It was a research gap.’

‘It was a chapter I had been avoiding, which I knew somewhere and had not admitted, and the count made it a fact rather than a feeling.’ Priya shrugged. ‘I spent that summer on it.’

‘Because of a count.’

‘Because a count is not arguable and a feeling is.’ She was definite. ‘I could tell myself I was making progress on that chapter. I could not tell myself that when the number was forty against a hundred and twenty.’

Claire was quiet for a second.

‘Mine is the same mechanism and less flattering,’ she said.

‘Say it.’

‘My document type distribution shows that I generate about four times as many responses as filings.’ She was frank. ‘Which is a fact about my practice that I would not have stated and which is obviously true once seen.’

‘Say what it means.’

‘That I spend most of my professional life arguing with examiners rather than drafting new applications.’ Claire shrugged. ‘Which is what a mature patent practice looks like and which I had not internalised, and which has changed how I think about hiring.’

‘From a label count.’

‘From a label count that I was maintaining for entirely different reasons, and which turned out to be a description of my working life that nobody had ever produced.’ She paused. ‘Which is the argument I would make to somebody who thinks this is filing.’

‘Say the argument.’

‘That every category you apply is a small statement about a thing, and a few thousand statements is a portrait.’ Claire was definite. ‘You do not build it deliberately. It accumulates because you were doing something else, which is precisely what makes it honest.’


## The second axis

The problem with any hierarchy is geometric rather than a matter of care. A structure places each thing in exactly one location, and almost nothing in professional life belongs in exactly one place. Choosing a deeper structure only means choosing which single dimension takes precedence, and every choice is wrong for some proportion of the questions somebody will later ask.

Categories resolve this by running perpendicular. A location says where something lives; a category says what it is. The practitioners in this conversation hold entirely different material, scientific literature and legal filings, and arrived at structurally identical vocabularies without ever comparing notes: a small number of families, each answering a different question about the same object, each independent of the others. Technique, organism, journal, chapter, and status in one case. Matter type, stage, document type, and priority in the other. Five families and four, forty-one items and eleven.

The rule both eventually stated is that families must be independent. If knowing one tells you the other, they are the same family expressed twice, which one practitioner discovered after four months of diligently applying both a journal category and a prestige category that were the same thing. The complementary rule concerns granularity: a distinction that matters while filing and never while retrieving is not worth maintaining, and the honest test is whether somebody has ever filtered by it rather than whether they might.

Colour turns out to carry meaning at the family level rather than the individual one, which is what makes it useful. One practitioner colours by urgency so that scanning eighty matters and counting four reds takes two seconds where reading would take forty. The other colours by family with exactly one deliberate exception, on the grounds that two exceptions is not a system and that a rule which cannot be stated in a sentence stops meaning anything.

The suggestion mechanism produces different value depending on the material. For unconventional content it proposes categories the person had not considered, because they know why they saved something and the suggestion knows what is in it, and the gap between those is where useful proposals come from. For conventional content it produces consistency rather than discovery, catching the one entry in five that would otherwise be under-labelled because somebody was moving quickly on a Friday afternoon. The counts on the proposals do additional work, since a proposal backed by two hundred entries is an established family and one backed by three is a stub.

The finding that changed one practitioner's behaviour immediately concerns search ranking. Categories are weighted second, above body text, which means labelling determines findability rather than only organisation. A category is a deliberate claim about a whole entry; a body mention is an occurrence in a sentence. Weighting the claim higher is correct, and it explained why two thousand imported items with a third of them labelled had been persistently harder to find than anything created since. That is a considerably more motivating argument than tidiness, because it describes something experienced daily.

Filtering is where the second axis pays for itself, because it answers questions the structure cannot. Everything at one stage across all clients. Everything of one document type in one file format. The thirty papers a thesis actually rests on, scattered across six chapters. Each of those cuts across the hierarchy, and each replaces an operation that previously meant examining eighty items individually with degrading attention.

Two failure patterns recurred, and both are about judgment categories rather than descriptive ones. Something meaning important, applied to three hundred items, distinguishes nothing. Something meaning urgent, applied on receipt rather than on assessment, stops meaning urgent within two years. Neither is visible during daily use, because every individual application looked reasonable at the time, and the distribution view is the only place where the accumulation becomes apparent.

The mistakes both described follow a sequence worth anticipating: too many categories, created as a way of deferring a decision; then an overcorrection that loses real distinctions; then inconsistent application, which is the failure that persists. A half-applied category is worse than an absent one, since an absent category tells somebody nothing and they know it, while a half-applied one returns a subset that looks like an answer.

What neither expected is that the counts describe the person. One practitioner found a chapter with a third of the material of the others, recognised it as a subject she had been avoiding, and spent a summer on it, because a count is not arguable and a feeling is. The other found that she generates four times as many responses as filings, which is an accurate description of a mature practice that she had never stated and which has changed how she thinks about hiring. Neither built those descriptions deliberately. They accumulated while somebody was doing something else, which is precisely what makes them honest.


## FAQs

### What is the difference between a page and a label?

A page says where something lives and a label says what it is. Practitioners describe reading such a board for composition as well as status: noticing that an entire column is one colour reveals that work is concentrated in one area and neglecting another, which is a finding about attention rather than about progress. One describes this emerging rather than being designed, since the categories were already present. Anybody who has never examined their own distribution should do so once, since the result is frequently more informative than expected. ### What is the risk of treating labels as a filing system?

That somebody applies them exhaustively rather than accurately. Practitioners describe the temptation to attach every plausible description, which produces entries carrying eight categories and a vocabulary where nothing distinguishes anything. The families structure prevents this, since each family contributes at most one, and the discipline is to apply the accurate description rather than every applicable one. Invisible. Practitioners with stable vocabularies describe applying categories without deliberation, filtering without constructing anything, and reading colour rather than text. ### Is there a profession where this matters less?

Anybody whose material genuinely has one dimension, which practitioners struggle to name an example of. A collection of purely chronological records might qualify, though even those usually carry a type and a status. The more common situation is somebody who believes their material is one-dimensional because their tool only expresses one dimension, which practitioners describe as the assumption worth testing before accepting. Practitioners across very different professions report the same finding, which suggests it follows from the nature of professional material rather than from any particular kind of work. The exercise takes a few minutes and produces an answer that is difficult to argue with in either direction.

### How does somebody test whether they need a second axis?

By listing the questions they have wanted to ask and could not. Practitioners describe this exercise producing three or four questions within minutes, each cutting across the structure: everything of one kind regardless of location, everything at one stage regardless of project, everything marked as significant regardless of where it sits. ### What happens if somebody skips categories entirely?

The collection remains navigable through structure and search and loses the cross-structure questions permanently. ### Can categories be added later to an existing collection?

Yes, and practitioners describe doing it selectively rather than comprehensively. Applying a new family retroactively to thousands of items is not an evening; applying it to the actively used subset is. ### What should somebody do this week?

Look at the distribution of whatever categories they already have, which takes about ten minutes and reliably produces at least one surprise. Practitioners describe finding a category applied to four items that should cover thirty, or a judgment marker applied to a third of everything and therefore distinguishing nothing. Both are invisible in daily use and both are correctable in an evening once seen, which makes the ten minutes an unusually good return. Anybody with no categories at all should instead spend the ten minutes writing down questions they have wanted to ask their own collection and could not, since those questions define the families directly and building from them produces something that survives rather than something abandoned within a season.
