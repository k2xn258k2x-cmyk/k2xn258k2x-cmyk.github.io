Almost every system for organising knowledge is built on an assumption nobody states, which is that knowledge is made of words. Search matches words. Categories are words. Structure is expressed in words, and the entire apparatus of retrieval assumes that whatever somebody wants to find can be described in language they will remember using. For a great deal of professional material that assumption holds well enough that its existence never becomes visible.

It fails completely for anybody whose primary material is visual. A documentary maker's footage, a surveyor's photographs, a clinician's images, a historian's scanned collection, an architect's site record: in each case the pictures are not illustrations attached to the real work. They are the work, or the evidence for it, and the written material around them is commentary. Treating that content as an attachment to a note inverts the relationship between the substance and the note about the substance.

![Browsing a Life in Photos and Videos](/assets/img/feature-screenshot-placeholder.svg)

The practical consequence is a familiar failure that people rarely name. Somebody accumulates several thousand images, each with a filename chosen by a camera, and the collection becomes reachable only through whatever they wrote beside it. The images are present, occupy space, and are in practice unreachable, because nobody remembers a number a device assigned at the moment of capture and no amount of diligence produces a memory of eleven thousand of them.
<!--more-->


Two entirely separate capabilities are needed to close that gap, and they address different halves of the problem. The first is recognition. People identify images by looking at them, instantly and reliably, in a way that has no equivalent for text: somebody scanning a wall of thumbnails finds the picture they want in seconds without being able to describe what they are looking for. Any arrangement presenting visual material as a list of names has discarded the fastest retrieval mechanism the user actually possesses.

The second is that a large proportion of photographs contain words. A sign, a label, a certificate, a page of handwriting, a whiteboard, a printed form, a caption written on the back of a print decades ago. Those words are as meaningful as anything typed, and they are invisible to any system that treats an image as an opaque block of pixels. Extracting them converts a picture from something only findable by memory into something findable by content, which is a change of kind rather than of degree.

There is a third layer that people discover later and value disproportionately. Documents arriving from other organisations frequently contain images inside them: a report with photographs embedded, a spreadsheet with pictures in cells, an archive containing both. The text visible in those embedded images is doubly buried, inside a picture inside a file, and reaching it requires opening the container and then reading the images within it.

What all of this changes, in the end, is behaviour rather than capability. Somebody who trusts that visual material will be findable photographs differently: more freely, more often, and with less deliberation about whether a given shot will justify itself. The deliberation is the expensive part, because it happens at the moment somebody knows least about what will matter later, and removing it is worth considerably more than any individual retrieval.

What follows is a conversation between two people whose visual collections could hardly be less alike, one accumulating at several hundred items a day and the other assembled across five decades.


## Berlin, Vermont, and two very different piles

Raj had suggested it from an edit suite, with headphones round his neck.

‘I want to compare something and I suspect we are at opposite ends of it,’ he said.

‘Say the ends.’

‘Mine arrives faster than I can look at it and yours has been sitting still for forty years.’

Felix laughed.

‘That is accurate and slightly unkind.’

‘Say your numbers.’

‘Perhaps two thousand photographs, of which I have scanned about six hundred.’ Felix was matter of fact. ‘They cover a working life. Conferences, departments, a great many people who are now dead, and about eleven boxes still in the barn.’

‘And the scanning is ongoing.’

‘The scanning is a Sunday afternoon activity and has been for two years, and I have accelerated recently for a reason I want to come to.’

‘Mine is the opposite problem entirely,’ Raj said. ‘A shooting day produces between four and eight hundred stills alongside the footage, and I have four productions in various states.’

‘How many altogether?’

‘I genuinely do not know and it is somewhere above forty thousand.’ He shrugged. ‘Which sounds unmanageable and is, if you approach it as a filing problem.’

‘And if you do not?’

‘Then it is a looking problem, which is different, and which I only worked out about eighteen months ago.’


## Looking rather than reading

‘Show me the thing you use most,’ Felix said.

Raj shared. A grid of images filled the screen, dense, several rows deep.

‘That is a shooting day,’ he said. ‘Point it at a folder and it shows you what is in it, as pictures.’

‘Which sounds obvious.’

‘Which sounds obvious and which almost nothing does.’ He was definite. ‘Every tool I used before this showed me a list of names, and a name assigned by a camera contains no information at all.’

‘Say what the difference produces.’

‘Speed that is not comparable.’ Raj scrolled through several hundred images in a few seconds. ‘I have just looked at an entire morning. If I were reading names I would still be on the first twenty and I would know nothing.’

‘Because you recognise rather than remember.’

‘Because recognition is instant and remembering is not, and the two are so different that treating them as the same operation is where all of this goes wrong.’ He paused. ‘I can find a specific frame in four hundred without being able to tell you a single thing about how it is named.’

Felix asked how it fitted into a working day.

‘Evening review,’ Raj said. ‘I get back, I point it at the day's folder, and I go through the grid deciding what needs processing.’

‘Which is a triage.’

‘Which is a triage and it takes eleven minutes for eight hundred images, because most of them are variations and I am looking for the ones that are not.’ He shrugged. ‘Then those become entries with the actual material attached, and the rest stay in the folder.’

‘You do not bring everything in.’

‘I bring in perhaps one in thirty, which is the correct ratio for a production and would be wrong for you.’

Felix described his own use, which he said was slower and stranger.

‘Mine is a box at a time,’ he said. ‘I scan a box, which is an afternoon, and then I look at what I have.’

‘And?’

‘And I sit there for considerably longer than the scanning took, because a grid of a hundred and forty photographs from one decade is a thing I have never seen.’ He was quieter. ‘They existed as a box. A box is a thing you take one item out of at a time.’

‘Whereas the grid.’

‘Whereas the grid is all of them at once, which produces a completely different experience and which I did not anticipate.’ Felix paused. ‘I found four photographs of the same person across a decade in one screen, which I had never noticed, because in a box they were eleven inches apart in a stack.’

‘That is a research use.’

‘That is a research use that arrived from a browsing tool and it has changed how I approach the chapters.’ He shrugged. ‘I now scan a period before I write about it, deliberately, because seeing the decade at once tells me things the writing does not.’


## Two ways of looking, and they are not the same

‘There is a second one,’ Raj said, ‘and the distinction took me a while.’

‘Say it.’

‘The first looks at a folder on my machine. The second looks at what is already inside the vault.’ He demonstrated. ‘Everything attached to anything, browsable by type, by entry, or by page.’

‘Which is a completely different question.’

‘Which is a completely different question and I used the wrong one for months.’ Raj was rueful. ‘I would go looking in the folder for something I had already brought in and attached, which meant I was searching the raw material for something that had already been processed.’

‘How do you decide now?’

‘Has it been taken in yet.’ He counted. ‘If it is raw and sitting in a folder, the folder tool. If it is attached to an entry, the internal one. And the moment I stated that to myself the confusion stopped entirely.’

Felix said his use of the internal one was more frequent.

‘Because almost everything I have is already in,’ he said. ‘My scanning produces files and I attach them the same afternoon, so the folder is a staging area for perhaps four hours.’

‘Then what do you use it for?’

‘Finding a photograph when I cannot remember which entry it belongs to, which is most of the time.’ Felix was frank. ‘I attached something to a chapter eleven months ago. I want it now. I have no memory of which chapter.’

‘And browsing all the images.’

‘And browsing all the images by type, at which point I recognise it immediately, and it tells me which entry it is on.’ He shrugged. ‘Which is retrieval by recognition rather than by structure, and it works precisely because I am bad at the structural version.’

‘That is the honest description of why this matters,’ Raj said.

‘Say what you mean.’

‘Every organisational system assumes you will remember where you put things.’ He was definite. ‘And the visual tools are the admission that for images you will not, and that you do not need to, because you will know it when you see it.’


## The picture beside the sentence

‘Now the thing I would defend hardest,’ Felix said, ‘which is where the image actually sits.’

‘Inside the text.’

‘Inside the text, in the place in the paragraph where it belongs, rather than in a list at the bottom.’ He shared a chapter. ‘Look at this.’

Raj read for a moment.

‘That is a photograph of a room,’ he said.

‘That is a photograph of a departmental meeting room in a building that no longer exists, and it sits directly beneath the sentence about what happened in it.’ Felix was definite. ‘Which means anybody reading that passage, including me, encounters the image at the moment it is relevant.’

‘And in a list?’

‘And in a list it is a filename at the bottom of a page, beneath four other filenames, and I would have to decide to open it.’ He shrugged. ‘Which I would not do, because I know what is in it, and the reader who is not me would not do it either.’

‘Say what the difference produces for the writing.’

‘It changes what I write.’ Felix said it slowly. ‘Because when the photograph is there, in the paragraph, I stop describing what is visible in it and start writing about what it means, which is the only part worth having.’

‘Whereas without.’

‘Whereas without, half of every paragraph is me describing a room, badly, from memory, so the reader can follow.’ He laughed. ‘Which is wasted effort and worse prose.’

Raj described his version, which he said was operational rather than literary.

‘Mine is reference photographs inside scene notes,’ he said. ‘A location shot sits directly above my reasoning about why we should shoot there.’

‘Which somebody else reads.’

‘Which my cinematographer reads, and which previously meant sending a document and a folder of images and a message explaining which was which.’ He shrugged. ‘Three objects, and the connection between them existed in the message, which is a thing people delete.’

‘And now?’

‘Now the reasoning and the image are the same object, and when I hand somebody a scene they get the whole thing.’ Raj was definite. ‘Which has removed an entire category of confusion that I had assumed was inherent to the job.’

They discussed the mixing of the two, which both did.

‘I attach as well as embed,’ Felix said. ‘Which sounds redundant.’

‘Say the rule.’

‘Embed the ones that carry an argument. Attach the ones that are evidence.’ He counted. ‘A photograph that makes a point about a person goes in the paragraph. Forty photographs from the same event go in a section, because putting forty images inside prose produces something nobody can read.’

‘Mine is the same and I would put it as one and many,’ Raj said. ‘One image inside the text. Many images in a section attached to the entry.’

‘Which is the same rule stated better.’

‘It is the same rule and I have been applying it without noticing, which seems to be the theme of this call.’


## The words inside the pictures

‘Now the part I actually wanted to talk about,’ Raj said, ‘because it changed something fundamental and I want to know whether yours changed the same way.’

‘Text in images.’

‘Text in images, extracted automatically, and searchable like anything else.’ He was already pulling something up. ‘Watch this.’

He typed two words about a certification, and a result came back.

‘That is a photograph,’ Felix said.

‘That is a photograph of a label on a wall in a distillery, taken in about four seconds while somebody was talking to me, and I have never typed anything about it anywhere.’ Raj was emphatic. ‘I did not tag it. I did not caption it. I photographed a wall.’

‘And the words on the wall are searchable.’

‘The words on the wall are searchable, which means that photograph is a document rather than a picture.’ He paused. ‘And I have perhaps four thousand photographs of walls, signs, labels, price boards, and pages of handwritten notes that people have shown me.’

‘Say what that changes about the shooting.’

‘I photograph everything now.’ Raj said it plainly. ‘Every sign, every label, every certificate on a wall, every page anybody shows me. Because the cost is four seconds and the material becomes findable without any further effort from me.’

‘And previously?’

‘Previously I decided, at the moment, whether something was worth photographing, which is a judgment made by somebody who does not yet know what the film is about.’ He shrugged. ‘I was wrong constantly and I only ever found out much later.’

Felix's version was slower and he said it had made him alter a two-year habit.

‘Mine is the backs of photographs,’ he said.

‘Say more.’

‘People wrote on the backs of prints. Names, dates, places, occasionally a remark.’ He was almost apologetic about his own excitement. ‘Which is where all the information is, and which I had been ignoring for two years because scanning the back doubles the work.’

‘And now you scan both sides.’

‘Now I scan both sides of everything, because the handwriting on the back is extracted and becomes searchable, which means I can find a photograph by the name of somebody in it.’ Felix was definite. ‘Written half a century ago by a person who has been dead for thirty years, in pencil, and it works.’

‘How well?’

‘Better on printing than on cursive, which is what anybody would expect, and well enough on both to be worth it.’ He shrugged. ‘Somebody who wrote in careful capitals is completely reliable. My late colleague Harriet had appalling handwriting and I get perhaps half of hers, which is still half more than nothing.’

‘Have you found anything you had lost?’

Felix took a moment.

‘I searched a name last month, expecting nothing, because I was testing it.’ He said it evenly. ‘And a photograph came back that I have owned for fifty years and could not have described. It is captioned on the back in his own handwriting and it is the only image I have of a man I shared an office with for six years.’

Neither of them said anything for a second.

‘I had not looked at the back,’ Felix said. ‘It was in a box, face up, for five decades.’

Raj asked about the mechanics, which Felix said he had asked Suki about.

‘It happens by itself and the result is kept,’ he said. ‘So the reading occurs once, and afterward the words are just there, and a search reaches them like anything else.’

‘Which is why it is fast.’

‘Which is why the eleventh search is as fast as the second, and I was expecting something that ground away every time I typed.’

‘And the weighting?’

‘Above my own prose, which surprised me and which Suki explained.’ Felix recounted it. ‘Text found inside an image ranks higher than body text, because a word appearing in a photograph is unusual and specific, whereas a word in my writing could be anywhere. The specificity is the signal.’

‘That is exactly right and I would not have guessed it.’


## What neither of them can do

‘I want to be honest about the limits,’ Raj said, ‘because I have been enthusiastic for forty minutes and somebody reading this would form an inaccurate impression.’

‘Say them.’

‘It reads words. It does not know what a picture is of.’ He was precise. ‘A photograph of an agave field with nothing written in it is findable by nothing at all except my own description or my recognition of it.’

‘Which is most photographs.’

‘Which is the large majority of mine, and the extraction is doing an enormous amount of work on a minority.’ Raj shrugged. ‘Which is fine, because that minority is the part that would otherwise be entirely lost, and the majority I can find by looking.’

Felix nodded slowly.

‘Mine is the same and the balance is different,’ he said. ‘Perhaps a third of my prints have something written on them, which is a considerably higher proportion than I expected.’

‘Because people captioned things.’

‘Because in that era people captioned things, and a departmental photographer wrote on the reverse of everything as a matter of course.’ He paused. ‘Which means a third of my collection has become searchable and two thirds has not, and the two thirds requires me to do something.’

‘What do you do?’

‘I write a line.’ Felix was matter of fact. ‘When I attach an uncaptioned photograph I write one sentence saying who is in it and where, which takes fifteen seconds and which is the only way it will ever be found.’

‘Do you always?’

‘I do not always and I should, and the ones I have skipped are exactly the ones I later spend twenty minutes browsing for.’ He laughed at himself. ‘Which is a lesson I have learned four times and have not yet acted on consistently.’

Raj had a different gap.

‘Mine is video,’ he said. ‘Which I browse and which contains an enormous amount of spoken material that is not text anywhere.’

‘Say what you do about it.’

‘I write timecodes into the entry, by hand, during the evening session.’ He shrugged. ‘Which is laborious and which is the only thing that makes an eleven-minute interview reachable at a specific moment.’

‘How long does that take?’

‘Perhaps four minutes per interview and it is the single most valuable four minutes in my process.’ Raj was definite. ‘Because a timecode written next to a description of what is said at it is the difference between having the footage and being able to use it.’

‘So the visual tools get you to the file.’

‘The visual tools get me to the file and my own four minutes get me to the moment inside it, and I would not want anybody to think the second part is automatic.’ He paused. ‘It is not, and it is worth doing anyway.’


## The photograph Raj could not use

‘I want to describe a failure,’ Raj said, ‘because everything so far has been things working.’

‘Go on.’

‘I photographed a page of a ledger in a palenque, handwritten, going back about forty years.’ He was matter of fact. ‘Production records. Yields, dates, and the names of people who had worked there.’

‘Which sounds like exactly the material you want.’

‘It is precisely the material I want and I got almost nothing out of it.’ Raj shrugged. ‘Because I photographed it at an angle, in a doorway, with the light behind me, while somebody held it open with one hand.’

‘And the extraction.’

‘Recovered perhaps a fifth, which is the dates and none of the names, and the names are the point.’ He was rueful. ‘Which is entirely my fault and not the tool's, and it took me a while to admit that.’

‘What did you do?’

‘Went back four days later and photographed it properly, flat, on a table, with the light from the side.’ He counted. ‘Eleven photographs instead of three, about four minutes instead of thirty seconds, and I got nearly all of it.’

‘So the lesson is take more care.’

‘The lesson is more specific than that and I want to state it properly.’ Raj was thinking. ‘The lesson is that when a photograph is being taken as a document rather than as an image, it is a different activity and requires different care.’

‘Say the distinction.’

‘A photograph of a room is a photograph. A photograph of a page is a scan performed with a phone.’ He spread his hands. ‘And I had been treating both as the same four-second operation, and one of them is not.’

Felix made a noise of recognition.

‘That is exactly the difference between my two scanning modes,’ he said.

‘Say yours.’

‘A print goes on the scanner properly and takes ninety seconds.’ He counted. ‘A page of a departmental newsletter, which is a document rather than an image, I used to photograph casually, and the results were poor for the same reasons.’

‘And now?’

‘And now everything that is words goes on the scanner and everything that is a picture can be photographed.’ Felix shrugged. ‘Which is slower and which produces material that is actually readable, and I have redone about forty items on that basis.’

‘Do you resent the redoing?’

‘I resent it enormously and I would rather have known at the beginning, which is why I am saying it now,’ he said.


## Pictures buried inside documents

‘There is a layer under that which I found by accident,’ Raj said.

‘Say how.’

‘A production company sent me a location report as a document, and it contained about thirty embedded photographs with the addresses printed on them.’ He described it. ‘And I searched a street name months later, for an entirely different reason, and the report came back.’

‘Which means it read inside the file.’

‘Which means it opened the document, found the images inside it, and read those.’ Raj was still slightly impressed. ‘Which is two layers down. A word, in a picture, in a file.’

‘Say why that matters more than it sounds.’

‘Because I do not control what people send me.’ He counted. ‘Collaborators send documents. Producers send spreadsheets. Archives send bundles. And the useful content is frequently inside an image that somebody pasted in, because that is how people share a photograph of a thing.’

‘And without it?’

‘Without it, every document containing an image is partially opaque, and I would have no way of knowing which parts I was missing.’ He shrugged. ‘Which is the worst kind of gap, because an absence produces no evidence.’

Felix had encountered the same thing from a different direction.

‘A university archive sent me a spreadsheet,’ he said. ‘Which is an odd object to receive and which is what somebody in an administrative office produced.’

‘What was in it?’

‘Rows about events, and pasted into the cells, photographs of the events, with printed captions and in some cases the programme visible.’ He was matter of fact. ‘Which is a terrible way to build an archive and it is what exists, and I am not in a position to ask them to do it again.’

‘And you can search it.’

‘I searched a conference name expecting nothing and it returned the spreadsheet, because the name was printed on a programme in a photograph in a cell.’ Felix laughed. ‘Which is a genuinely absurd number of layers and it worked.’

‘Have you tested archives?’

‘A colleague sent me a bundle containing both, and it reaches inside that as well.’ He shrugged. ‘Which I tested deliberately, because by that point I wanted to know where it stopped, and I have not found where it stops.’

Raj said something he had been thinking about.

‘This is the part that changes what you can accept from other people,’ he said.

‘Say more.’

‘Previously, receiving a badly assembled document was a problem I had to solve.’ He counted. ‘Extract the images. Rename them. Put them somewhere. Write down what they were. Which is an hour per document and which I therefore did not do.’

‘And the material sat there.’

‘And the material sat there, technically in my possession and practically absent.’ Raj was definite. ‘Whereas now I attach the thing as it arrived, in the form somebody sent it, and its contents participate.’

‘Which also preserves what it is.’

‘Which also preserves that this arrived as one object from one person on one date, which matters more in your work than in mine.’

‘It matters enormously in mine,’ Felix said. ‘A photograph from a departmental archive means something different from a photograph from a family, and the difference is entirely in where it came from.’


## Something Felix noticed about the grid

‘There is one more thing and it is not practical at all,’ Felix said.

‘Then it is probably the interesting one.’

‘Looking at a decade of photographs at once does something to me that looking at them one at a time does not.’ He was choosing words carefully. ‘And I want to describe it properly because I think it is a real effect rather than sentiment.’

‘Go on.’

‘A photograph on its own is an occasion. You look at it and you remember the occasion, or you do not.’ He paused. ‘A hundred and forty photographs at once is a period, and a period has a shape that no individual occasion contains.’

‘Say what the shape looks like.’

‘I can see when the department was happy.’ Felix said it plainly. ‘Which sounds absurd and I would defend it. There is a stretch of about four years where everybody is standing close together in every photograph, and a stretch afterward where they are not.’

Raj did not say anything for a moment.

‘Did you know that?’

‘I lived through it and I did not know it, because I experienced it one day at a time and never saw it laid out.’ He shrugged. ‘And it is a chapter now, and it would not have been.’

‘That is a research finding from a browsing tool.’

‘That is a research finding from a grid of thumbnails and I am aware of how that sounds,’ Felix said. ‘It is also the single most useful thing that has happened to the book this year.’

Raj said something that surprised him.

‘I have the same effect and I have never named it,’ he said. ‘When I review a shooting day as a grid I can see whether the day went well.’

‘From the photographs?’

‘From the density and the variation.’ He was working it out. ‘A good day has clusters, because we found something and stayed with it. A poor day is evenly spaced, because we were working through a list and nothing arrested us.’

‘And you can see that.’

‘I can see it in about two seconds and I have never once thought about why.’ Raj laughed. ‘Which is presumably the same mechanism as your four years of people standing close together.’

‘Aggregate visual information.’

‘Aggregate visual information that neither of us could extract by any other means, and which is available for free the moment you look at everything at once.’


## What this does to how they behave

‘I want to close on something that is not about features,’ Raj said, ‘which is that all of this has changed what I do rather than what I can do.’

‘Say it.’

‘I photograph without deciding.’ He was emphatic. ‘Which is the whole of it. Before, every photograph was a small judgment about whether the thing would be worth having, made by somebody standing in a room who does not yet know what the film is.’

‘And the judgment was bad.’

‘The judgment was necessarily bad, because the information required to make it arrives in the edit, eight months later.’ Raj shrugged. ‘Now the cost of a photograph is four seconds and the benefit is that it becomes findable by its own contents, so I take it.’

‘How much more do you take?’

‘Perhaps three times as much, and I have not once regretted a photograph and have frequently regretted the absence of one.’

Felix said his change was slower and larger.

‘I have accelerated the scanning,’ he said. ‘Considerably.’

‘Say the numbers.’

‘Six hundred done in two years, and I have done a hundred and eighty in the last two months.’ He was slightly embarrassed. ‘Which is a change of pace that has an entirely rational cause.’

‘Which is?’

‘That scanning used to produce a digital copy of a photograph, which is a nice thing to have and which does nothing.’ Felix was precise. ‘And now scanning produces something that enters the searchable body of my work, with whatever is written on it, connected to whatever it belongs to.’

‘So the same afternoon produces more.’

‘The same afternoon produces something that participates rather than something that is stored, and I am seventy-two, and there are eleven boxes in a barn.’ He said it evenly. ‘That arithmetic is not complicated and it has made me faster.’

Raj did not respond immediately.

‘Say the thing you are not saying.’

‘That I would like the boxes done before I am not able to do them, and that the boxes contain the only copies of a considerable number of photographs of people who are gone.’ Felix shrugged. ‘Which is not a productivity argument. It is why I spent last Sunday afternoon scanning rather than writing.’

‘Is that the right trade?’

‘I have decided it is, because the writing can happen for another decade and my eyes and hands may not,’ he said. ‘And because a scanned photograph with a caption is available to somebody after me and a box in a barn is not.’


## The afternoon in the barn

Felix mentioned it almost in passing, near the end, and Raj stopped him.

‘Say that again.’

‘I said the boxes are in the barn and I have been going out there on Sundays.’

‘You said something before that.’

Felix paused.

‘I said there are eleven of them and I have opened four.’

‘And the other seven?’

‘Are exactly as my predecessor left them, which is to say as I left them, which is to say in whatever order they were put down in.’ He shrugged. ‘Boxes accumulate the way boxes do. Nobody decides.’

Raj asked something he said afterward he had not planned to.

‘Do you know what is in them?’

‘Broadly. A decade each, roughly, and one of them is not photographs at all, it is correspondence.’ Felix was matter of fact. ‘Which I am not going to scan, because letters are a different project and I am not going to start a second one.’

‘Why not?’

‘Because I am seventy-two and the photographs are the thing that cannot be reconstructed.’ He said it without any drama. ‘A letter says what somebody thought and I frequently remember what they thought. A photograph is the only record of what a room looked like and who was standing in it, and nobody remembers that.’

‘That is a triage.’

‘That is a triage and I made it about eight months ago and I have not revisited it.’ Felix shrugged. ‘I have limited afternoons and I have decided which category gets them.’

Raj was quiet for a second.

‘Can I say something that may be out of place?’

‘You may.’

‘I archived eight hundred pieces of somebody else's writing with two other people in eleven days,’ he said. ‘Which sounds like an enormous project and was four evenings each.’

‘You are offering.’

‘I am observing that a barn full of boxes is a different arithmetic when it is not one person.’ Raj was careful. ‘And I am not going to press it, because it is your family and your decades and I have no standing in it whatsoever.’

Felix did not answer immediately.

‘It is a fair observation and I am going to think about it,’ he said. ‘My daughter has offered twice and I have said no twice, for reasons I would find difficult to defend if anybody asked me to.’

‘I am not asking.’

‘You are not asking and you have made the point, which is what people do in this group and which is occasionally exhausting.’ He said it fondly. ‘I will tell you what I decide.’


## The picture is the knowledge

The industry that has grown up around organising professional knowledge is built almost entirely on the assumption that knowledge is made of words, and the assumption is invisible until somebody whose material is visual tries to work inside it.

For those people the pictures are not illustrations attached to real work. A documentary maker's stills, a scanned collection spanning a working life, a surveyor's site record, a clinician's images: in each case the visual material is the substance or the evidence, and the writing is commentary on it. Treating that content as an attachment inverts the relationship, and the result is a familiar failure. Several thousand images, named by devices, reachable only through whatever somebody wrote beside them at the time.

Closing that gap requires two capabilities addressing entirely different halves of the problem. The first is recognition, which is the fastest retrieval mechanism people possess and which lists throw away completely. Somebody scanning a wall of thumbnails locates an image in seconds without being able to describe what they are looking for, and this holds whether the collection is eight hundred frames from a morning's shooting or a hundred and forty prints from one decade. The second capability is different in kind: browsing what is already inside a collection rather than what is sitting in a folder outside it. Both practitioners described using the wrong one for months, and both described the confusion ending the moment they stated the distinction, which is simply whether the material has been taken in yet.

The observation underneath is worth stating plainly, because it explains why visual browsing exists at all. Every organisational system assumes somebody will remember where they put things, and for images they will not. The visual tools are an admission of that, and they work precisely because recognition does not require the memory the structure was counting on.

Placement matters as much as retrieval and is easier to underestimate. An image inside a paragraph, at the point where it is relevant, is encountered by whoever reads that paragraph. The same image in a list at the bottom is a filename somebody would have to decide to open, which they will not. One practitioner described the effect on his own writing: with the picture present, he stopped describing what was visible in it and started writing about what it meant, which is the only part worth having and which had previously been consuming half of every paragraph. The complementary rule both had arrived at without noticing is one and many: a single image carrying an argument goes inside the text, and forty images from one occasion go into a section, since prose interrupted forty times is unreadable.

Extraction is the change of kind. A photograph containing words, whether a sign, a label, a certificate, a whiteboard, or a caption written in pencil half a century ago, becomes a document rather than a picture. One practitioner searched two words about a certification and recovered a photograph of a wall he had taken in four seconds while somebody was talking to him, having tagged nothing and captioned nothing. The layer people find by accident is the one that reaches into documents. What all of it produces, finally, is a change in behaviour rather than in capability. One practitioner photographs perhaps three times as much as before, having removed a judgment that was being made by somebody who could not yet know what would matter. The other has quadrupled his scanning rate, because a scan now enters the searchable body of his work rather than producing a digital copy that does nothing, and because he is seventy-two with eleven boxes in a barn containing the only images of people who are gone. Neither of those is a productivity argument. Both are descriptions of what somebody does differently once they trust that visual material will be found.


## FAQs

### Why do most knowledge systems handle visual material poorly?

Because they assume knowledge is made of words. Search matches words, categories are words, and the entire retrieval apparatus assumes whatever somebody wants can be described in language they will remember. For text-based work the assumption is invisible. For anybody whose primary material is visual it fails completely, since the images are the substance and the writing is commentary, and treating the substance as an attachment to the commentary inverts the relationship. The assumption is invisible until somebody whose work is primarily visual tries to operate inside it, at which point it becomes the dominant difficulty. Practitioners in unrelated fields arrive at the same conclusion independently, which suggests it follows from how visual material behaves rather than from any particular kind of work.

### What is the specific failure that follows?

Accumulation without retrieval. Somebody gathers several thousand images, each named by a device at the moment of capture, and the collection becomes reachable only through whatever they happened to write beside it. The material is present, occupies space, and is in practice unreachable, since nobody remembers a number a camera assigned and no amount of diligence produces recall of eleven thousand of them. The failure is silent, because an image nobody can find produces no evidence of its absence. Anybody with a large image collection can test this in a minute by trying to locate a specific photograph they know exists. It is one of the decisions that costs nothing at the outset and requires going through everything to apply later.

### What does visual browsing actually change?

It replaces remembering with recognising, which are not comparable operations. Somebody scanning a grid of thumbnails finds a specific frame among several hundred in seconds, without being able to describe it or name it. Reading a list of the same images would take considerably longer and would convey nothing, since device-assigned names contain no information. Practitioners describe reviewing eight hundred images from a morning in about eleven minutes, which is not achievable by any list-based method. The speed difference is large enough that people describe it as a different activity rather than a faster version of the same one. Anybody uncertain can settle it in a few minutes using their own collection rather than reasoning about it.

### What is the difference between the two visual browsing surfaces?

One scans a folder on the machine and the other browses what is already inside the collection. That is the whole distinction and it maps onto a single question: has this material been taken in yet. Raw files sitting in a folder are approached one way, attached material another. Practitioners describe using the wrong one for months, repeatedly searching the raw folder for something they had already processed, and the confusion ending the moment they stated the rule. Stating the rule explicitly, rather than working it out by trial, saves a few months of quiet confusion. The benefit accumulates rather than arriving at once, which is why the habit is difficult to justify in advance.

### When would somebody use the internal browser rather than search?

When they cannot remember where something is but would recognise it instantly. Practitioners describe this as retrieval by recognition rather than by structure, and note that it works precisely because they are unreliable at the structural version. The technique works precisely because it does not depend on the organisational memory that most people overestimate in themselves. ### Why does embedding an image in text matter?

### What does automatic text extraction from images do?

Converts a photograph containing words into something findable by those words. ### What would each of these practitioners tell somebody starting?

Point a visual browser at your largest folder before doing anything else, since seeing what you have is a different experience from knowing you have it. Photograph anything printed rather than transcribing it, and treat pages as scans rather than as snapshots. Embed the single image that carries an argument and attach the forty that constitute evidence. Write one line for anything without text in it.
