The call usually comes from a department administrator, and it usually comes late. A professor with thirty or forty years of service has announced a retirement date, the office must be cleared by the end of term, and somewhere in that office — in desk drawers, on a shelf of external drives, inside two laptops that no longer hold a charge — sits the working record of an entire research career. Nobody knows exactly what is there. The professor knows what mattered. The filing system, such as it was, lived in one person's head, and that person is about to hand back their keys.

This is the work of the independent research-data cleanup consultant: part archivist, part forensic technician, part diplomat. The job is not to throw things away, and it is not to upload a lifetime of files to somebody else's cloud and call it preserved. The job is to find out what exists, describe it honestly, separate what belongs to the university from what belongs to the scholar and what belongs to research participants who were promised confidentiality decades ago, and hand over an inventory that a stranger could trust. This article follows one such engagement, and the tool at its centre: VaultBook's File/Folder Analyzer, working with VaultBook's full search — including OCR over scanned attachments — its Reader, and its data visualisation, all inside one encrypted, offline, single-file vault that becomes the audited inventory and finding aid for the whole rescue.

The university's own repository and records system stays exactly what it should be throughout: the system of record for whatever is formally archived. The vault is the working instrument that gets the collection to the repository's door in a described, defensible state — and it keeps working in offices with no network, on drives that predate the cloud, under confidentiality promises that were made before anyone had heard of a sync folder.

## First Principle: Inventory Before You Touch Anything

Every experienced voice in the file-rescue world gives the same first instruction, and it is boring: copy first, organise later, delete nothing until the inventory exists. Old drives fail on their own schedule; a drive that has sat in a drawer for six years may spin up ten times or once. So the consultant's first passes are read-only. Drives are labelled physically, connected one at a time, and surveyed. This is where the File/Folder Analyzer earns its place in the workflow: pointed at a mounted drive or a copied folder tree, it gives the engagement a structured picture of what is actually there — the file population, its types, its sizes, its sprawl — before a single file is moved. That survey becomes the first entries in the vault: one entry per drive, recording what the drive is, where it came from, what condition it is in, and what the analyzer found on it. The inventory is the engagement. Everything else — deduplication, description, retention decisions, repository transfer — is commentary on it.

## What the File/Folder Analyzer Actually Does Here

It helps to be precise about the division of labour. The File/Folder Analyzer does not rescue data by magic, and this article will not pretend otherwise. ## Why Not Notion, Evernote, OneNote, Google Keep or Apple Notes for This Job

The consultant in this article is not short of famous tools. The market-leading note apps — the deliberate-choice leaders Notion and Evernote, and the preinstalled defaults Microsoft OneNote, Google Keep and Apple Notes that the market research behind this series ranks at or near the top in the United States, the United Kingdom, Germany, Japan and India — are all, in their own terms, good at what they are for. The question is narrower: which of them can serve as the working inventory for a confidential, multi-drive, offline research-data rescue? Worked through one by one, on mechanisms rather than slogans, the answer is consistent.

**Notion** is the deliberate-choice leader in most of the markets surveyed, and its databases and linked pages are genuinely attractive for an inventory. But a Notion workspace lives on Notion's servers, under an account, reached through a network. A retiring professor's consent forms, candid correspondence and unpublished data would be uploaded to a third-party platform as the price of organising them — a custody change the participants who signed those consent forms never agreed to, and one the consultant cannot undo by deleting pages later. Notion is also built for collaboration among account holders; the engagement's core need — a sealed working instrument that opens identically in a basement archive room with no connectivity — is simply not its shape. Where Notion is genuinely stronger is live, shared workspaces: a whole laboratory annotating the same living documents in real time should use it, and this article's consultant says so to clients whose problem is actually that.

**Evernote**, the legacy leader with a large paying base in the US, Japan and Germany, holds its notebooks in its own cloud and syncs through its own service. For a personal archive that is a convenience; for a third party's confidential research estate it is the same custody problem in an older suit — the inventory of what exists, where it lives and what restrictions govern it would itself sit on a vendor's infrastructure, behind an account whose recovery flows the consultant does not control. VaultBook's vault, by contrast, is one file in the consultant's possession: it can be carried on encrypted media, opened on an air-gapped machine, and handed over as a file, not as access to somebody's tenant.

**Microsoft OneNote** is the institutional default across much of the surveyed world, bundled with Microsoft 365 in universities — including, very likely, this professor's own university. That is precisely its weakness here. OneNote notebooks live inside an institutional account, and institutional accounts close: the retirement that creates this engagement also ends the account the inventory would be stored in, on a timetable set by IT policy rather than by the archive's needs. A finding aid that must outlive the professor's employment cannot be a tenant of the professor's employer account. VaultBook needs no account at all — no sign-in, no tenant, no closure date — and the vault file opens the same way the day after the account closes as the day before.

**Google Keep** is the world's most-installed quick-capture notes app, and for capturing a thought it is excellent. It is not an inventory instrument: its model is short notes and labels in a Google account, with no per-entry encryption, no attachment indexing of the depth this work needs, and the same account-custody and upload questions as the other cloud tools, with less structure to show for them. Nobody sensible would catalogue forty years of governed research data in Keep, and Keep's designers would not ask them to.

**Apple Notes**, the preinstalled leader on the iPhone-heavy markets, locks the inventory to one vendor's ecosystem and sync — elegant inside that ecosystem, and a real constraint when the engagement's machines include the professor's elderly Windows laptop, the consultant's own workstation and an archive-room computer of unknown provenance. VaultBook is a single HTML file that runs in a browser on whatever machine is in front of the consultant, which is the actual hardware situation of every real cleanup.

Two honest limits belong in the record. First, the university's own repository and records system — not any note app, VaultBook included — is the right home for the formally archived collection, with its preservation commitments and institutional permanence; the vault is the working inventory and finding aid that gets the collection there described and defensible. Second, for genuinely collaborative, living documentation — a current laboratory's shared protocols, a team's meeting record — the cloud leaders are stronger, and a consultant who pretended otherwise would lose the room. The claim here is the scenario's claim: for inventorying a confidential, scattered, offline research estate under consent restrictions and a closing institutional account, custody, per-entry AES-256-GCM encryption, offline reliability, one-file portability and freedom from account or server dependence are the mechanisms that decide the job — and on those mechanisms VaultBook is the tool built for it. Nothing in this comparison turns on what any of these tools charges; the case rests entirely on where the data sits, who can open it, and what still works when the network and the account are gone.

Edge cases are where cleanup engagements earn their fee, precisely because they cannot be automated: each is a judgement, and the vault's job is to make the judgement findable, dated and owned.

## What This Workflow Refuses to Do

A word about refusals, because they define the service as much as the tools do. This workflow refuses to upload a client's archive to consumer cloud services for convenience — not for transcription, not for storage, not for sharing a draft inventory — because the consent and custody problems that creates cannot be un-created. It refuses to delete before the inventory exists and the decisions are recorded. It refuses to describe what it has not seen: an unopened drive is described as unopened, an unreadable file as unreadable. It refuses to let the finding aid become a rival to the repository's record, or to keep a copy of the vault beyond the engagement's terms. And it refuses the fantasy that the right app makes this work automatic. VaultBook's contribution is real but specific: one encrypted, offline file in which the analyzer's surveys, the OCR-searchable scans, the transcripts, the photographs, the decisions and the audit trail live together and can be found again. ## Closing: The Archive Deserves a Map

A research career is one of the few working lives whose raw material genuinely outlives its maker: datasets other scholars will re-analyse, interviews no one can conduct again, photographs of places and instruments and field sites that no longer exist. When that material sits undescribed on failing drives in a cleared office, its survival is luck. The work described in this article — surveyed drive by drive, described entry by entry, governed document by governed document, inside one encrypted offline VaultBook vault — replaces luck with a map. The university's repository keeps what it should keep, under its own record. The family keeps what is theirs. The restricted stays restricted, and can prove why. And the finding aid itself, a single file that asks nothing of any platform, remains openable by whoever needs it next. That is what the File/Folder Analyzer is for, in the end: not tidiness, but testimony — an honest account of what a life's work consists of, made while the one person who knows is still there to confirm it.
